# How scientists measure climate change's role in extreme weather

After a heat wave, flood or powerful storm, a familiar question follows: Did climate change cause it? Scientists approach the question differently. They estimate whether human-caused warming made an event more likely or more intense — and how much difference it made.

That work is called extreme event attribution. It uses statistics and climate models to compare the weather possible in today's climate with what might have happened in a world without human greenhouse gas emissions.

"We cannot say any single storm was caused by climate change. We can say whether climate change stacked the odds and, if so, by how much," said Ingrid Bjornsson, a climate attribution scientist at Nordic Climate Center.

## Comparing two climates

The core comparison involves two groups of simulated weather events. One comes from models representing today's climate. The other represents a hypothetical climate without human greenhouse gas emissions.

Scientists compare how often events of a given intensity occur in each group. If an event is 10 times more likely in today's climate, they describe its probability as having increased tenfold.

Results also can be expressed as a change in the "return period," the average time between events of a given magnitude. An event with a 100-year return period has a 1% chance of occurring in any given year. The term describes a probability, not a schedule.

Attribution can address severity as well as likelihood. For hurricanes and typhoons, studies typically examine wind speed or rainfall rate rather than whether a storm formed. Storm formation depends on factors beyond sea surface temperature alone.

## Why some events are easier to assess

The basic physics is well established: Warmer surface temperatures intensify heat waves, while warmer air holds more moisture, increasing the potential for flood-producing and storm rainfall.

But that does not make every event equally straightforward to analyze. Heat waves are among the easiest to attribute. Multiple published studies have found that some recent heat waves would have been virtually impossible without human-caused climate change.

Rainfall depends on atmospheric dynamics that are harder to simulate accurately, so confidence in rainfall attribution is generally lower than for heat.

Drought presents another challenge: time. Detecting a climate change signal in a single drought year is harder than detecting one in a multiyear trend.

Scientists also must account for local influences, including deforestation and urban heat island effects, to isolate the contribution of greenhouse gases.

## Reading the uncertainty

An attribution finding depends on the quality and resolution of the models, the length of observational records and how well the models represent the region studied.

Different models can produce different probability ratios for the same event. Studies using a wider range of models produce wider confidence intervals, showing more of that uncertainty.

Speed matters, too. The World Weather Attribution group publishes rapid analyses and then submits them for peer review. Bjornsson said rapid results should be treated as preliminary: Full analyses using larger groups of models and longer observational records may differ.

A study can also find that climate change did not significantly affect a particular event. Such findings receive less media attention.

For readers, the useful questions are what the study measured, how large an effect it found and how uncertain that estimate is. Bjornsson said claiming more certainty than the data support undermines both scientific trust and the usefulness of attribution research for policy.
