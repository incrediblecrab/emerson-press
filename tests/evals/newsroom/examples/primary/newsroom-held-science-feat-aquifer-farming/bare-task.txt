# As aquifer falls, Sutter County farmers face costly shift to dryland

For Helen Kowalski, the decision to stop irrigating 600 acres of her Sutter County farm came when her well began failing, not when a conservation payment became available.

“I didn't convert because of an incentive program. I converted because I had a pump pulling air. You adapt or you sell,” she said.

Kowalski, 61, has farmed 1,400 acres for 34 years. Three years after converting part of her operation to dryland wheat, she is profitable in most years on that acreage. But getting there required restructuring $340,000 in debt as revenue fell sharply before she could adjust costs.

Her experience illustrates the challenge facing farmers across Sutter Flats: Declining groundwater is pushing them toward farming without irrigation, while financing, crop choices and markets complicate the switch.

The Clearwater Basin Aquifer, which underlies the three-state region, has fallen 14 meters, or about 46 feet, since 2000 in its most heavily irrigated zones.

Patrick Brennan, Sutter County's water manager, said the decline suggests economically viable irrigation will end within 15 to 25 years in the county's deepest-pumped zones. Voluntary conservation has not been sufficient to change that trajectory, he said.

For neighboring wheat farmer Al Reyes, 47, the pressure already shows up in pumping bills. His lift costs have doubled in five years as the water table drops and his pump runs longer to deliver the same volume.

Reyes plans to transition to dryland farming within three years. How to pay for it remains unresolved.

“The bank understands an irrigated wheat farm. A dryland farm in this county is unproven,” he said.

Regional dryland wheat yields average 28 bushels per acre, compared with 55 bushels under irrigation, though production costs are substantially lower. The transition also requires a different crop calendar and different varieties, said Lena Park, a University of Sutter extension agent. Seed catalogs historically have offered fewer dryland options because the regional market was small.

Reyes has attended two county workshops but said neither answered his central question: What can he plant on 1,200 acres without established local dryland processing infrastructure?

Two local grain elevators have told him they cannot handle certain dryland specialty crops. The nearest market for those crops is 90 miles away.

Assistance is available, but access and participation remain obstacles. The county offers $18,000 per irrigated acre through a well-retirement buyout program. Just 2,400 acres have been retired toward a 40,000-acre target.

State agricultural officials are studying the program's design. Early data suggest smaller operators participate at higher rates than larger ones.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture also offers up to $40,000 per farm through a pilot conservation program. Kowalski received assistance that she said covered about 12% of her transition costs.

Reyes has not applied. The program requires a three-year farm plan, and he does not yet know which crops would be viable on his land without irrigation.

Brennan said the county's most urgent need is a regional monitoring system giving farmers real-time, localized groundwater information instead of annual state surveys. Falling water levels also are concentrating naturally occurring minerals, including arsenic, in some areas, he said.

The county is modeling what its food and tax base could look like in 20 years as irrigation declines. Brennan said those projections are uncomfortable for officials dependent on agricultural property tax revenue.

Kowalski now finds herself an example to younger farmers, a role she considers both gratifying and uncomfortable.

“I didn't plan to be a pioneer. I was just broke and out of water.”
