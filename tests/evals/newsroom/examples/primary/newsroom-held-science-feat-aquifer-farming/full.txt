# As aquifer falls, Sutter Flats farmers face the cost of going dry

Helen Kowalski switched 600 acres to dryland wheat three years ago when her well began failing. After 34 years farming in Sutter County, she had little room to wait for a better reason.

“I didn't convert because of an incentive program. I converted because I had a pump pulling air. You adapt or you sell,” she said.

Kowalski, 61, farms 1,400 acres. Her dryland operation is now profitable in most years, she said, but getting there required restructuring $340,000 in debt as revenue fell sharply before she adjusted her costs.

Across Sutter Flats, a region spanning three states, the declining Clearwater Basin Aquifer is forcing farmers to weigh the cost of continuing to irrigate against the financial and practical difficulties of farming without it. The aquifer has declined 14 meters since 2000 in the most heavily irrigated zones. For farmers considering a switch, questions about financing, crop choices and access to markets remain unresolved.

Patrick Brennan, Sutter County's water manager, said the rate of decline suggests irrigation will no longer be economically viable in the county's deepest-pumped zones within 15 to 25 years. Voluntary conservation has not been sufficient to change that trajectory, he said.

Neighboring wheat farmer Al Reyes, 47, is still irrigating. He said his pumping costs have doubled in five years as the water table drops and his pump runs longer to deliver the same volume.

Reyes plans to move to dryland farming within three years but does not know how to finance the change.

“The bank understands an irrigated wheat farm. A dryland farm in this county is unproven,” he said.

Regional wheat yields average 28 bushels per acre without irrigation, compared with 55 bushels under irrigation, though dryland production costs are substantially lower. Kowalski's experience shows how difficult the period between losing revenue and lowering costs can be.

She received assistance through a U.S. Department of Agriculture pilot conservation program that offers up to $40,000 per farm. She said the aid covered about 12% of her transition costs.

Reyes has not applied. The program requires a three-year farm plan, he said, and he does not yet know which crops are viable on his land under dryland conditions.

Lena Park, a University of Sutter extension agent, said dryland farming requires a different crop calendar and different varieties. Seed catalogs for the region have historically offered fewer dryland options because the market was small, she said.

Reyes said two county workshops left him without an answer about what to plant on 1,200 acres in a county lacking established dryland processing infrastructure. Two local grain elevators told him they could not handle certain dryland specialty crops, he said. The nearest market for those crops is 90 miles away.

The county offers $18,000 per irrigated acre through a well-retirement buyout program. So far, 2,400 acres have been retired toward a target of 40,000.

Brennan said the county's most urgent need is a regional monitoring system that gives farmers real-time information about local water table conditions instead of relying on annual state surveys. The county is also modeling its food and tax base 20 years from now if irrigation continues to decline, he said. The projections are uncomfortable for officials who depend on agricultural property tax revenue.

Kowalski said younger farmers now see her as an example, a role she finds gratifying and uncomfortable.

“I didn't plan to be a pioneer. I was just broke and out of water.”
