**Headline:** Marrowbank's Bench Workshop Keeps Neighborhood Waiting Spots on Their Feet

MARROWBANK — In a workshop tucked behind the parks department, carpenter Eren Voss lifts a bench with sound cast-metal ends but several worn wooden slats, laid out on the floor for inspection. The first step isn't sawing or sanding — it's matching the bench's identification tag to its location and maintenance record.

"The tag tells us where it lives," Voss said. "That matters when somebody is waiting for that particular bench to come back."

The workshop repairs public benches pulled from parks, streets and transport waiting areas across the city. When a condition assessment supports it, crews replace damaged slats while keeping usable structural parts, such as the metal ends on the bench currently on Voss's floor. But not every bench can be repaired, and the workshop makes no claim that preserving an old frame is always cheaper than starting over.

The stakes of a repair, or a delay, can be felt directly by residents who rely on a specific bench rather than seating in general. Rella Norn, who uses one bench on the route between a bus stop and the neighborhood shops as a regular resting place, said the location itself is what matters.

"I know which bit of the walk comes before it and which bit comes after," Norn said. "Another bench across the grass is not on that walk."

A single removal can leave a gap on a walking route even when several other benches remain elsewhere in the same park, which is part of why the parks office says it prioritizes repairs by condition, route need and available parts rather than simply the order in which a bench was donated or installed.

That calculus grows more complicated for memorial benches. Under the city's memorial-bench agreement, plaques are recorded and retained when appropriate, but donors do not gain permanent control over how the city maintains the bench. Staff contact the recorded plaque contact when a major change affects an existing memorial bench, following the city's established process.

Inside the workshop, practical constraints shape the pace of repairs. Common slat sizes are kept in stock, but older or unusual designs can require specially prepared replacement pieces. A newly cut slat isn't installed just because the color matches; staff also check its dimensions and overall suitability for that bench. Templates for several retired bench designs still in public use hang on the workshop wall, evidence of models the city no longer manufactures but still maintains.

Once a bench is finished, the crew records a return-to-service date, which lets the parks office distinguish a bench that's done in the workshop from one that has actually been reinstalled at its site. Transport and installation are scheduled alongside other parks work, so a completed bench may sit briefly before it goes back outside.

The office has begun attaching a location sketch to removal notices after fielding questions from residents unsure whether a missing bench was gone for good. Residents can also report a damaged or missing bench through the parks service without knowing its inventory number.

City officials describe the workshop as a repair operation, not part of any announced reduction in the total number of public seats citywide. The packet reviewed for this story does not include a universal lifespan for a repaired bench or a measured citywide savings figure from reuse — only the workshop's daily judgment calls about which pieces of a given bench are worth keeping, and which are not.

For residents like Norn, though, the outcome of those judgment calls is measured in more immediate terms: whether the particular bench on their particular walk is there when they need it.
