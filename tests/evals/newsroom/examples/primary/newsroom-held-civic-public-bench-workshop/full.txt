**Headline: In Marrowbank workshop, a bench's tag decides its fate**

MARROWBANK — Before carpenter Eren Voss touches a bench, he checks its tag.

Every bench that comes through the city's parks workshop carries an identification tag linking it to a specific location and maintenance record. Voss matches that tag before starting a job, because the workshop's task is not just to fix wood and metal — it's to send the right bench back to the right spot.

"The tag tells us where it lives," Voss said. "That matters when somebody is waiting for that particular bench to come back."

On a recent day, one bench sat in the workshop with its cast-metal ends intact but several wooden slats worn through, laid out on the floor beside it. When a condition assessment supports it, the crew replaces damaged slats while keeping usable structural parts, such as sound metal frames. Not every bench qualifies. The workshop does not claim that preserving an old frame is always cheaper than starting over, and some benches are retired rather than repaired.

The stakes of a single bench are not abstract to Rella Norn, a resident who rests regularly on one bench along the route between a bus stop and the neighborhood shops.

"I know which bit of the walk comes before it and which bit comes after," Norn said. "Another bench across the grass is not on that walk."

A removal can leave a gap on a route even when other benches remain elsewhere in the same park, which is why the parks office weighs more than a repair queue's chronology. Condition, route need and available parts all factor into which benches get attention first, rather than simply the order in which a bench arrived — including those donated as memorials.

Marrowbank's memorial-bench agreement allows plaques to be recorded and retained when appropriate, but it does not give donors permanent control over the city's maintenance decisions. When a major change affects an existing memorial bench, staff contact the recorded plaque contact under the city's process.

Inside the workshop, practical limits shape the work. Common slat sizes are kept in stock, but older, unusual bench designs sometimes require specially prepared replacement pieces — and a newly cut piece isn't installed just because its color matches. Staff also check its dimensions and suitability for that particular bench. Templates for several retired designs still hang on the workshop wall, kept because examples of those designs remain in public use.

The crew logs a return-to-service date for each completed bench, letting the parks office track what's finished in the shop versus what's back at its site. Because transport and installation are scheduled alongside other parks work, a finished bench can sit briefly before it returns outside.

Residents don't need a bench's inventory number to report damage or a disappearance — they can do so through the parks service directly. After the office received questions about whether removed benches were gone for good, it began attaching a location sketch to removal notices.

The workshop's tally is one of repairs, not a reduction in the city's overall seating. Whether a given bench lasts another year or another decade, staff say, depends on the piece in front of them — not a formula.
