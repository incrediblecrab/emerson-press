# How Fenwick Reach chooses which curb ramps to rebuild first

Fenwick Reach gives priority to curb-ramp projects that connect routes to public buildings, transport stops and frequently used services, rather than simply rebuilding the corners that attract the most complaints.

The current capital program funds work at 16 locations selected from resident reports and city inspections. As of Sept. 18, 2026, other identified needs await assessment or later budgets. The central question is how work at individual corners can make a route usable.

“A usable ramp on one corner does not complete a journey if the next corner still stops you,” access engineer Rina Voss said.

## What do the report totals tell residents?

The access office received 48 reports concerning 28 distinct curb-ramp locations during its latest review period. Several reports concerned the same location, so 48 reports does not mean 48 separate ramps need rebuilding.

Nor can residents subtract the 16 funded locations from the 28 reported locations to determine how many reported sites remain unfunded. The office has not supplied the overlap between those two groups, and the funded projects also draw on city inspections.

The office does not treat the absence of a complaint as evidence that a ramp is usable. Staff also must reconcile duplicate records: Some older entries use intersection names that changed after two streets were combined.

## What do inspectors look for?

Inspectors record slope, available turning space, drainage and obstructions such as utility covers. A ramp can need work because of its approach or landing even when the sloping surface looks intact.

Those conditions also affect the work required. Locations needing drainage changes or utility relocation require coordination, rather than a simple surface replacement.

The city’s current design guide supplies the criteria for these projects. That does not establish whether every existing ramp complies with legal requirements; the reporting record contains no such determination.

## How does the budget affect the work?

The $365,000 program budget covers design and associated street work as well as ramp construction. It is not an equal cash allowance for each of the 16 locations.

Dividing the budget by 16 therefore would not show the construction cost of an individual ramp. The city has not published a final construction cost for every location, and some projects require drainage or utility work that others may not need.

Completing the funded projects will not establish that the entire street network is accessible.

## How will residents see the reasons for the choices?

A mobility advisory group has asked the city to show the route connections behind its selections, not only a list of corners. The city will publish a map linking selected locations to the intended routes before work begins.

“We want to see the path that becomes possible, not just sixteen completed entries on a spreadsheet,” group member Pera Linn said.

Residents can provide information during the review about destinations and recurring access barriers without disclosing a medical diagnosis. That information addresses the city’s focus on routes: where people need to go and where they encounter barriers along the way.

Temporary arrangements during construction will be announced separately for each site. After construction, the advisory group will review completed routes with staff, rather than certify them in advance from drawings alone.
