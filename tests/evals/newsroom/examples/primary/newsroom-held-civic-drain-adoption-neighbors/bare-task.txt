Watchful Eyes, Not Wet Boots: Mossgate Residents Help Spot Drain Trouble

MOSSGATE — Tera Noll doesn't take a special route through her neighborhood to check the storm drain near the curb. She just keeps an eye on it while running her usual errands.

"I know this corner because I pass it all the time," Noll said. "The program gave me somewhere to send what I was noticing."

Noll is one of a growing number of Mossgate residents taking part in the city's new drain-observation program, which asks registered volunteers to watch designated street drain openings and report problems to the works office. After leaves fall from the trees lining her street, Noll checks the drain along her regular walking route, which also includes a stop at a neighborhood shop — making the check part of an ordinary trip rather than a separate patrol.

When she finds loose leaves on the surface, Noll places them in a bag and photographs the cleared opening to include with her report. The program permits residents to remove loose surface debris from a safe public position, but it does not allow them to open grates or enter drains themselves.

City officials are quick to note what a clear surface does — and doesn't — mean. A cleared opening does not establish that the underground pipe is free of blockages, or that nearby properties are protected from flooding. Reports use the city's official location references, officials said, so staff can tell apart neighboring drain openings that might look similar in a close-up photograph. Some participants have given their adopted locations informal nicknames, but official work orders continue to rely on the city's standard identifiers.

Works supervisor Elrin Voss said the program is designed to guide city crews, not replace them.

"A useful report can get the right crew to the right place," Voss said. "It does not ask a neighbor to become that crew."

The city says it remains fully responsible for underground maintenance and will dispatch trained crews when a resident's report indicates work beyond the volunteer role. Officials describe the program as a supplement to routine inspections, not a transfer of the city's drainage duties to private residents. The works office compares incoming reports against its regular inspection schedule rather than relying solely on locations with an active volunteer, and a drain without a registered observer remains fully the city's responsibility.

Participants are instructed not to work during dangerous conditions or from within a traffic lane to complete a check, and residents may take part simply by observing and reporting even if they are unable to remove leaves themselves. The city provides collection bags and a short orientation session at the community center for those who sign up.

When a report comes in, the works office acknowledges it and records its status — though officials caution that an acknowledgment is not a statement that a repair has already been made.

The program's first season has produced a steady record of resident reports, though city officials say they do not yet have measured data showing a reduction in flooding or maintenance costs. Before considering any expansion, the city plans to review whether the report descriptions residents submit give crews enough information to act on.

Officials also emphasized that the program is not an emergency-warning service and does not replace the city's existing channels for reporting urgent flooding or storm conditions.
