Mossgate program turns dog-walk routines into drain-watch duty, city says it won't replace its own crews

MOSSGATE — Tera Noll's Thursday walk to the corner shop now includes a stop most passersby wouldn't notice: the storm drain across from her usual route, where fallen leaves collect after a windy night.

Noll knelt at the curb, scooped the loose leaves into a bag and photographed the cleared opening — the routine steps the city's drain-observation program asks of its registered volunteers.

"I know this corner because I pass it all the time," Noll said. "The program gave me somewhere to send what I was noticing."

The program, now in its first season, asks residents to watch designated street drain openings near their homes or regular routes and report problems to the city works office. Volunteers may clear loose surface leaves from a safe position on the curb or sidewalk. They are not permitted to open grates, enter drains or work from the traffic lane, and they're told to skip a check altogether in dangerous conditions.

City officials are explicit that the program does not shift responsibility for drainage onto residents. "A useful report can get the right crew to the right place," said works supervisor Elrin Voss. "It does not ask a neighbor to become that crew." The city remains responsible for underground maintenance and dispatches trained crews when a resident's report points to work beyond what a volunteer can do.

That distinction matters because a clear surface opening, officials say, doesn't mean the pipe beneath it is clear or that nearby properties are protected from flooding. Reports use the city's own location references, rather than any names residents might use, so staff can tell apart openings that look similar in a close-up photograph. Some volunteers have given their adopted drains informal names, but official work orders stick to the city's identifiers.

When a report comes in, the works office logs it and records its status. An acknowledgment, officials stress, is not a statement that repair work has already happened. The office also continues its routine inspection schedule citywide, rather than relying only on locations that have an active volunteer — a drain with no registered observer is still the city's responsibility to check.

Participation doesn't require physical work. Residents can join by observing and reporting alone, without removing leaves themselves. The city supplies collection bags and runs a short orientation at the community center for anyone who signs up.

The first season has generated a steady record of resident reports, though city officials said they have no measurement yet of whether the program has reduced flooding or maintenance costs. Before expanding it, the works office plans to review whether the descriptions residents submit give crews enough detail to act on.

Officials also want it understood that the program is not an emergency service. It does not substitute for the city's urgent-reporting channels during active storms, and residents who spot dangerous flooding are directed to those channels instead.

For Noll, the check has folded into an errand she was already running. "It's part of the walk to the shop," she said, "not a separate trip out to patrol the whole street."
