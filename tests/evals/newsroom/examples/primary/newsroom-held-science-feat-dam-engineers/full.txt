**Aging Colton Dam Holds — For Now — as Engineer Who Built It Watches and Waits**

RIDGELINE — Once a year, Bernard Cho drives out to see the dam he helped design more than six decades ago. He stands where he always stands, looks at the concrete face rising above the river, and checks whether it is still holding.

"Old habit," Cho, 77, said. "I like to see it holding. This year it was holding. Next year, I expect it will still be holding. The year after, I don't know."

The Colton River Dam, built in 1961, supplies water to 220,000 people across the Ridgeline region. Federal dam safety engineers designated it a Level 2 structural concern in 2023, and the Ridgeline Water District has not secured a fully funded plan to fix it.

Cho was one of the dam's original design engineers. He has watched its slow deterioration with what he calls professional regret. "We designed that dam to last 50 years with proper maintenance," he said. "It's lasted 63, and the maintenance has been adequate but not comprehensive. I don't know whether to take credit for the engineering or responsibility for what comes next."

Leah Petrov, 41, a dam safety engineer at the water district, has spent three years documenting deterioration in the dam's upstream face, where an alkali-silica reaction in the concrete has accelerated since 2018, coinciding with warmer average summer temperatures. "We're seeing expansion rates we didn't see in the first two decades of monitoring," Petrov said. "We have less time than the original projections said."

Federal records show the dam's downstream inundation zone includes 38,000 residents in three communities. Those communities have no designated emergency shelters within their boundaries; residents would need to travel 14 to 22 miles to reach designated safe zones. The district's emergency action plan, last updated in 2019, covers evacuation routes, but Petrov said the community notification system it depends on has not been tested recently.

The district has applied for $180 million in federal dam rehabilitation funding. The program received $300 million nationally for a priority list of more than 60 structures, and Colton is not yet in the top tier. "We are buying time," Petrov said. "We know that. It's not a comfortable thing to know." In August, the district's board voted to prioritize the federal application and explore bonding options if federal money does not arrive in time.

Petrov's team estimates internally that the dam could be maintained safely for another 12 to 15 years under an accelerated repair program, or six to eight years without one. Neither estimate has been formally submitted to federal regulators. She has also not shared the full scope of her monitoring data publicly, citing district concerns about property values and community alarm — a decision she described as difficult.

Dr. Grace Mbeki, a water policy researcher at Ridgeline University, said that tension is common for infrastructure with slow-developing risks. Transparency is required under the district's own public information charter, she said, but is practically constrained by who controls the release of raw monitoring data.

Cho reviewed Petrov's reports at her request and said the data was consistent with what he would have predicted — and worse than he had hoped. He said he wrote to the district twice in the past decade offering his knowledge of the original design drawings, and received no response until Petrov contacted him directly. His records, she said, showed reinforcement spacing that updated drawings did not capture. "He knew things about this dam that weren't in the files," Petrov said. "That matters."

She said she can measure the danger but not the response. "I can tell you the concrete is losing 2.8 millimeters a year," Petrov said. "I cannot tell you who decides when that number becomes an emergency."

Cho, for his part, remembers what he and his colleagues believed when they built it. "In 1961, we thought we were building something permanent," he said. "We weren't, of course. Nothing is. But we built it to last long enough that someone else would have to figure out what to do with it."
