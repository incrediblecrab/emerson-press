**Bus Schedule, Not Wages, Shapes the Workday at Rillstone Switchgear**

By [Staff Writer]

The three minutes matter.

Every weekday, assembler Omi Doren steps off the 6:05 a.m. bus and walks seven minutes to Rillstone Switchgear, arriving with barely enough time to reach her station before her 6:15 a.m. shift begins. The margin is thin, but it is the return trip that shapes her workday more.

Doren's shift ends at 2:15 p.m., and the bus back to town leaves the same stop at 2:25 p.m. Miss it, and the wait for the next one stretches to an hour under the current weekday timetable.

"Ten more minutes at the bench can become an hour at the bus stop," Doren said in an interview conducted as part of a field note taken Aug. 6.

Doren, who does not own a car, said she sometimes turns down optional extra work at the end of her shift rather than risk missing the 2:25 p.m. departure. She said a dependable connection to and from work would matter more to her than a workplace parking spot — a benefit she has no use for.

Her experience is not universal among Rillstone's workforce, but it is not isolated either. The factory, which makes electrical cabinets on an industrial estate built before the area's more recent housing expansion, surveyed its 85 employees on commuting habits. Forty responded, and of those, 14 said they regularly depend on the bus. The survey does not establish how nonrespondents get to work, and factory recruitment records do not isolate transportation as a cause of six current vacancies at the plant.

Still, the numbers were enough to prompt production manager Teri Vann to request a price quote for an employer-run shuttle between the central bus interchange and the factory. A local transport operator quoted $140 per working day for a limited morning-and-afternoon pilot service, or $2,800 for a 20-day trial. The employer has not approved a budget for the pilot or set a start date.

Part of the difficulty, according to those who run the public route, is that Rillstone's shift schedule was not built into the bus timetable — the timetable was built first, and shifts have had to work around it. The public bus operator said its vehicles also carry students to and from school, and shifting this route's departure times could not be done without affecting other passengers' trips.

Driver Leda Quill put it plainly: the published schedule is not a separate vehicle waiting on a single factory's shift change, but one link in a longer chain of trips across town. "Moving one departure means deciding which other passengers will wait," Quill said.

That constraint helps explain why Vann is looking at a shuttle rather than lobbying for a timetable change. The proposed service would be run by and for the employer alone — a supplement to the public route, not a replacement for it.

Any solution also has to fit around the realities of factory floor work. Rillstone's production line includes a shared morning briefing, employees said, which makes staggering start times for individual workers difficult in some roles.

For now, nothing has changed. No bus-route cut has been adopted, no attendance penalty has been imposed on Doren or others over late arrivals, and no hiring increase tied to a shuttle has been confirmed. The pilot remains a quote on paper, and Doren's mornings and afternoons remain bound to the same three-minute margins they have followed for months — a small arithmetic of minutes that, for now, is doing the scheduling.
