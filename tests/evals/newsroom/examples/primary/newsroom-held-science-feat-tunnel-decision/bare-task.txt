After 11 Years and $280 Million in Delays, Redcliff Tunnel Nears Decision Day

A road tunnel proposed to run 4.2 kilometers beneath an urban neighborhood has spent 11 years in environmental review, and state officials say a final decision is now expected by June.

The Redcliff Tunnel, first estimated to cost $840 million in 2013, is now projected at $1.4 billion — an increase of roughly $280 million attributable to inflation and delay costs alone, according to project figures.

Marcus Oduya, 58, the civil engineer who led the tunnel's original planning team, retired in 2022 without seeing a decision made. He now works as a transportation consultant and says the years of waiting have shaped his professional outlook.

"I designed that tunnel to solve a traffic problem that existed in 2013," Oduya said. "I can't tell you whether it solves the 2025 problem because we haven't made decisions fast enough to find out."

The delay stems from several unresolved disputes: an underground aquifer that an early environmental study underestimated, opposition from the Ridgemont neighborhood over surface disruption during construction, and two competing surface-level alternatives proposed by environmental advocates.

An independent review commissioned by the state in 2023 found the project's aquifer management plan inadequate for the depth profile in its northeastern section. The plan was revised, but critics say the revision still falls short.

Carmen Osei, 43, leads the Ridgemont Traffic Justice Coalition, which has pushed for a rerouted alignment avoiding the neighborhood's historic district rather than outright cancellation of the tunnel.

"We were told in 2016 that they had heard our concerns," Osei said. "We were told in 2019 that they had heard our concerns. We are still here in 2025."

Meanwhile, the traffic problem the tunnel was meant to address has grown. The surface arterials it would relieve now carry 92,000 vehicle trips daily, up from 71,000 when the project was first studied.

Transportation planner Veronica Cheng, who advises the city on surface alternatives, said a transit-oriented redesign could absorb a third of those trips at a fifth of the tunnel's cost — but would require removing roadway space from cars. "That is politically very hard," she said. Cheng added that transit ridership in the corridor has grown 40% since 2013, strengthening the case for a transit-focused approach.

The stakes of further delay are rising. Federal highway funds committed to the project — $620 million — expire in September, and losing them would force the state to restart its funding process. The project's contractor has meanwhile maintained a standby contract costing $12 million a year since 2021, awaiting word on whether to proceed.

A state transportation department spokesperson said the decision "will not be delayed again," with the department's director saying the choice will weigh community feedback, traffic modeling and cost. Officials have not indicated which alignment they favor.

City council members remain divided. Three have called for a full project restart, while two others warn that cancellation would waste $340 million already spent on design and permitting.

Public opinion is similarly split. A 2024 survey found 58% of Ridgemont residents prefer the rerouted alignment, while 61% of citywide respondents favor the faster original route.

Osei said her coalition is prepared to sue if the original alignment is approved without addressing historic district concerns, though a previous lawsuit was dismissed on procedural grounds.

Oduya, who said he occasionally visits the project's staging area, described the scene: "It's a hole in the ground and a billboard. Eleven years. The city is holding its breath."

Whatever the state decides, Oduya said he hopes for resolution. "A bad decision we can learn from," he said. "No decision is just time and money and opportunity disappearing."
