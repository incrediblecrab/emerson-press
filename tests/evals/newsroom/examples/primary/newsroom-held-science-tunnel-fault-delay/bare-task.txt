Eastside Connector Tunnel Delayed 4 Years, $1.9 Billion Over Budget After Fault Discovery

Metro City Transit Authority officials said Wednesday that an unmapped geological fault discovered during tunnel boring will push back completion of the Eastside Connector by at least four years and add $1.9 billion to the project's cost, bringing the total estimate to $7.4 billion.

The transit line, now expected to open in 2031 instead of 2027, is meant to carry 80,000 daily riders through a corridor currently served only by buses, with trips averaging 22 minutes longer than the planned rail service would offer.

Boring machines paused in August after geotechnical sensors detected rock fracturing and groundwater pressure inconsistent with pre-construction surveys, according to the authority. Engineers determined the anomalies were caused by a fault running about 340 meters across the tunnel path, 38 meters below a densely populated neighborhood.

The fault does not appear on any historical geological mapping of the city. The authority said it appears to be a secondary offshoot of a larger regional fault that has not moved in more than 10,000 years.

"Pre-construction surveys do not capture every subsurface feature," Transit Authority Director Sandra Muñoz said. "We are dealing with a real geological constraint, not a project management failure."

Muñoz said no workers were injured and that boring was halted as a standard precaution.

Lead geotechnical engineer Dr. Chen Li said the authority is studying three options: reorienting the tunnel bore by 11 degrees, treating the soil across the fault zone to stabilize it, or slowing the boring machines as they pass through the area, at significant cost in time. Ground treatment will be necessary regardless of which option is chosen, Li said.

The authority has not indicated which option it prefers, saying an engineering decision would come before any public announcement. Officials said they plan to present a preferred solution to the board within 90 days.

Three blocks of residential buildings sitting above the fault zone are being monitored for surface settlement. The authority said no movement beyond normal tolerance has been detected.

This is the third cost increase for the Eastside Connector since construction began in 2019. The two previous increases, totaling $1.1 billion, were attributed to COVID-19-related supply chain disruptions and rising steel prices.

Federal transit officials said the project's primary funding grant, which covers $2.2 billion of the original $4.4 billion budget, would need to be renegotiated if the project's scope changes substantially.

Bond rating agencies said they are reviewing the authority's debt rating in response to the revised cost profile.

City council members representing eastern districts, where the tunnel is being built, called for an independent audit of the geological survey process conducted before construction began.

Two stations at the western end of the line have already been completed and have been in operation since 2022.

The delay adds to broader concerns about the city's transit infrastructure, which received a D+ rating in the most recent national infrastructure report.

The Transit Authority said further details on the tunnel's revised route or construction timeline would be released once the board selects a mitigation option.
