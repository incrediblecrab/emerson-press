# Nettleford College's instrument-repair program weighs learning time against getting instruments back to schools

A clarinet came apart on a workbench at Nettleford College one afternoon in July, and trainee Omi Keld began fitting a new pad to it. When the job looked finished, Keld carried the instrument to an instructor's inspection bench for a check that would decide whether it was actually done.

That pause — between a repair that looks complete and one an instructor will sign off on — sits at the center of how the college's one-year instrument-repair program operates. The program enrolls 24 adult trainees drawn from varied work and education backgrounds, training them to repair instruments that local school music departments send in for service.

Schools that use the workshop are not buying a guaranteed express service. They are supplying instruments for supervised repair work, and every instrument that goes back out must pass an instructor's functional check before it leaves.

That arrangement creates a tradeoff. The workshop exists to teach trainees, which takes time and sometimes repeated attempts at a single task. Schools, meanwhile, want their instruments back in students' hands. Nettleford's process tries to hold both at once, asking schools to accept a longer wait in exchange for a lower cost and a role in training new repair technicians.

During the 2025-26 teaching year, the workshop accepted 180 school instruments and returned 150 after repair. Twenty were still awaiting parts at the year-end count, and 10 were assessed as beyond the workshop's repair scope. The college has not published how those figures translate into wait times or outcomes by school or by trainee, and the count reflects instruments, not the number of schools involved or trainees' individual qualifications.

Schools pay a $35 handling contribution per accepted instrument, plus any parts charge agreed on for a particular job. That payment does not cover the program's full teaching cost, which the college's budget and a $20,000 annual grant support. A commercial repair shop quoted $120 for one comparable clarinet job, with a shorter turnaround — a single price for a single job, not a stand-in for what every instrument costs to fix at either shop.

The college normally asks schools to allow six weeks for a repair, with longer waits possible when parts are unavailable.

Band teacher Rella Brill said she manages around those waits by keeping spare instruments on hand and shifting rehearsal groups while pupils' instruments are away. "A cheaper repair is useful only if I can keep the student playing while we wait," Brill said.

Keld, who previously repaired household appliances, said instrument work demands a different kind of checking than the trades Keld knew before. "The part can fit and the instrument can still tell you the job is not finished," Keld said.

Instructor Teri Sorn said trainees sometimes repeat a task before an instructor accepts it, adding learning time that a commercial quote would not need to allow for. The college has not published a matched study comparing its repairs' long-term durability against commercial providers.

Finishing the program earns a college certificate, not a guaranteed job or an assurance that a graduate can repair every family of instrument.

The workshop's next grant renewal is pending, and the $35 school contribution is not guaranteed to stay unchanged.

Back at the inspection bench, Keld waited while the instructor worked the clarinet's keys, one at a time.
