Why Some Westhollow Street Numbers Skip, Split or Carry Letters — and Why That's Not an Error

WESTHOLLOW — Residents who notice a missing street number or an address ending in a letter are not looking at a mistake, the city's addressing office says. They are looking at how the numbering system actually works.

The office assigns identifiers to entrances and properties under a local numbering plan that is maintained separately from land-title records, according to the office. On most streets, odd numbers run on one side and even numbers on the other, so consecutive numbers do not have to sit next to each other. Older streets retain some inherited numbering patterns that do not follow the current plan exactly, the office said.

The office said it has fielded questions recently after two former shop units were combined and a nearby property gained a new entrance.

In an example the office uses to explain the system, units formerly numbered 12 and 14 became a single unit using the number 12 after they were combined, leaving 14 unused rather than triggering a renumbering of the street. A separate example uses 20A and 20B for two approved entrances, assigned because inserting ordinary whole numbers there would have conflicted with existing addresses. The office said these are illustrations of how the system works and are not the addresses of actual households involved in the current questions.

"A missing number may tell you about an old division of the street, not a missing building," said Tessa Linn, the addressing officer.

A change in ownership, a new shop name or a new business tenant does not by itself require a new street number, the office said. When an address change is proposed, the office evaluates the entrance's position, the identifiers of neighboring properties and the needs of services that must locate the property, such as emergency responders.

Once a change is approved, the office notifies relevant city services — but the business itself must still update its own signs, deliveries and customer records, according to the office. The city does not promise that every private map or delivery database will update at the same time, the office said.

"The city's record changed in one step. Our invoices, labels and delivery notes did not all change with it," said Orin Fale, a shopkeeper.

The office also distinguishes a building's official street identifier from internal apartment or suite numbers. Renumbering units inside a building can still affect emergency and delivery information, so the office asks property managers to consult it before making such changes.

The addressing process does not determine who owns a property or settle disputes over a shared entrance, the office said. It does preserve earlier address references so that historical planning files can be connected to current locations — a task complicated on some older plans that used the name of a former pub or workshop instead of a street number, requiring staff to compare multiple records.

The city said it is preparing a public explanation of the numbering process after receiving repeated requests to fill apparent numbering gaps simply for visual neatness. Officials said the city has not proposed a townwide renumbering effort.

The office's record does not include statistics on emergency-response delays or failed deliveries tied to these numbering quirks. The rules described apply to Westhollow's own records and are not necessarily identical to practices in other towns.
