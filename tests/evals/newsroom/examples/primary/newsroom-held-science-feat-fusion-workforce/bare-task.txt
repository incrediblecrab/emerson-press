# Coal plant skills find a new test in Montana fusion project

Piotr Kaminski spent 28 years at a coal plant turbine shop. Now the former welding inspector is training to weld cryostat sections that operate at minus 269 degrees Celsius — a new assignment built on skills he says still hold.

“The physics is different. The tolerances are different. But I'm still making a seal that has to hold. The skill transfers. The confidence from twenty-eight years transfers,” said Kaminski, 54.

His employer, Vantage Fusion Corp., is building a fusion research and pre-commercial facility in Kelso Basin, Montana, expected to employ 320 people by 2027. The project offers a potential next chapter for workers from conventional power plants, but it also exposes a gap between fusion companies’ ambitions and the training available to realize them.

No standard apprenticeship curriculum covers superconducting magnet installation in a fusion environment. Some specialties are scarce enough to require international recruiting. And the industry’s largest workforce projections depend on commercial facilities that have not yet been built.

For Kelso Basin, the stakes extend beyond the facility. The surrounding county’s coal industry employed more than 1,200 people at its peak in 2008; it now employs fewer than 200. Local officials see the fusion project as an economic anchor.

About 40% of Vantage’s senior technicians came from fossil fuel plant backgrounds, construction director Derek Flynn said. The company considers that a deliberate workforce transfer strategy.

“We needed people who understood how to keep large, complex machinery running,” Flynn said. “The energy source is different. The discipline is the same.”

Vantage offers qualified fusion technicians a starting wage of $42 an hour, compared with a median of $36 an hour for similar roles at conventional power plants in Montana.

But transferring experience does not eliminate the need for new training.

Luz Morales, 34, an electrical engineer from the decommissioned Glenwood coal plant, joined Vantage after a federally funded retraining program. She now designs control systems for superconducting magnet power supplies.

The toughest adjustment, she said, was the pace.

“In coal, you have decades of documentation. Here we're writing the manual as we go.”

That unfinished manual extends to credentials and safety. Kaminski holds a two-year probationary credential while Vantage develops internal certification standards. He expects full certification before the facility’s first operational phase.

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration has not issued specific standards for fusion workplaces. Vantage operates under adapted cryogenics, high-voltage and radiation safety regulations.

To build a local training pipeline, Vantage partnered with Kelso Community College on an eight-month certificate in cryogenic systems maintenance. Its first 18 students graduated in September. Yet Morales said helium liquefaction expertise, needed to maintain the cryogenic systems, is so rare that Vantage recruited internationally.

Flynn said the company plans to double its technical workforce by 2026, competing with fusion startups, advanced manufacturers and national laboratories for limited talent.

The National Fusion Workforce Consortium, an industry group, estimates commercial-scale fusion will need 50,000 new skilled workers by 2040. No coordinated national training program exists. Department of Energy officials say workforce is cited as a top risk in project plans across the sector; the department has asked fusion companies to document training approaches in grant reports.

Industry analysts caution that fusion companies collectively account for only a few hundred current operational jobs. The projected hiring surge assumes construction of commercial facilities that do not yet exist.

Morales said she does not know whether Vantage’s facility will ultimately achieve its goal. After years of managing decline, she said, building something genuinely new is reason enough to try.

Kaminski’s advice to younger workers in his former union is more direct: “Don't wait. The plant is not coming back. But the skills you have — somebody needs them.”
