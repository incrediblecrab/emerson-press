# Coal plant veterans bring their skills to Montana fusion project

Piotr Kaminski spent 28 years at a coal plant turbine shop. Now the former welding inspector is training to weld cryostat sections that operate at minus 269 degrees Celsius for a fusion facility in Montana.

For Kaminski, 54, the new work draws on something familiar.

"The physics is different. The tolerances are different. But I'm still making a seal that has to hold," he said. "The skill transfers. The confidence from twenty-eight years transfers."

His move is part of a workforce strategy at Vantage Fusion Corp., which is building a research and pre-commercial facility in Kelso Basin. The project, expected to employ 320 people by 2027, is recruiting fossil fuel plant veterans while developing training for jobs that have no standard apprenticeship curriculum. Its experience shows both the possibilities for workers leaving coal and the uncertainty of an industry whose commercial facilities have yet to be built.

Approximately 40% of the facility's senior technicians came from fossil fuel plant backgrounds, said Derek Flynn, Vantage's construction director. That is a deliberate hiring strategy, he said.

"We needed people who understood how to keep large, complex machinery running," Flynn said. "The energy source is different. The discipline is the same."

Luz Morales, 34, an electrical engineer from the decommissioned Glenwood coal plant, joined Vantage after a federally funded retraining program. She now designs control systems for superconducting magnet power supplies.

The toughest adjustment, she said, was the pace.

"In coal, you have decades of documentation. Here we're writing the manual as we go."

Some of that work involves establishing how people will qualify for their jobs. No standard apprenticeship curriculum covers superconducting magnet installation in a fusion environment. Kaminski holds a two-year probationary credential while Vantage develops internal certification standards. He expects full certification before the facility enters its first operational phase.

Vantage and Kelso Community College have developed an eight-month certificate program in cryogenic systems maintenance, work involving extremely low temperatures. Its first 18 students graduated in September. Other specialties remain difficult to find: Morales said Vantage recruited internationally for helium liquefaction expertise needed to maintain those systems.

Flynn said the company plans to double its technical workforce by 2026, competing with other fusion startups, advanced manufacturing firms and national laboratories for limited talent.

The National Fusion Workforce Consortium, an industry group, estimates commercial-scale fusion will require 50,000 new skilled workers by 2040. No coordinated national training program exists. But industry analysts note that fusion companies currently account for only a few hundred operational jobs. The consortium's forecast assumes commercial facilities that have not yet been built.

In the county containing Kelso Basin, coal employment has fallen from more than 1,200 at its 2008 peak to fewer than 200. Local officials see Vantage as an economic anchor. The company offers qualified fusion technicians a starting wage of $42 an hour, compared with a median of $36 an hour for similar roles at conventional Montana power plants.

Morales said she does not know whether the facility will ultimately achieve its goal. After years of managing decline, she said, building something genuinely new is reason enough to try.

Kaminski has a message for younger workers in his former union.

"Don't wait. The plant is not coming back. But the skills you have — somebody needs them."
