# Grandparents share words and stories at Fellmere library

Laila Venn repeats a picture-book character's action with her hands while a librarian holds the illustration where the children can see it. The book is "The Tin Boat," and the telling gives families more than words to follow.

Venn is one of the volunteer grandparents who help lead Fellmere library's monthly story hour in English and Arabic. Paired with staff readers, the grandparents bring their voices and family experience to a session open to families whether or not they speak both languages.

The program makes room for exchanges that may be familiar around a family table. Parents and caregivers stay with the children, and the reading moves between passages and discussion rather than translating every sentence twice in a fixed sequence.

"My grandson sometimes explains a word to me in English, and I give him a word he has heard at our table. Here that exchange belongs in the room," Venn said.

The public reading begins with preparation away from the audience. Venn discusses the chosen picture book with the librarian beforehand. Volunteers asked for time to examine unfamiliar books in advance rather than being handed any title immediately before a session.

The library checks that both readers are working from the agreed story and that their explanations do not change its basic events. For "The Tin Boat," it also arranged permission for the planned public reading under its program arrangements.

That preparation supports a format in which speech, pictures and gestures all have a part. Readers alternate passages and discussion, leaving room to talk about the story without requiring each sentence to have an immediate counterpart in the other language.

"People join in through the picture, the movement or the sound. There is more than one way to follow what is happening," librarian Orla Reed said.

Some sessions also bring questions from adults about pronunciation or expressions used in their families. The readers discuss those as particular uses, rather than offering a single rule for every speaker. Volunteers contribute reading voices and experience; their role does not establish them as certified translators for every language task.

The story hour is not an assessed language course. The library has supplied no study of children's vocabulary gains or comparison with formal language teaching.

Each session lasts about 40 minutes. Families can step out and return when a child needs a break, and a low display holds related books they can borrow afterward through the library's ordinary borrowing process. The program budget covers volunteers' local travel costs.

The library plans to keep English and Arabic as the pairing through this term before considering other volunteer-led sessions. It has made no commitment to offer every requested language or move to a weekly schedule.

For now, the monthly gathering occupies a reading corner shared with the ordinary children's collection. After the stories and discussion, the cushions are packed away.
