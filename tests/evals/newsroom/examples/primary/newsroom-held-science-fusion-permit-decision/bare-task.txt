US Regulators Approve First Commercial Fusion Power Plant in Wyoming

The Federal Energy Licensing Commission voted 4-1 on Thursday to grant a construction permit to Meridian Fusion Sciences for a commercial fusion power plant in Reston Flats, Wyoming, the first such permit ever issued in the United States.

The proposed facility, called the Reston Fusion Station, is designed to generate 400 megawatts of net electrical output using a tokamak reactor design. Construction is expected to take seven years, with Meridian projecting first power generation by 2032 at the earliest.

The approval followed a 38-month safety review that examined neutron activation of structural materials, tritium handling and electromagnetic confinement failure scenarios. The Commission concluded that fusion reactions carry no chain-reaction risk and that the facility's primary radiological hazard is tritium, a mildly radioactive hydrogen isotope. Unlike fission reactors, which split heavy atoms, fusion combines light hydrogen isotopes and does not produce long-lived radioactive waste.

Commission Chair Elena Voronova said the vote reflected the agency's finding that fusion's hazard profile differs fundamentally from fission, and that existing nuclear regulations required substantial adaptation to apply to the new technology.

Meridian Fusion Sciences CEO Dr. Sanjay Parikh said the permit "represents the most rigorous safety evaluation ever conducted for a fusion facility," adding that the company intends "to meet every condition attached to it."

The plant will require a continuous supply of deuterium and tritium fuel, with tritium bred on-site using lithium blankets. The permit requires Meridian to submit quarterly construction reports and gives the Commission authority to halt work if safety benchmarks are not met.

The lone dissenting commissioner cited insufficient public comment time on the tritium storage plan, which was revised 11 months into the review. Within hours of the vote, two environmental groups filed for administrative review, arguing that the environmental impact statement covering tritium storage was inadequate.

The Commission has not yet issued guidance on how future fusion operators should handle the eventual decommissioning of activated structural components once a plant reaches the end of its operating life.

Meridian has raised $4.1 billion in private investment and holds a conditional $1.3 billion loan guarantee from the Department of Energy, contingent on meeting construction milestones. The Reston Flats site was chosen in part for its remote location and access to Wyoming's existing high-voltage transmission infrastructure.

Wyoming's governor welcomed the permit, citing an estimated 2,400 construction jobs and 180 permanent operations positions once the plant is running.

Not everyone is convinced the project will meet its ambitious timeline. Dr. Marcus Webb, a nuclear policy researcher at the Clearwater Energy Institute, said "approval-stage optimism has historically outpaced operational performance in first-of-kind energy facilities" and that "the timeline risk here is real."

No fusion plant anywhere in the world has yet operated at net positive energy output on a sustained commercial basis, underscoring the technical hurdles still ahead for Meridian and the broader industry. According to the Fusion Industry Association, global fusion investment has exceeded $6 billion in private capital since 2020, with more than 30 companies pursuing commercial timelines.

The Reston Fusion Station permit marks a milestone for an industry that has long promised abundant, low-waste power but has yet to demonstrate it at commercial scale. Whether Meridian can turn Thursday's regulatory approval into an operating plant by the early 2030s remains, by the company's own admission, a multiyear undertaking still ahead.
