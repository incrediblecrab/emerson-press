**Headline:** Women-led group at Sable Quay shed relearns an old skiff, plank by plank

MEDFORD — On a Thursday evening at the Sable Quay Boat Shed, Elva Rill peels a strip of masking tape, writes a word on it and presses it onto a fitting she has just pulled from a donated wooden rowing skiff.

"I knew how to get the meeting ready," Rill said. "I wanted to know what was happening on the other side of the shed door."

For years, Rill helped organize events for the local boating club without setting foot in its workshop. Now she is one of several women meeting weekly in the shed to repair the skiff, a donated boat whose hull work has drawn a small, mixed-experience crew under the direction of Hara Senn, a woodworker who leads the sessions.

The group is not a course and does not award a professional trade qualification. It is, members say, a place to learn by doing, at a pace set by the boat's own condition rather than a syllabus.

On a recent evening, members labeled removed fittings and set them beside a worn rail, comparing it against a replacement piece before deciding how to proceed. A chalkboard near the door listed the week's available tasks, including some marked as waiting for specialist input.

"The question at the bench is what the boat needs next," Senn said. "Everyone should have a chance to understand the answer."

The group keeps a repair notebook, a record members say matters because attendance varies week to week. Entries note what was assessed, what was removed and what remains unresolved, so someone arriving after a gap can pick up the thread rather than guess at it.

That habit of recording gaps extends to the boat's own past. Its earlier owner supplied photographs but no complete record of every repair made over its life, and Senn has asked members to write down what they don't know about earlier work rather than fill the blank with a confident guess.

The skiff has not been declared ready for launching or for carrying passengers. Senn said a separate condition and launch-readiness review is planned once repairs are further along; the group is not treating finished-looking wood as proof the boat is seaworthy.

The shed itself is shared space. The group uses it under an agreement with the local boating association, which also schedules other users, and limited storage means members mark and put away parts at the end of each session so the area is clear for whoever comes next. Tools are introduced under the shed's supervision arrangements, not left out for unrestricted use.

The project runs on a materials grant, with members also paying session fees that go toward ordinary shed costs. Organizers have not disclosed a full restoration value for the skiff or claimed the finished boat will be worth more than what the project has spent on it.

The boating association said the project has prompted questions from others about access to future sessions, though it has not conducted a formal survey of interest.

Members say they have not set a launch date. Once the skiff is cleared for service, the group plans to discuss how it might be used and shared — a conversation, Senn said, that can wait until the boat itself says it's ready.

The skiff will remain the property of the community project, not become anyone's personal boat, once the work is done.
