# Hawthorn Gauge trades $1,000 for cash before an invoice comes due

Hawthorn Gauge received $39,000 immediately by selling a $40,000 customer invoice, paying $1,000 to close the gap between delivering an industrial order and collecting payment.

The fixtures maker delivered a batch of measuring fixtures to Pember Assembly in July and issued an invoice payable 60 calendar days after its issue date. But Hawthorn's 11 employees are paid weekly, and its material supplier requires payment within 30 calendar days of the supplier's invoice date.

That mismatch explains the benefit of the transaction, known as factoring: Hawthorn gets cash without waiting for the customer's payment deadline. The cost is a smaller receipt for the completed order, and the agreement leaves Hawthorn exposed to some risk if the customer does not pay.

"The invoice says we earned the money. Friday's payroll asks whether it is in the account," Hawthorn owner Lena Brack said.

Hawthorn sold the invoice to Rillstone Factors. Under their agreement, Pember will pay Rillstone rather than Hawthorn when it settles the bill. Rillstone does not receive an ownership share in Hawthorn through the transaction.

The $1,000 difference between the invoice's face value and Hawthorn's immediate payment is a 2.5% charge on the $40,000 invoice. It is not a stated annual interest rate. Hawthorn has given up that portion of the invoice's value in exchange for receiving the rest immediately.

The distinction matters when weighing financing choices. Brack said a conventional credit-line application was still under review when this payroll period arrived. The bank has not issued a final credit decision or a rate quote, so there is no bank offer in the record against which to compare the factoring charge.

For this payroll period, factoring provided cash while the credit application remained unresolved. That does not establish that factoring is cheaper than every loan, or that the bank would ultimately reject Hawthorn's application.

Nor does the transaction mean Pember has fallen behind. As of Aug. 3, no late payment had occurred on this invoice. Pember purchasing manager Daro Vale said the buyer's 60-day terms were published in its supplier agreement and had not changed for this order. Pember has accepted the fixtures, and the record contains no quality dispute.

Payment risk nevertheless remains. The agreement allows Rillstone to return an unpaid invoice to Hawthorn once 75 calendar days have passed since its issue date. That is 15 days after the customer's payment deadline. Selling the invoice therefore does not permanently remove all consequences of nonpayment from Hawthorn.

Rillstone also decides which invoices it will accept. It checks the invoiced customer's payment record and may reject an invoice even when the seller wants immediate cash.

"We can advance the payment date, but we do not turn a disputed order into an undisputed one," Rillstone manager Omi Keel said.

Hawthorn, which makes fixtures to customer drawings in a converted service garage rather than carrying a large finished-goods inventory, has used factoring on two of its last 14 invoices. It has not provided the fee for the other financed invoice, so those figures cannot establish an annual companywide financing cost.

Repeated use would bring additional transaction costs. For the July invoice, the trade-off is specific: Hawthorn received $39,000 immediately instead of waiting to collect $40,000, while retaining the risk that Rillstone could return the invoice if it remained unpaid after 75 days.
