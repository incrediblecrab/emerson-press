# Volunteers carry the Fellshore Ledger from sorting room to shop counter

Behind the Fellshore Ledger's office, delivery coordinator Rena Callen sorts bundles by destination in a borrowed room. Volunteers check the labels against a paper route sheet before loading bags and a handcart. Shelves of older issues share the space, ready for occasional questions about dates.

Getting the weekly newspaper out of that room is only the beginning.

"A bundle is not delivered just because it has left this room," Callen said. "We need to know the shop was open and somebody actually took it in."

The Ledger's small volunteer route carries print copies into local shops and public meeting places, where readers can encounter the newspaper without seeking out its website. As of Sept. 18, the paper is recruiting help with existing routes, not announcing a new edition or promising expansion into every neighborhood. The work involves checking what happened to each bundle and using that information to plan the next delivery.

At shopkeeper Elva Marr's counter, a small stack sits where customers wait for service. Her account of what they read begins with the notices.

"People turn first to the notices, then they tell me which meeting I have forgotten," Marr said.

The community center takes copies for its foyer, too. Readers do not have to attend a particular activity to pick one up. Those distribution points give the print paper a place in readers' ordinary stops, alongside the website the Ledger continues to maintain.

The route sheet measures something narrower than that readership: copies delivered. It does not show how many people eventually read a copy or pass it along. Returned or undelivered copies are kept separate from the number successfully placed at distribution points, and the route records provide no audited total of unique readers.

Volunteers also note closed premises and copies left unused from the previous week so the next route can be adjusted. The stops have changed as businesses opened, closed or altered their hours. Bad weather can change delivery timing, and the Ledger does not guarantee that every outlet will have copies at one exact hour.

New volunteers accompany an experienced delivery worker before taking a route alone. Callen keeps private contact information off the public distribution list.

The volunteers' responsibilities have a boundary beyond delivery, too. They do not decide the Ledger's editorial position or approve articles. If someone raises a concern along the route, a volunteer passes it back to the editorial office, which handles corrections, rather than promising a change at the doorstep.

The Ledger draws income from advertising and reader support. Its full accounts are not disclosed in the reporting materials, which make no claim that print distribution is profitable on its own.

For the delivery team, the next week's work takes shape in the details brought back from this one: whether a shop was open, whether someone accepted the bundle and whether older copies remained. Those notes return to the sorting room, where the labels and route sheet guide another round of deliveries.
