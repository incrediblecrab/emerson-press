Feds Award Record $14.2 Billion Contract for Massive Offshore Wind Farm

WASHINGTON — The Department of Energy on Thursday awarded a $14.2 billion contract to Horizon Marine Energy to build what officials called the largest single renewable energy development in U.S. history, a sprawling offshore wind installation planned for waters off the Cape Hartley coast.

The project calls for 280 turbines located 80 kilometers offshore, with a combined capacity of 4,200 megawatts.

"This contract moves the United States from a follower to a leader in offshore wind," Energy Secretary Isabel Makin said. "At full output, it will power more than 1.8 million homes."

Each turbine will be rated at 15 megawatts, a class not yet in commercial use anywhere and scheduled to enter production in 2026. Horizon CEO Peter Strand said the company expects to begin installing offshore foundations in early 2027, with first power delivered to the grid by mid-2029.

The array would be more than the entire U.S. offshore wind fleet combined; the country currently has 3,800 megawatts of offshore wind operating, all on smaller arrays along the Northeast coast, according to the department.

Independent analysts cautioned that the project's actual output will run well below its nameplate figure. Offshore wind capacity factors typically range from 40% to 55%, they said, meaning the array would average roughly 1,680 megawatts of real generation at the low end of that range.

Connecting the offshore turbines to the mainland grid will require an undersea high-voltage direct current cable stretching 92 kilometers to an onshore substation. Three commercial fishing associations raised concerns that portions of the cable route cross productive lobster grounds and called for an independent impact assessment before construction begins.

The contract requires Horizon to meet domestic content requirements for at least half of the project's major components, a threshold the company said will require substantial new U.S. manufacturing. Horizon has partnered with Strandvik Turbine Systems, a Norwegian manufacturer, to build the 15-megawatt turbines at a new plant in Portview, Maine.

The 15-megawatt turbines also require installation vessels larger than any currently operating in U.S. waters. Horizon has contracted for two purpose-built vessels from a shipyard in Gulfport.

Strand said Horizon has secured $6.1 billion in committed financing and is seeking the remainder through a federal loan guarantee program.

The Department of Energy estimates the project will support 7,400 construction jobs and 600 permanent operations positions at peak activity. Once operational, Horizon will pay the federal government $420 million annually in lease fees.

Cape Hartley sits in a wind resource area that the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration classifies as "Excellent," with average offshore wind speeds of 9.2 meters per second at hub height. Environmental regulators approved the project area under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act following a three-year environmental review.

The coastal state's legislature passed a complementary grid integration bill in April requiring utilities to build additional onshore transmission lines to accommodate the new generation.

The contract includes financial penalties if the project fails to deliver commercial power by December 2030, a deadline officials say is intended to keep the ambitious timeline on track.

The project marks a significant bet on an emerging class of larger, more powerful turbines that have not yet been deployed commercially anywhere in the world, and on new domestic manufacturing and shipbuilding capacity that does not yet exist.
