# At Ashmere seed library, volunteers keep the seeds' stories — and their gaps

One paper packet carries a family's informal name for a bean. Another identifies its contents only as a red flower. At the Ashmere seed library, those descriptions belong in the record, even when they cannot tell the next gardener exactly what is inside.

The small donated packets sit in drawers in a community garden's meeting shed. Volunteer cataloger Mara Vell asks donors for the name they use, the harvest year and what they know about where the seed came from. An incomplete answer stays incomplete; volunteers do not turn it into a confident commercial variety name.

That distinction shapes the volunteers' work: making donated seeds available while preserving what is known about them and showing where the information ends. The labels distinguish a donor's description from information checked against another supplied record.

"The name on the envelope is part of the story, but it may not be enough to tell the next gardener exactly what will grow," Vell said.

Donor Eda Noll brings a copy of a handwritten record with her latest bean donation. She has kept the record through several growing seasons, giving the library information beyond a name on an envelope. But her account comes with a limit.

"I can tell you which summer they came from. I cannot promise your garden will give you the summer I had," Noll said.

The available records include no germination test result or guarantee of yield for the seeds described. The library does not present its collection as certified commercial stock or verified genetic preservation.

Volunteers also keep older and newer donations separate enough to retain their collection dates, rather than combining them under one fresh label. A packet may be withdrawn if its record or condition raises a question the team cannot resolve.

For recipients, the arrangement is straightforward. Visitors may take the offered small packets without paying a purchase price. A future donation is welcome, but taking seeds does not create a debt to return the same quantity or variety. Volunteers limit packet sizes so the available stock can reach more than one grower.

The library began as a seed-swap table at the garden's annual gathering. Its drawers came later, from a closed stationery shop. The shed opens during staffed garden sessions rather than operating as an unattended public seed dispenser.

A simple stock list helps volunteers avoid advertising packets that have already been taken. It does not identify individual recipients or treat each seed as a separate participating gardener.

The garden invites recipients to bring back observations, treating them as reports from particular growing conditions rather than controlled comparisons. It has supplied no quantified effect on townwide biodiversity or household food costs.

A planned display will explain some labels and donor histories with permission, without publishing donors' private contact details. For now, the records accompanying the seeds retain both kinds of description: the family's name for its bean and the flower known only as red.
