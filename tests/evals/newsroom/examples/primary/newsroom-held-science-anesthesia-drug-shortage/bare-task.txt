Fire at Indiana Plant Sparks National Propofol Shortage

CLEARFIELD, Ind. — A fire that destroyed a major manufacturing line at Meridia Pharma's Clearfield plant last week has triggered a national shortage of propofol, the anesthetic used in an estimated 50 million surgical procedures a year in the United States.

The fire broke out Oct. 22 at Meridia's primary U.S. propofol facility, destroying the plant's largest fill-and-finish line. That line had produced about 40% of Meridia's U.S. propofol supply. Meridia holds a 58% share of the national propofol market, making the loss significant for hospitals nationwide.

The cause of the fire remains under investigation by the Indiana State Fire Marshal's office. No injuries were reported.

The Food and Drug Administration placed propofol on its critical shortage list Oct. 25 and issued import facilitation orders for two overseas manufacturers, Pharmax Belgium and Adena Laboratorios of Spain, to help ease the supply gap. The agency said it expects imported product from Pharmax Belgium to begin arriving within three weeks. Officials cautioned that the facilitation orders do not waive good manufacturing practice requirements; imported product must still undergo FDA inspection or documentation of equivalence.

Hospital procurement officers said propofol allocations from distributors have already been cut by 25% to 35% at the institutions they represent. Hospital networks in the Southeast and Midwest reported the most severe constraints, a result of their greater reliance on Meridia as a regional distributor.

Dr. Patricia Lam, anesthesiology chief at a regional hospital, said providers have shifted some elective cases to alternative agents but that propofol's properties are hard to replace.

"We have shifted some elective cases to alternative agents, but propofol's pharmacokinetics — particularly how quickly patients wake up — cannot be fully replicated by any single substitute," Lam said.

The two intravenous anesthetics most commonly substituted for propofol, etomidate and ketamine, have different sedation depths and side-effect profiles, according to reporting notes on the shortage. Lam also noted that anesthesia training programs focus heavily on propofol protocols, meaning a switch to other agents requires rapid in-service retraining for providers.

The FDA said it is monitoring substitution rates to track whether alternative agents are being used safely in patient populations for whom they may be less appropriate.

Meridia CEO Dr. Carlos Estrada said the company is redirecting output from its secondary manufacturing plant in Arizona to help offset the loss and expects the damaged Clearfield line to be rebuilt within 12 to 18 months.

No elective surgery cancellations had been publicly confirmed as of Tuesday, the most recent reporting date, but hospital administrators said decisions about which cases to prioritize were being made daily.

Meridia's stock fell 18% in the week following the fire before partially recovering after the FDA's import facilitation announcement.

The shortage has drawn attention from drug policy researchers, who have cited it as a case study in the risks of single-source dependence, noting that one company held a majority of the U.S. market while relying on a single primary domestic manufacturing site. Propofol's relatively short shelf life — 24 months from manufacture — also makes it harder to stockpile than solid oral medications, complicating efforts to build reserves against future disruptions.

Propofol is currently the highest-volume item among 47 active shortages of sterile injectable medications listed in the federal drug shortage database.

Congressional members from Indiana have asked the FDA to provide monthly status reports on the shortage as the agency and Meridia work to restore supply.
