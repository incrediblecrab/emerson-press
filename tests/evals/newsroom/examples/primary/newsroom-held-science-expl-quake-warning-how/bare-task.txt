**How earthquake early warning systems work — and their limits**

When the ground begins to break along a fault, it takes time for the shaking to spread. That delay, measured in seconds, is the foundation of earthquake early warning systems now operating in parts of the United States and several other countries.

Earthquakes generate two main types of seismic waves, according to reporting notes on the technology. P-waves travel fastest but cause relatively little damage. S-waves follow behind, arriving slower but carrying most of the destructive shaking energy. Early warning systems are built around that gap: sensors detect the P-waves first, then estimate the earthquake's magnitude and location. That information is transmitted electronically — a signal that moves faster than the S-waves still traveling through rock. The result is a warning that can, in some cases, arrive before the worst shaking does.

How much warning depends on distance. Areas close to the epicenter may get only a few seconds of notice; places farther away can get more than a minute. But there's a catch known as the "blind zone": locations nearest the epicenter often get no warning at all, because the destructive S-waves arrive before the system can process data and send an alert.

Even a short warning can matter. Automated systems can use five to 10 seconds to slow trains, open fire station doors, halt industrial equipment or prompt surgeons to pause procedures, according to the notes.

In the U.S., the system is called ShakeAlert, operated by the U.S. Geological Survey and covering California, Oregon and Washington through roughly 1,500 seismic sensors. The network has cost about $460 million to build, including sensors, software and integration with public alert systems, as of 2023.

Japan's system, in operation since the early 2000s, has the longest track record. During a catastrophic magnitude 9.0 subduction earthquake, it sent an alert about 90 seconds before strong shaking reached a major population center, giving residents and transit operators time to react.

Cell phones play a growing role. Emergency alert systems can push warnings to millions of phones at once without a separate app, and the alert sounds even if a phone is silenced.

Still, the technology has hard limits. "The goal is not to prevent an earthquake," said Dr. Kwame Osei, a seismologist at Pacific Basin University. "It is to give automated systems and prepared individuals a window to take a protective action." Warnings are issued only after a rupture has already begun — the systems cannot predict quakes in advance. And in a megathrust earthquake directly beneath a city, Osei said, even perfect detection would give people above the fault no warning at all. "The practical limit on warning time is physics, not technology," he said.

Accuracy also improves over time. Initial magnitude estimates can shift as more sensors report data, meaning an alert may be updated within seconds of being issued. Researchers, including Osei, are testing machine-learning methods to estimate final magnitude from just the first half-second of P-wave data, which could extend warning times further.

Sensor density matters, too: more sensors shrink the blind zone and sharpen estimates, but they also raise maintenance costs. That expense helps explain why much of Latin America, Africa and Southeast Asia — despite facing high seismic risk — still lack functional early warning coverage.

Systems are also designed to avoid crying wolf. False alerts, sent for events that don't produce damaging shaking, can erode public trust, creating a persistent tradeoff between sensitivity and reliability.

For now, experts say the best individual response to an alert remains simple: drop, cover and hold on under a sturdy surface. The warning window, they note, is usually too short to evacuate a building.
