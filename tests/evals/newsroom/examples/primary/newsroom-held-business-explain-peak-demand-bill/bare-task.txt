**Same Energy, Smaller Bill: How a Laundromat Cut Costs by Timing, Not Trimming, Its Power Use**

Arden Wash used exactly 5,000 kilowatt-hours of electricity in both May and June 2026, according to bills from Cobalt Municipal Power — yet its total charges dropped by $135, from $1,160 to $1,025. The reason lies not in how much power the laundromat used, but in when it used it.

Cobalt's commercial tariff bills customers for three things: a flat $20 monthly charge, 12 cents per kilowatt-hour of energy consumed, and $9 per kilowatt of "billed peak demand." Because Arden Wash's total energy use didn't change, its energy charge stayed fixed at $600 in both months. What changed was the demand charge, which fell from $540 in May to $405 in June as billed peak demand dropped from 60 kilowatts to 45. Those figures exclude separately itemized taxes, which were not part of the comparison.

The distinction between the two charges is key. Kilowatt-hours measure total accumulated electricity use over the whole month — the laundromat's overall workload. Billed kilowatts measure something different: the rate of electricity draw during a single, specific stretch of time. Under Cobalt's tariff, that stretch is the highest 15-minute average load recorded on weekdays between 4 and 7 p.m. Whatever demand a business hits during that peak window effectively sets its demand charge for the entire month, regardless of how it used electricity the rest of the time.

That's what changed at Arden Wash. Owner Mara Selt installed timers to stagger the startup of several large dryers rather than switching them on simultaneously. The bills show lower billed demand after the change. Spreading out machine starts didn't reduce the electricity needed to finish the laundry — each load still draws the same energy — but it prevented multiple dryers from drawing power at the same instant, which is what determines the demand figure.

Utility analyst Oren Quill said the mechanism is straightforward but easy to misunderstand. "The bill remembers the busiest quarter-hour, even after the machines have gone quiet," he said. A single crowded interval, even a brief one, can lock in the demand charge for weeks afterward.

The change comes with trade-offs. Selt said some customers prefer starting several loads at once because their time at the laundromat is limited. "The meter rewards spreading things out," she said. "A customer may need to be finished before the bus leaves." Balancing customer convenience against demand costs is an ongoing calculation for the business.

Several caveats apply. The bills do not isolate every variable that could differ between May and June, including weather, customer volume or machine use, so the timer change cannot be treated as the sole cause of the lower reading. Cobalt offers laundromats a review of past interval data, but does not guarantee that a business will repeat its lowest demand figure the following month. The residential tariff, unlike the commercial one, does not include a demand charge at all. And while Arden Wash's rent is unaffected by its utility costs — its lease includes no automatic reduction tied to lower bills — the electricity savings accrue directly to the business.

The bills also do not establish any reduction in carbon emissions from using the same energy at different times, since total consumption was unchanged. Arden Wash, which has operated in the same storefront since 2008 offering both self-service and staffed washing, illustrates a dynamic increasingly familiar to commercial ratepayers: under demand-based pricing, timing can matter as much as total usage.
