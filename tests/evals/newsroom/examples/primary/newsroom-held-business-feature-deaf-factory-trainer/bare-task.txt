**At Bracken Spur, a Deaf mentor reshapes how the factory teaches metalwork**

By [Staff Writer]

The training corner at Bracken Spur Fabrication used to hold empty shipping cartons. Now it holds a mounted screen, and Vara Chenro is often the one deciding what appears on it.

Chenro, a Deaf metalworker who has spent 12 years on the floor at Bracken Spur, became a paid training mentor in January. The role has put her at the center of an overhaul of how the company teaches new employees to work with brackets and enclosures for commercial equipment — work that, until recently, relied on spoken demonstrations paired with a printed checklist.

That checklist, Chenro and other trainers said, never captured everything an instructor explained aloud. The gap surfaced concretely in June 2026, according to a training note from that month, when Chenro stopped a demonstration video because the camera angle hid the instructor's hand position — the exact detail a trainee would need to copy the technique correctly.

"Access is not putting words under a picture that still hides the thing you need to see," Chenro wrote in comments supplied for this report.

The company's revised materials now include captioned demonstrations, closer camera framing and numbered written steps, all reviewed by production trainers before use. Chenro helped select camera angles and check instructions as part of her paid mentor duties — not, she and the company were careful to note, as a volunteer accessibility side project.

The fixes are not always simple. A caption alone cannot show a movement the camera failed to capture, trainers said, which is why the team sometimes reshoots a demonstration rather than patch it with text.

Bracken Spur has put real resources behind the effort: $2,400 for screens and mounting equipment, plus 60 staff hours recorded in preparing the first batch of videos. The equipment cost does not include a price on those staff hours, and the company has not estimated what future revisions will cost.

The first group to use the new materials — 22 trainees, including hearing workers and people new to the factory's technical vocabulary — could pause and replay demonstrations, though all still required supervised practice and an instructor's signoff before working unsupervised. Seventeen of the 22 reported using the replay function, though the factory has not measured how much, if at all, individual performance changed as a result.

Trainer Omi Raylen said the value is in not making every trainee wait for a live repeat of an explanation. "A question asked twice is still cheaper than work done once on the wrong understanding," Raylen said.

The company continues to provide individually agreed communication support for workers, officials said, rather than treating the videos as a substitute for that support.

Bracken Spur, which employs 64 people, has no matched injury-rate or defect-rate study to show the new training format has changed outcomes on the floor. Company officials describe the effort as a work in progress rather than a finished system.

Little is documented about what drove Chenro toward this work beyond her mentor role — the record does not describe her personal motivations, any change in her hearing, or any special skill attributed to her deafness. What is documented is a specific, repeated judgment call: whether a training video shows what a worker actually needs to see.

For now, that judgment sits largely with Chenro, watching playback in a corner that used to hold cardboard boxes.
