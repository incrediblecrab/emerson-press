# Why Orlan College's $40 million endowment cannot simply cover its budget gap

Orlan College reports a $40 million endowment and projects an $800,000 operating-budget gap. But the endowment is a pool of invested assets, much of its planned spending tied to specific purposes, rather than a checking account available for any college bill.

The college's June 2026 financial statement puts a value on those assets at a reporting date. It does not establish that the entire amount is available for immediate spending. Understanding what the endowment can contribute requires separating its total value from its annual draw, then separating restricted spending from money available for general operations.

As of Aug. 31, the college has not adopted its final operating budget. Shorter library evening hours remain one proposed saving, and trustees have requested alternatives. The endowment's limits help explain the budget problem, but they do not settle which expenses the college should cut.

## What is already going into the budget?

The adopted annual spending plan calls for a $1.6 million endowment draw, equivalent to 4% of the stated $40 million valuation. Of that draw, $1.4 million supports purposes specified in gift agreements, including scholarships and named teaching posts.

The remaining $200,000 can support general operations under the college's records. That money is already included in the operating budget used to calculate the projected $800,000 gap. Subtracting it again would count the same money twice; it is not an unused contribution that would reduce the gap to $600,000.

The distinction also means the full $1.6 million draw cannot be treated as freely available deficit funding. Most of it has designated uses.

"An asset can belong to the college without being available for any bill the college chooses," Treasurer Nessa Quill said.

## Why not sell investments?

Selling an investment and obtaining permission to spend the proceeds for a different purpose are separate matters. Under the college's restricted-gift agreements, a sale changes the form of the holding without removing the donor's specified spending limits.

The college says changing a restricted gift's use requires a separate process. The headline asset total does not establish that those changes can be made.

There is also a difference between an investment's reported value and cash available immediately. Approximately $9 million of the endowment valuation is in property and other investments that cannot immediately be converted to cash at a guaranteed price.

The endowment's market value can change with investment performance. The $40 million figure is therefore neither an unrestricted bank balance nor a promised future sale price.

Quill says spending decisions also account for future years in which scholarships and teaching posts will need support.

## What remains a budget choice?

Student representative Daro Linn says the distinction between restricted assets and unrestricted money matters, but it does not answer every question about spending priorities.

"Explaining a restriction is not the same as explaining why the library is the part asked to close earlier," Linn said.

Trustees have requested alternatives that include delaying administrative software purchases and raising additional unrestricted funds. No new unrestricted donation has been pledged in the supplied record, so fundraising remains a proposed response rather than money already available to close the gap.

The financial statement does not give trustees authority to spend every invested dollar immediately. It also does not establish that shorter library hours, or any other particular budget choice, are unavoidable. The endowment explains why the college cannot treat $40 million as ready cash; the unfinished operating budget leaves the spending decisions open.
