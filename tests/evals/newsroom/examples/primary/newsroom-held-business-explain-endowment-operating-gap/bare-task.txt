# Why Orlan College's $40 million endowment cannot simply erase its budget gap

Orlan College reports a $40 million endowment and a projected $800,000 operating-budget gap. But those figures describe different things: the value of invested assets and a shortfall in the operating budget. The endowment is not a $40 million checking account available to cover any expense.

The college's June 2026 financial statement puts a value on its endowment holdings at that reporting date. How much those holdings can provide for operations depends on the college's spending plan, donor restrictions and the investments themselves. As of Aug. 31, the final operating budget has not been adopted.

## What is available for operations?

The college's adopted annual endowment spending plan calls for a $1.6 million draw, equivalent to 4% of the reported $40 million valuation. Most of that draw — $1.4 million — supports purposes specified in gift agreements, including scholarships and named teaching posts.

The remaining $200,000 can support general operations under the college's records. But it is already included in the operating budget used to calculate the projected $800,000 gap.

That distinction matters to the arithmetic. Subtracting the $200,000 again would count the same money twice. The projected gap remains $800,000 after that planned contribution.

The restricted portion supports college activities, but it cannot simply be redirected to whichever bills need paying. The college says changing a restricted gift's use requires a separate process; permission cannot be inferred from the headline endowment total.

“An asset can belong to the college without being available for any bill the college chooses,” Treasurer Nessa Quill said.

## Why not sell investments?

Selling an investment and gaining permission to spend the proceeds are separate issues.

Under the college's restricted-gift agreements, selling an asset changes the form of the holding without removing the donor's specified spending limits. Turning a restricted investment into cash does not, by itself, make that cash available for general operations.

Some holdings also cannot readily be sold at a certain price. Approximately $9 million of the endowment valuation is in property and other investments that are not immediately convertible to cash at a guaranteed price.

That does not establish that the rest is unrestricted spending money. Liquidity — the ability to turn an investment into cash — is different from authority to use it for a particular expense.

The endowment's market value can also change with investment performance. The reported $40 million is not a promised future sale price. Quill says spending decisions account for future years in which scholarships and teaching posts will continue to need support.

## Does that settle the budget debate?

No. Explaining why the endowment is not freely available does not establish that any particular operating cut is necessary.

Student representative Daro Linn says the distinction matters but leaves questions about priorities within the unrestricted budget.

“Explaining a restriction is not the same as explaining why the library is the part asked to close earlier,” Linn said.

The college has proposed shorter library evening hours as one possible saving, not an adopted final-budget decision. Trustees have requested alternatives, including delayed administrative software purchases and additional unrestricted fundraising. No new unrestricted donation has been pledged in the supplied record.

The financial statement therefore answers neither side of the debate on its own. It does not give trustees authority to spend every invested dollar immediately, and it does not establish that no other budget choice exists. The endowment's size helps describe the college's assets; it does not measure the cash available to close this year's operating gap.
