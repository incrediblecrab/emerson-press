**As Nuclear Waste Site Wins License, a New Mexico Town Splits Over What Comes Next**

RIDGEMONT, N.M. — Claudette Moreau has lived on the same street for more than 50 years, long enough to watch her students graduate and leave, long enough to file seven administrative challenges against a project she has opposed since 1994. This month, it received its construction license anyway.

"My students graduated and moved away. I stayed because someone has to," said Moreau, 72, a retired teacher who founded a citizens' group that has fought the Granite Basin deep geological nuclear waste repository for three decades. "In thirty years of fighting this, I have not changed one vote. But I have made sure they were slow."

The repository, about 22 miles from this town of 3,800 people, received its construction license in April. Waste acceptance is projected to begin in 2032, following an expected 400 temporary construction jobs and, later, about 180 permanent positions with average wages of $68,000, according to Ridgemont County officials.

For Moreau, the license is a loss she expected. For her neighbor Hector Vega, it is something closer to relief.

Vega, 55, whose ranch borders the site, has cautiously supported the project since 2019, when the federal government tied a $140 million economic development package — a highway bypass, a water treatment plant upgrade and a job training center — to the repository's approval.

"I don't love having a nuclear waste facility next door," Vega said. "But I live next door to poverty too. And poverty doesn't come with $140 million in infrastructure money."

Ridgemont's population has fallen 22% since 2000, and school enrollment is down 35%. The town's grocery store, hardware store and two restaurants closed between 2015 and 2023. County officials say that without the repository, the fiscal trajectory is continued decline.

The Ridgemont County Commission voted 4-1 to accept the development package, said Commission Chair Leticia Durán. The dissenting vote cited concerns about transport risk along the county's two-lane state highway. According to the licensing record, waste shipments will move by existing rail lines, not the county highway.

The Kwena Nation, whose members hold treaty rights to land within 30 kilometers of the site, formally objected throughout the licensing process. Tribal liaison James Silvercloud said the tribe was never meaningfully consulted, only notified.

"There is a difference between being consulted and being told what has already been decided," Silvercloud said. "We have always been told." He said the tribe will pursue legal remedies but does not expect to prevail; he said the case matters for establishing a record that future tribal advocates can use.

The Nuclear Regulatory Authority determined that the site's geology met long-term isolation standards. Three independent geologists who reviewed the record supported that finding; two others published dissenting papers on water infiltration modeling.

Moreau said she has accepted that her challenges will not stop construction. "At my age, winning means the record is correct," she said. "It means someone reads these filings in a hundred years and knows this was not done with consent." She said she has voted in every election for 54 years and will keep voting in every one she has left.

Vega said the repository has not yet affected his property value, though he doubts he could sell easily. He plans to retire before waste deliveries begin, leaving the ranch's future to his son. "It's his problem now," he said. "I made it survivable for him to have a problem."

Moreau and Vega live eight miles apart. They have never met in person. Ridgemont's debate over the repository has played out instead through public hearings and county newsletters — parallel monologues from neighbors who agree on almost nothing except that someone has to stay.
