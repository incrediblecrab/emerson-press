# For Plover Reed Flowers, standing cafe orders offer a measure of certainty

Nessa Sorn separated shorter flower stems for cafe tables from taller ones reserved for a weekend event Aug. 12, sorting a harvest destined for two kinds of customers: those with regular orders and those whose occasional purchases can bring a higher price.

At Plover Reed Flowers, that distinction shapes more than the height of an arrangement. Six cafes have standing orders totaling 900 stems a week during an agreed summer season. Event orders can pay twice as much per stem, but they require more sorting and do not arrive reliably every week.

The grower’s choice is not simply between a low price and a high one. It is between different demands on a small farm’s flowers, workers and delivery time — and different degrees of certainty about what customers will buy.

Plover Reed grows decorative cut flowers on two rented acres, with Sorn and two seasonal workers doing the work. The cafe orders pay 60 cents per delivered stem, yielding $540 in weekly gross receipts when the farm supplies all 900 contracted stems.

Event orders can pay $1.20 per stem. Neither figure is profit: Growing, labor, packing and delivery costs still must be covered. The farm has not produced a complete margin comparison among its cafe, event and farm-gate sales.

“The regular order tells me something about next week. It does not tell me what every bed will produce,” Sorn said.

The cafe agreements give the farm some room to respond to that uncertainty. Rather than requiring the same flower variety every week, they allow seasonal substitutions within a stated color range.

That flexibility has already been used. The farm recorded two weeks of substituted varieties after a crop bloomed later than expected, and all six cafes accepted the replacements. That experience does not establish that every future substitution will be accepted, or that event customers will offer similar flexibility.

The cafes also have room to adjust. Customers can change quantities with seven days’ notice, and the agreements cover the summer season, not year-round purchases.

For cafe owner Omi Holt, the shorter stems serve a practical purpose: They let customers see across small tables.

“The arrangement has to leave space for lunch, not just look good before anyone sits down,” Holt said.

That preference gives shorter stems a place in the farm’s orders, while taller stems can be reserved for event work. But serving several small customers brings its own demands.

In a former potting shed used as the packing room, buckets are marked for each cafe rather than individual diners. The seasonal workers are paid for packing and delivery preparation as well as cutting flowers. The farm makes two cafe delivery rounds each week, with each round taking approximately three hours.

Many small bunches add handling work that a single large collection order may not require. Event work, meanwhile, calls for more sorting by color and length. A higher receipt per stem alone does not settle which order provides the better return.

Sorn also sells mixed bouquets at the farm gate, but passersby have made no commitment to buy surplus flowers.

For now, the standing cafe orders provide a defined summer outlet for part of the harvest, with flexibility over varieties and limits on certainty. The stems separated Aug. 12 reflect that balance: shorter flowers for regular tables, taller ones for an occasional event, each carrying a different price and workload.
