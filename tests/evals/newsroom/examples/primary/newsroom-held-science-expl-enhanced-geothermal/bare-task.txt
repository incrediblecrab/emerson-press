# Enhanced geothermal aims to turn deep underground heat into round-the-clock power

A renewable power source that runs regardless of the weather is drawing fresh attention from investors, oil companies and federal researchers: heat trapped deep beneath the Earth’s surface.

Enhanced geothermal systems, known as EGS, could make that heat accessible far beyond the volcanic regions where conventional geothermal plants operate. As of April 22, 2025, drilling advances and government-backed demonstrations are helping move the technology forward. But high costs, earthquake risks and questions about how long underground reservoirs will last remain significant obstacles.

## How does it work?

Conventional geothermal plants tap naturally occurring hot water and steam in underground reservoirs near the surface. That limits them largely to geologically active places such as Iceland, the western United States and the Philippines.

Enhanced geothermal creates a reservoir where none exists naturally. Developers drill into deep, hot, dry rock and inject pressurized water to fracture it. Fluid then circulates through those fractures, picking up heat that can be used to generate electricity.

Earth’s temperature generally increases about 45 to 54 degrees Fahrenheit for every six-tenths of a mile of depth. In most continental locations, rocks roughly 3 to 6 miles down are hot enough for electricity generation.

Plants can also use heat that is below the temperature needed for high-pressure steam turbines. Binary cycle plants transfer geothermal heat to a secondary fluid with a lower boiling point than water, vaporizing it to drive a turbine.

Novadrill Energy, founded by former oil field engineers, is targeting wells in the Great Basin reaching 437 degrees Fahrenheit for that approach.

## Why the new interest?

Geothermal’s central attraction is its ability to supply continuous, or baseload, electricity. Unlike wind and solar, it does not depend on weather or daylight, making it a complement to those renewable sources.

Its surface footprint is also relatively small. Plants can be located at industrial facilities, mines or near population centers.

Geothermal power emits about 5 to 50 grams of carbon dioxide per kilowatt-hour, depending on fluid composition and plant design, compared with about 490 grams for natural gas generation.

Meanwhile, technologies developed for oil and gas, including horizontal drilling and precision directional steering, are applicable to geothermal wells. Venture capital investors and oil companies seeking new uses for their expertise are investing in the sector.

“Enhanced geothermal sits at the intersection of oil and gas drilling technology and renewable energy,” said Tomoko Hara, a geothermal energy researcher at Basin Energy Institute. “The resource is almost everywhere. The challenge is the cost and risk of the exploration well.”

The U.S. Department of Energy is funding demonstrations at multiple sites. Its Utah FORGE project in the Milford Basin has created fracture networks in hot, dry granite about 2 miles underground.

## What stands in the way?

Deep wells cost $10 million to $30 million each. Multiple wells and fracturing can push upfront project costs to $50 million or more before electricity production begins.

Fracturing also creates uncertainty about how fluid will move through the reservoir and can trigger earthquakes. In 2017, an EGS project in an Asian city caused a magnitude 5.5 earthquake that damaged buildings and halted the program. Developers use real-time seismic monitoring and injection pressure controls to limit that risk.

Reservoir longevity is another unresolved issue, Hara said: As heat is extracted and rock cools, output could decline.

The Energy Department aims to reduce EGS electricity costs to $45 per megawatt-hour by 2035, comparable to current natural gas combined-cycle generation. For now, the technology’s enormous theoretical resource is not the same as affordable, dependable power. Turning one into the other is the challenge.
