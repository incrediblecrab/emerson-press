# Ospen Toolworks employees agree to buy shop in $1 million cooperative deal

Employees at Ospen Toolworks have agreed to buy the shop through a cooperative in a $1 million deal signed Monday by retiring owner Halen Pike, with 18 workers committing money to become members while eight others can keep their jobs without buying in.

The agreement sets an Oct. 1 ownership transfer, subject to the lender’s final equipment valuation. It is a signed sale agreement, not a completed transfer, and it does not guarantee permanent employment, dividends or a future resale value for membership shares.

The distinction between working at Ospen and becoming an owner is central to the arrangement. Of the shop’s 26 employees, excluding Pike, 18 have agreed to become founding cooperative members. Each will contribute $5,000, providing $90,000 toward the purchase.

The eight employees who are not joining can remain in their current jobs at the same base pay and apply for membership later. Keeping a job does not require making the founding contribution; joining the cooperative means putting money into the purchase and participating in member voting.

Nonmember machinist Iri Bell said the $5,000 contribution is beyond her current savings, although she wants the shop to remain open.

“I can be glad the shop survives without being able to buy a piece of it this month,” Bell said.

The cooperative is considering installment payments for later members but has not adopted a policy. That leaves the terms of any future installment option unresolved as the founding members prepare for the planned transfer.

Member voting will follow a one-person, one-vote rule, rather than giving greater weight to job titles or years of service. That does not mean all members will vote on daily production decisions. The cooperative’s five-member board will appoint a general manager.

Along with the $90,000 in member capital, the purchase will be financed through a $510,000 community-lender loan and $400,000 in seller financing. Those three sources total the $1 million purchase price.

A separate $250,000 working-capital facility has been approved. It is not part of the purchase price, and the reporting record does not establish that any of it has been drawn. The lender requires quarterly financial reports and has not promised to cover future operating losses.

The shop makes small batches of replacement parts for food-processing equipment and water pumps. Its largest customer accounted for 28% of last year’s revenue and has not committed to a new long-term contract, leaving a significant source of business uncertain.

“We are buying the responsibility for the next order, not a guarantee that it will come,” founding member Toma Wren said.

Under the agreement, Pike will remain for six months as a paid adviser on customer relationships and quoting work. He had explored selling to a competitor that wanted the customer list but had made no written offer for the plant.

Ospen opened in 1987 and uses both computer-controlled lathes and an older manual milling machine. The employees’ agreement provides a route to cooperative ownership, but the planned October transfer still depends on the equipment valuation, and ownership will carry business risks rather than a promise of returns or lasting jobs.
