Terminal to End Routine Sunday Loading as Vesper Quay Order Book Shrinks 25%

VESPER QUAY — The Vesper Quay Grain Terminal will stop routine Sunday loading in October after booking sharply fewer autumn export orders than last year, the terminal announced Monday.

The terminal has signed autumn export orders for 180,000 tons, down from 240,000 tons at the same point last year — a 25% decline in booked volume. That figure reflects orders on the books, not total grain production in the region.

Most of the drop traces to one customer. Pelden Grain Trading, which had booked 40,000 tons at this point last year, canceled its planned order for this autumn after changing its storage arrangements. That cancellation accounts for 40,000 tons of the 60,000-ton year-over-year decline. The terminal has not explained the remaining 20,000 tons.

The terminal's new seasonal-staff roster allocates 4,800 paid hours, down from 6,000 in last autumn's roster, a 20% reduction. The terminal did not supply figures for actual hours worked last season or for how hours are allocated among individual employees.

Nineteen permanent jobs will remain in place, but 12 seasonal employees will receive shorter contracts.

Operations director Mara Keld said the cut in hours is smaller than the cut in booked volume because inspection and maintenance work will continue regardless of loading volume. Weekday truck-receiving hours will not change, she said.

"We are matching the loading roster to signed ships, not predicting the size of the harvest," Keld said.

The terminal has offered affected seasonal staff first consideration for unscheduled vessel work, without guaranteeing additional hours.

Growers with existing delivery contracts may still deliver their contracted quantities, the terminal said. It has not announced a new farmgate price.

Lora Flint, chair of the local grower association, said the effect on farms will vary. Some members can store grain themselves and wait out slower delivery schedules, she said, while others depend on prompt delivery slots and have less flexibility if loading days are reduced.

Seasonal loader Evin Moss said the shortened season creates a gap in his own work calendar. Moss also works at a winter warehouse, but that job does not begin until November.

"A shorter season leaves a space in the calendar that the next job does not automatically fill," Moss said.

The terminal handles grain from 63 contracted farms and also stores grain for two food manufacturers under separate contracts; that storage business is not included in the 180,000-ton export figure.

The port authority said it has not changed the terminal's berth allocation or handling charges in connection with the announcement.

The terminal's oldest silo dates to 1981; a newer covered conveyor opened in 2018.

The reporting record does not include a regional harvest estimate for this autumn, and no evidence has been presented connecting the decline in booked orders to broader trade policy.
