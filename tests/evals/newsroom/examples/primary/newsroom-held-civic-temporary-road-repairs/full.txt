Loamford crews return to patched streets — but the second pass was always the plan

LOAMFORD — Crews are coming back to two streets where water-main work ended in July, but the city says this isn't a redo. It's the second of two steps that were in the project plan from the start.

The works department temporarily patched the excavated strips after replacing the water main, restoring them for traffic but not as the final road surface, according to the department. Final resurfacing is planned for October.

"A surface that lets the street reopen is not necessarily the surface we intend to leave there," works engineer Arven Cole said.

The city's engineering brief requires checks of the filled trenches before the contractor places the final surface over them. The brief cites two reasons for the delay: settlement still occurring in the new fill, and scheduling conflicts with other utility work. Those reasons are specific to this project, not a standing rule that road repairs must sit unfinished for months.

Both stages — temporary patch and final resurfacing — were part of the original plan, though the first notice sent to residents used the word "repairs" without explaining that the work would happen in two parts. That vague notice is at the center of resident frustration.

"If it is temporary, tell us what that means and who comes back when it starts breaking up," said Mira Pell, a resident representative.

Residents have reported uneven joins where the patched strips meet the older pavement, especially at points where bicycles cross the edges. The department said it will address problems that need attention now rather than ask people to wait until October, and that the contractor remains responsible for maintaining the temporary work under the project agreement. Reports on the road surface go to the city's inspection team, not water-meter staff.

The works department has recorded $8,000 in costs for the completed temporary work. It estimates the final resurfacing at $38,000, work that has not yet been done or paid for. The record shows no finding of contractor misconduct and no evidence the city paid twice for the same completed job.

The final treatment will cover the affected carriageway sections — not every side street in the area. Its exact timing depends on the outcome of the trench checks and on finding a work window that fits the contractor's material specifications; the project record does not include a weather forecast or a guaranteed completion date.

A separate communications cable job was coordinated with the water-main work so it wouldn't require reopening the same strip again soon after. Before resurfacing begins, the department plans to mark the work areas and circulate access arrangements. Pedestrian access to homes will be maintained throughout; vehicle access may be restricted in parts of the work zone at times.

One unrelated wrinkle remains on the street signs themselves, which still carry an older neighborhood spelling that doesn't match the current utility work map.

In response to the confusion over the initial notice, the council has asked that future communications distinguish clearly between temporary reinstatement and completed resurfacing — a distinction officials say was always built into the project, even if it wasn't built into the first letter that went out to residents.
