Loamford Crews to Return in October for Final Paving of Two Water-Main Streets

LOAMFORD — Crews will return next month to finish paving two streets where water-main replacement work was completed this past summer, city officials said, addressing questions from residents who assumed the road repairs were done.

The works department patched the two streets in July 2026 after replacing underground water mains, restoring the excavated strips so traffic could resume. That patch was never intended as the permanent road surface, officials said. Final resurfacing is planned for October.

"A surface that lets the street reopen is not necessarily the surface we intend to leave there," said works engineer Arven Cole.

The two-stage approach was built into the project plan from the start, according to the city, though the first notice sent to residents used the general term "repairs" without spelling out that a temporary patch would precede permanent resurfacing. That wording gap appears to be the source of confusion among residents who believed the work was complete.

Before the final surface goes down, the city's engineering brief requires inspectors to check the filled trenches. Officials cited two reasons for the delay between patching and permanent paving: settlement that can still occur in the new fill, and the need to avoid conflicts with other scheduled utility work in the area. A separate communications cable project was coordinated with the trench work specifically so that strip would not need to be reopened again right after resurfacing.

City officials emphasized those reasons are specific to this project and do not reflect a blanket policy requiring every road repair to sit unfinished for months. "Those are the reasons recorded for this project, not a rule that every road repair must remain temporary for the same period," the works department said.

Cost records show the temporary work cost $8,000, while final resurfacing is estimated at $38,000. The final phase has not yet been completed or paid for, and the record contains no finding of contractor misconduct or evidence the city paid twice for the same completed job.

In the meantime, some residents have reported uneven joins where the patched strips meet the older road surface, particularly at spots where bicycles cross the edges. The department said it will address defects that need attention during the interim rather than ask residents to simply live with them until October. Under the project agreement, the contractor remains responsible for maintaining its temporary work in the meantime. Residents can report problems to the city's inspection team, which handles road-surface issues rather than water-meter staff.

Resident representative Mira Pell said the process needs clearer communication going forward. "If it is temporary, tell us what that means and who comes back when it starts breaking up," she said.

The city council has since asked that future project notices distinguish clearly between temporary reinstatement and completed resurfacing, to avoid the kind of confusion seen with this project.

When work resumes, the city said it will mark off the final work areas and circulate access arrangements beforehand. Pedestrian access to homes will be maintained throughout, though vehicle access may be restricted in parts of the work zone at times. The planned resurfacing covers only the affected carriageway sections, not every side street in the neighborhood.

The city cautioned that exact scheduling still depends on completion of the trench inspections and finding a suitable work window that meets the contractor's material specifications; no precise weather forecast or firm completion date has been set.

Separately, residents may notice that street signs in the area still carry an older neighborhood spelling that doesn't match the current utility work map — a discrepancy officials say is unrelated to the paving schedule.
