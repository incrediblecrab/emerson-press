Headline: Calder Fen Charts a Four-Step Path for Its Surplus Office Furniture

CALDER FEN — After consolidating several city offices, the Calder Fen property office has counted 280 chairs, 110 desks and 70 cabinets — 460 pieces of furniture — no longer needed in their original locations, and it is now moving that inventory through a set process for reuse, sale, donation or recycling.

Officials caution that the 460 items are not 460 sale lots, and not all are assessed as ready for immediate reuse. Some pieces need condition checks before anyone decides what happens to them next.

The furniture became surplus for administrative reasons, not necessarily because it broke. In several cases, two services that used to occupy separate offices now share one building, leaving extra desks and cabinets with no assigned room. Property manager Elra Voss said, "A chair can still have years in it without having a place in the room it came from."

Before anything is labeled surplus, the property office confirms the item is no longer required for an approved city use. Some cabinets still carry labels from departments that have since changed names; staff will remove those labels before the items are transferred elsewhere.

Under the city's disposal policy, usable furniture is offered first to other city departments and eligible public institutions. During this internal reassignment period, receiving institutions can check an item's measurements and arrange how they'll collect it, rather than choosing based only on a photograph. Community-center coordinator Pella Dane said, "The hard part is often the van and the doorway, not whether we could use another table."

A desk or other item accepted by another department is logged as a transfer, not as a sale, and does not count toward sale revenue.

Furniture not reassigned during that internal stage moves to whichever of three routes fits its condition and demand: a public sale, an approved donation channel or recycling. For the public sale, the office may bundle several pieces into a single lot and describe their condition in the listing rather than pricing and selling every item separately. The city has not said every listed item will be sold individually or at the same price, and a sale date will be set only after the internal reassignment period ends — it should not be assumed from the date the inventory was published.

Whichever route an item takes, recipients and buyers are responsible for arranging their own collection within a stated time window; the city is not offering free delivery citywide.

Items with unresolved condition questions are set aside for further assessment rather than listed as donations ready for use. And while furniture has been cleared out of former offices, employees cannot simply take a piece from storage on their own.

Separately, computers and other data-bearing equipment are handled through a different disposal process and are not part of this furniture count.

The city plans to keep a record of where each inventoried item ends up — transfer, sale proceeds or recycling — though officials note that gross sale proceeds won't equal net savings once handling, storage and disposal costs are factored in separately. The office has not released projections of final proceeds or independent estimates of environmental impact, and it describes the current inventory as a one-time result of this round of consolidation rather than a full accounting of furniture the city owns.
