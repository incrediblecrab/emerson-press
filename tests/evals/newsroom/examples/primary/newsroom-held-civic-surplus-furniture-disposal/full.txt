**HEADLINE:** What happens to Calder Fen's old office furniture after a consolidation

**EXPLAINER**

When Calder Fen consolidated several city offices, the property office ended up with more chairs, desks and cabinets than the merged space needs. According to a citywide inventory compiled as of Sept. 18, the office has counted 280 chairs, 110 desks and 70 cabinets — 460 pieces of furniture in total. That figure describes individual items, not 460 sale lots, and the city has not said every piece will be sold on its own or at the same price.

Before anything is labeled surplus, the property office checks whether an item is still needed for an approved city use, according to the office. Surplus does not mean broken. Some furniture became unnecessary simply because two services now share one building.

"A chair can still have years in it without having a place in the room it came from," said property manager Elra Voss.

**How the process works**

The city's disposal policy moves furniture through stages, the property office said. First, usable items are offered internally to other city departments and to eligible public institutions. That reassignment period is meant to give receiving offices time to check dimensions and arrange collection, rather than pick furniture from a photograph alone, according to the office.

A desk picked up by another department is logged as a transfer, not as a sale, the office said. Only after the internal stage ends do unclaimed items move to a public sale, an approved donation route or recycling, depending on condition and demand.

For the public sale, the office may group several pieces into a single lot and describe their condition in the listing rather than photographing and pricing each item separately, the office said. No sale date has been announced, and the office said one will be set only after the internal reassignment period concludes — it should not be assumed to follow soon after the inventory's publication.

Whoever receives or buys an item is responsible for picking it up within the time allotted for that transfer or sale, the office said; the city does not offer citywide free delivery.

**What the numbers do and don't show**

The city said it will keep a record of where each inventory item ends up — transfer, sale or recycling — and how much any sale brought in. But gross sale proceeds will not equal net savings, because the city separately tracks handling, storage and disposal costs, according to the property office. The reporting notes reviewed for this account contain no forecast of what the sales will ultimately bring in and no independently measured estimate of any environmental benefit from reuse or recycling.

Some items are in limbo for now. Furniture with unresolved condition questions is being held for further assessment rather than listed as donation-ready, the office said. And several cabinets still carry printed labels from departments that have since been renamed or merged; staff plan to remove those labels before the cabinets are transferred.

A few categories fall outside this count entirely. Computers and other data-bearing equipment go through a separate disposal process and are not part of the furniture inventory. Employees cannot simply reclaim an item from storage because it was removed from their former office, the property office said.

The city cautioned that this inventory reflects a one-time consequence of the recent office consolidation — not a full accounting of furniture the city owns overall.

Community-center coordinator Pella Dane, whose facility has received city surplus items in the past, said logistics can matter as much as need. "The hard part is often the van and the doorway, not whether we could use another table," Dane said.
