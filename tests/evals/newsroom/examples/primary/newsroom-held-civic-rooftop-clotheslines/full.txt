Bell Orchard reopens rooftop drying space, ending years of stair-carrying

The fictional Bell Orchard housing cooperative has reopened a shared rooftop drying area, following the replacement of its access door and repairs to the drying lines, cooperative officials said.

The space was part of the building's original plans but had been closed while the cooperative reviewed how residents would reach it, according to the cooperative. Residents now travel by the building's lift and a short level corridor to the roof, rather than carrying wet laundry up a final flight of stairs.

Resident coordinator Mara Dene keeps a paper sign-up sheet beside the door so households can claim a time period without using a phone app.

"The small negotiation is usually whose sheets will be dry before the next person arrives," Dene said.

Bookings apply to designated sections of line, not the entire roof, and the rules build in time for residents to collect their dry items before the next booking begins — rather than treating the end of a slot as permission for one household to handle another's clothes.

On a recent morning, residents carried folded sheets up from their apartments, checked the wind, and took pegs from a communal basket to hang alongside their own. The basket holds donated spares; residents otherwise supply their own laundry and pegs.

Resident Elra Fenn said the rooftop space matters most for bedding, which she said crowds her small apartment when dried indoors.

"Up here the bed linen is somebody's washing. Downstairs it can become the whole room," Fenn said.

The area has set opening hours and closes under conditions spelled out in the cooperative's building-use plan. Residents are not permitted to move drying equipment into other parts of the roof outside the marked space, and the project does not turn the roof into a public terrace or an events venue.

A separate group of residents asked the cooperative to keep quiet hours in place, saying apartments directly below the roof can hear footsteps and the sound of baskets being moved overhead.

The cooperative spent $14,800 on the access door, replacement lines and related repairs. It has not calculated any projected savings on household energy costs from residents drying laundry outdoors instead of using machines, and officials said no such estimate was part of the project.

The building caretaker inspects the shared equipment and takes fault reports through the cooperative's regular maintenance process, as with other common facilities in the building.

The rooftop's outlook takes in the backs of nearby shop signs, not the landscaped street frontage visible from ground level.

Not every resident is required to use the space, and the cooperative said households that prefer their own drying equipment can continue using it. The cooperative plans to revisit how long each booking should last after residents have used the roof through a full season, gathering feedback on how the current system has worked in practice.

The arrangement applies to Bell Orchard's own roof and does not extend to residents of other buildings.
