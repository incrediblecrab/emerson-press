Bellwether County Adds Jobs and a Higher Jobless Rate — Because They're Measuring Different Things

Bellwether County's economy added workplace positions and saw its resident unemployment rate climb over the past year, a pairing that looks contradictory but reflects two separate measurements, according to the county's August 2026 economic bulletin.

The bulletin's payroll count found 25,600 jobs at county workplaces in July, up from 25,000 in July 2025. That figure counts positions located in the county, including ones held by people who commute in from elsewhere.

A separate resident labor-force estimate, drawn from where people live rather than where they work, put July 2026 employment at 19,740 residents and unemployment at 1,260, for a labor force of 21,000 and an unemployment rate of 6%. A year earlier, the resident count was 19,000 employed and 1,000 unemployed, a labor force of 20,000 and a 5% rate. The rate rose by one percentage point, not by one percent of its previous value.

Resident employment rose by 740 over the year. But 260 more people were counted as unemployed, and the overall labor force — employed residents plus those seeking work — grew by 1,000. When more people enter the labor force than find jobs right away, the unemployment rate can rise even as employment increases.

The two counts cannot be reconciled by subtraction. The payroll figure includes commuters and, unlike the resident count, differs in how it treats self-employment and people holding more than one job, the bulletin said. It does not identify how many of the additional 1,000 labor-force entrants were people moving into the county, recent graduates or residents returning to job searches.

County analyst Omi Drel said the payroll estimate could still change as more employer returns come in. "One count starts at the workplace," Drel said. "The other starts with the person who lives here."

The bulletin counts a resident as unemployed only if the person has no job, is available to work and is actively searching, under its stated survey definition. Students, retirees and caregivers who are not looking for work fall outside that definition; they are not automatically counted as unemployed.

Nessa Holt, a parcel sorter, returned to job searching in June after a year spent caring for a relative — the kind of reentry the bulletin's definitions are built to capture. Holt said she has applied for daytime work but has found that several local vacancies require overnight availability she cannot take on. "A vacancy can be close to home and still be a shift I cannot take," Holt said.

A separate county employer survey lists transport scheduling and machine maintenance among roles that are hard to fill, but the survey does not match specific openings to specific job seekers, so it does not establish that vacancies like the ones Holt described are typical of the county's job market.

The bulletin's year-over-year comparisons are not seasonally adjusted, meaning they compare the same month across two years rather than accounting for typical seasonal swings. Bellwether has published separate workplace and resident tables since 2012.

The figures do not show that every industry in the county expanded, or that workers already employed saw higher pay. They show two counts, measuring different populations, that moved in the same direction for different reasons.
