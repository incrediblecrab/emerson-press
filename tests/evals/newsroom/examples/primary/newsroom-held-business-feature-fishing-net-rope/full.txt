# From retired nets to rope, Delo Loop tracks what survives processing

In a former net-repair shed, Delo Loop Materials workers separated rope ties and attached debris from retired fishing nets delivered from a harbor store, according to a July sorting note. The space has separate marked areas for wet arrivals and processed feedstock. What comes into one area does not all reach the other.

The distinction matters for the company's rope pilot. A batch that arrived weighing 1,000 kilograms yielded 650 kilograms of recovered polymer for a rope blend, according to its batch record. Water, debris and processing losses accounted for the difference.

As of Sept. 8, the pilot connects gear collected ashore with rope sold for specified packaging and bundling uses. Between collection and a sale are sorting and processing steps, the addition of new polymer and limits on what the resulting product can claim. The record also leaves open questions about costs, future supply and sustained demand.

The nets were retired gear collected on land, not documented removals of abandoned nets from the sea. Harbor cooperative representative Omi Relsen said collecting separated gear ashore requires storage space and time from crews.

"A retired net is not ready for a new factory just because it has stopped going out on a boat," Relsen said.

The batch record shows how much material remained at each stage. Of the initial 1,000 kilograms, 250 kilograms were assigned to water and removed debris. That left 750 kilograms of usable polymer feedstock. Processing losses then reduced the amount to 650 kilograms of recovered polymer.

The manufacturer added 350 kilograms of new polymer, producing a 1,000-kilogram blend with 65% recovered content by weight. Although the incoming batch and the final blend weighed the same, they did not contain the same material. The rope blend was not 100% old fishing net.

Technical manager Rella Horne said the blend is intended to keep material properties consistent across batches with variable recovered feedstock.

"The claim on the label has to follow the material that reaches the product, not everything that arrived at the gate," Horne said.

Delo Loop keeps batch records for its recovered-content calculation rather than claiming every future batch will produce an identical yield. The pilot also does not establish that every type of fishing gear can go through the same recovery process.

The product's intended uses are limited. The current rope is sold for specified packaging and bundling uses, not for climbing, lifting people or untested marine safety applications. A packaging customer is trialing it for bundled goods but has not signed a full-year purchase order.

Supply remains unsettled, too. Three local harbor groups have supplied trial batches, but no guaranteed annual collection volume has been contracted.

Some donated gear arrives without a purchase charge. Transport, sorting, drying and processing still cost money, and Delo Loop has not released a full cost comparison with rope made entirely from new polymer. The supplied record contains no independent life-cycle comparison proving a particular emissions reduction.

For now, the batch record supports a narrower claim: 650 kilograms of recovered polymer reached the rope blend from 1,000 kilograms of incoming gear. The separation that began in the former repair shed determines what can be counted on the label.
