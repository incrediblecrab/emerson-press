# From retired nets to new rope, Delo Loop tracks what makes the cut

Retired fishing nets arriving at Delo Loop Materials do not go straight into new rope. A July sorting note records workers removing rope ties and attached debris from a batch delivered from a harbor store — an early step in a pilot whose recycled-content claim depends on what survives processing, not simply what gets collected.

The sorting space, a former net-repair shed, has separate marked areas for wet arrivals and processed feedstock. That separation reflects a distinction at the center of the project: Retired gear is a source of material, but it is not yet a manufacturing ingredient.

The nets were collected ashore after being retired from use. They were not documented removals of abandoned nets from the sea. The pilot also does not establish that every type of fishing gear can go through the same recovery process.

For one recorded batch, the path from collection to product began with 1,000 kilograms of incoming material before drying and sorting. The batch record assigns 250 kilograms to water and removed debris, leaving 750 kilograms of usable polymer feedstock.

Processing losses then reduced that amount to 650 kilograms of recovered polymer available for the rope blend. The manufacturer added 350 kilograms of new polymer, producing a 1,000-kilogram blend with 65% recovered content by weight.

The matching incoming and final weights do not mean all the collected material became rope. The final blend contains both recovered and new polymer; it is not 100% old fishing net.

“The claim on the label has to follow the material that reaches the product, not everything that arrived at the gate,” technical manager Rella Horne said.

Horne says the blend is intended to keep material properties consistent across batches with variable recovered feedstock. Delo Loop keeps batch records to calculate recovered content rather than claiming that every future batch will deliver the same yield.

The rope is sold for specified packaging and bundling uses. It is not sold for climbing, lifting people or untested marine safety applications. A packaging customer is trialing it for bundled goods but, as of Sept. 8, has not signed a full-year purchase order.

The work before manufacturing carries its own demands. Harbor cooperative representative Omi Relsen says collecting separated gear ashore takes storage space and time from crews.

“A retired net is not ready for a new factory just because it has stopped going out on a boat,” Relsen said.

Some donated gear arrives without a purchase charge. Transport, sorting, drying and processing remain business costs, however, and Delo Loop has not released a full cost comparison with rope made entirely from new polymer.

The environmental evidence also has limits. The supplied record contains no independent life-cycle comparison proving a particular emissions reduction. The batch figures document how much recovered polymer entered the blend, not a measured climate benefit.

Three local harbor groups have supplied trial batches, but no guaranteed annual collection volume has been contracted. With neither a guaranteed yearly supply nor a full-year order from the trial customer, the pilot leaves open questions about sustained collection and sales.

For now, its records show the steps between a harbor’s retired gear and a product containing recovered material: separating out water and debris, accounting for processing losses, adding new polymer and limiting the finished rope to specified uses. The collection weight starts that accounting. It does not finish it.
