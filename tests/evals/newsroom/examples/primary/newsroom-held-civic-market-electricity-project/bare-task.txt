Slateford Council Approves $132,000 Plan for Permanent Market Power Outlets

SLATEFORD — The Slateford Council has approved a $132,000 budget to install permanent electrical supply points at the town's outdoor public market, replacing a patchwork of generators and borrowed connections with a booked outlet system.

The plan calls for 14 shared pedestals, each with two separately booked outlets, for a total of 28 bookable outlets. The market currently has 52 registered stallholders, though not all trade on every market day or require electricity.

Under current arrangements, stallholders who need power rely on a mix of permitted generators and informal agreements with nearby premises. Those generator permissions remain in effect for now; council officials said the installation vote does not by itself revoke them, and existing arrangements will continue until replacement provisions are announced.

Construction is planned for the period between the end of the winter market season and the market's scheduled March 2027 reopening, though the timeline depends on final contractor scheduling. Licensed contractors will install and inspect the equipment before any outlet becomes available for use.

The approved budget covers installation and inspection costs only. Electricity charges and ongoing maintenance will remain separate operating expenses, and the council has not announced what it will charge stallholders to book an outlet or how it will divide electricity costs among users. Council members said they will consult stallholders on operating charges before adopting the market's next fee schedule.

Market manager Elva Rane said the change is meant to formalize what has long been an ad hoc system. "Power will become something we book and maintain together, rather than something each stall improvises separately," Rane said.

Not all traders were satisfied that cost questions had been resolved. Food trader Neri Vale said the value of the upgrade will depend on what it costs to use. "A reliable socket would help, but the running charge matters as much as the installation announcement," Vale said. Separately, traders who sell goods without powered equipment asked that any future fee system make clear which costs are shared across the whole market rather than charged only to those who use electricity.

The plan restricts how much equipment can draw power from each pedestal, and officials cautioned that approval to plug in one appliance does not extend to every device a trader owns. Before powered spaces are assigned, the market office will hold registration sessions requiring stallholders to list the equipment they intend to use. Powered positions will then be allocated through a published booking process, rather than granted to traders who simply arrive first and claim a socket.

The layout keeps pedestrian access lanes clear, placing supply points along existing service edges instead of the middle aisles where the market's stone paving — long used informally to mark stall positions — has traditionally guided placement. Some stall locations are expected to shift to align with the new supply points, though the council has not yet issued a final assignment map.

Officials said the project does not include charging stations for customers' vehicles or open public use of the outlets, and it does not increase the market's total number of stalls. The council has not released measurements of current generator noise or emissions, nor has it predicted a specific reduction from the switch to grid power.
