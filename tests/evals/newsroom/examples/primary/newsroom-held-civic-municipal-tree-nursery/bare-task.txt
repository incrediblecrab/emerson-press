**Headline:** Before the Planting Day: Inside Bellmarsh's Nursery for Future Street Trees

BELLMARSH — Long before a street tree takes root outside a home or business, it spends years growing under careful watch at the city's municipal nursery, where staff track roughly 800 young trees at various stages of development.

But that number can be misleading, said nursery supervisor Hessa Dale. Of those 800, only about 200 currently appear on the nursery's planning list as candidates for next season's planting — not trees ready to be moved into the ground immediately.

"People see the planting day," Dale said, walking a row of young trees with a clipboard in hand. "We are working on the years that have to happen before that day is possible."

The clipboard links each batch to its origin, potting history and intended size range — details that matter later, when city planners try to match a tree to a specific site. On a recent visit, staff were repositioning one batch to new spacing, giving branches and root containers room to grow before the trees are considered for placement.

A tree's presence at the nursery, Dale said, does not guarantee it is suitable for every proposed street location. The city's planting team weighs available space, overhead and underground utility lines, and expected long-term maintenance before assigning nursery stock to a site. Some trees remain at the nursery for another season if their own condition, or the destination site's preparation, isn't ready.

The nursery isn't the only source for the city's public trees, either. Bellmarsh also purchases some stock from outside growers, meaning the nursery supplements rather than supplies every tree planted along city streets.

For neighborhood volunteer Rena Sore, who helps record residents' requests for street trees, the nursery has been a lesson in patience. Sore visits periodically to understand what options are actually available before a request moves forward.

"On the street we ask for a tree this winter," Sore said. "Here you can see how far back somebody had to begin."

To keep track of that process, the nursery's records separate stock currently held, stock that has been allocated to a project, and trees that have actually been planted — three distinct categories that don't always move in lockstep. If a street project is delayed, Dale said, an allocation can shift rather than force a tree into the ground at an unsuitable time or an unfinished site. Staff also remove from consideration any trees that no longer remain suitable for planting, rather than counting every seedling or purchased sapling as a guaranteed future street tree.

The nursery sits beside a former parks department depot, sharing some storage and delivery access with the city's maintenance crews. Day-to-day work there includes watering, spacing, condition checks and updating records — not simply planting seeds and waiting.

Dale keeps a small collection of older plant labels on hand, using them to show how batch-naming conventions have changed over the years without severing the link to earlier planting records.

The nursery does not operate as a site for unrestricted public collection of trees; residents seeking a street tree still go through the city's parks request process. A planned public visit to the nursery, Dale said, will focus on showing the growing stages and the record-keeping system, rather than offering each visitor a tree to take home.

The city currently funds the nursery's operating budget, but any future street-planting project requires its own separate site and maintenance funding before the trees now growing in Bellmarsh's rows can reach the curb.
