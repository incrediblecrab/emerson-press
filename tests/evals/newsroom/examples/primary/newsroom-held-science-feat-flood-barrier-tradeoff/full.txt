# Caldwell's flood barrier plan faces an ecological test

After seven years working on Caldwell City's proposed tidal barrier, chief engineer Sara Oduya can describe the protection it would provide. She is less certain about what it would do to the life of the estuary.

"We can tell you what the gate does to water levels. We can tell you a lot less about what it does to species that evolved in this estuary over ten thousand years," Oduya said. "That uncertainty is real and we are honest about it."

The $3.1 billion Caldwell Estuary Tidal Gate is under final engineering review, with a decision on construction authorization expected in 2026. Oduya said it would protect 55,000 residents from surge events up to 3.5 meters. But as the project approaches that decision, questions remain about its ecological effects and who would require changes to gate operations if monitoring shows harm.

The estuary supports two species of conservation concern: the Caldwell silverside fish and a migratory shorebird that feeds in the intertidal zone. The project's environmental impact statement concluded that effects on both would be adverse but unlikely to jeopardize their survival. Three outside ecologists who reviewed the statement said its species population models lacked sufficient baseline data.

Marine ecologist Patrick Lund, who has spent four years studying the estuary, said the barrier would reduce tidal flushing by approximately 30% during closures. Those closures are projected to occur six to 10 times a year under future climate scenarios.

"Thirty percent less flushing means higher salinity at the upper estuary during dry periods and lower oxygen during wet ones," Lund said. "Some species can adapt. Some cannot. We don't have enough data to say which is which for every important species."

For Mayor Anna Rios, waiting carries its own cost.

"We had $890 million in flood damage from the 2018 storm," Rios said. "At some point, you build the thing, or you accept the losses."

Rios said she ran for mayor on flood protection and would stand for a decision. Lund said he does not oppose the barrier in principle, but contends the environmental review was compressed to meet political timelines.

"We needed three more years of baseline data. We got one," he said.

A coalition of environmental groups has filed for an extended review period. As of March 13, the federal agency overseeing the review had not responded.

Oduya said the project team has designed protocols to minimize closure duration and maintain fish passage through fish locks during operations. She acknowledged that these are mitigations, not solutions.

The proposed package also includes a 50-year ecological monitoring commitment, an endowment for species research and adaptive management provisions allowing gate operations to change in response to monitoring data. Oduya said she believes it is the strongest package achievable.

Lund said he would not oppose the project if the monitoring commitment and adaptive management provisions are binding. Their value, he said, depends on whether the city will modify operations when data shows harm.

"Adaptive management is a promise. It's only as good as the institution keeping it over fifty years," he said.

Oduya expects the final engineering review to take six months and hopes the ecological and infrastructure questions can be resolved in parallel. After seven years on the project, she describes the choice as reducing one risk while introducing another.

"The engineering is largely settled. The governance — who decides what the monitoring means and what to do about it — that is not settled."
