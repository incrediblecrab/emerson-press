# Where Copper Wren's 18% service charge goes — and what diners can skip

Copper Wren's 18% service charge goes into the restaurant's revenue to help pay labor costs, rather than being promised in full to the server who handled a particular table.

The charge, introduced in July, applies to dine-in food and drink checks. Under the restaurant's policy as of Aug. 17, it is mandatory for those orders, while an additional tip is optional. Takeaway orders are exempt from the charge.

For a diner with a $50 eligible check, the distinction starts with the bill: The restaurant adds a $9 service charge, bringing the total to $59 before separately calculated taxes. Customers can choose whether to leave an additional tip, and the payment screen begins with no additional tip selected.

## Where the money goes

Copper Wren retains service-charge receipts as business revenue used for payroll, paid leave and other labor costs. It does not promise to pass the full 18% from each table directly to that table's server.

When the charge began, the restaurant raised servers' base pay from $16 to $22 an hour. Kitchen workers also receive pay under the revised labor budget, though their individual rates were not provided.

Owner Leda Selt says the policy is intended to make the base wage less dependent on the size of individual table tips.

"We can promise an hourly rate more clearly than we can promise what a particular table will leave," Selt said.

That promise concerns the hourly rate, not a fixed number of shifts or a particular weekly take-home amount. The service charge and the wage increase therefore answer different questions: One establishes what eligible diners must pay; the other establishes servers' base pay per hour.

Server Toma Dain says the higher base rate makes quiet shifts easier to plan around, while busy nights previously could produce larger tips.

"A steadier floor matters, but people should not assume every worker's total went up by the same amount," Dain said.

The reporting record lacks matched shift-by-shift earnings data establishing every employee's total change. The higher base rate alone does not establish how each worker's overall earnings changed.

## What diners must pay

Copper Wren's notice asks diners not to treat the service charge as an automatic obligation to add another 18%. An additional tip is a separate choice, not a second required charge under the policy.

The restaurant discloses the service charge on menus and its booking page, rather than introducing it only on the final receipt. Menu base prices were unchanged when the charge began in July, so mandatory pretax totals rose for eligible dine-in orders.

The restaurant has not published a verified before-and-after profit comparison. Its stated use of the receipts for labor costs does not, by itself, establish whether profits increased or decreased.

For customers, the practical distinction is between the required 18% charge retained by the business and any extra tip they elect to leave. That describes Copper Wren's allocation policy, not a universal legal rule for service charges or tips at other restaurants.
