# At Threadword, machine-written drafts shift the work to checking

A July job note at Threadword Services captures a translation task that begins with words already on the page: Translator Aro Naylen was comparing a client's draft visitor guide against an approved list of place names and opening hours.

The assignment illustrates a growing part of the agency's work. Clients are bringing machine-generated drafts for review, leaving translators to determine what can stand, what needs revision and what cannot be verified without more information.

In the first half of 2026, 32 clients submitted machine-generated drafts to Threadword for review, compared with nine in the preceding six months. Those figures count clients requesting the service, not every document handled or the quality of every machine draft.

For the agency, which translates exhibition guides, booking messages and visitor information for museums and tour operators, the change is reshaping both the work it sells and how it prices that work.

“The document arrives already written. That is not the same as arriving already finished,” founder Anya Ravelle said.

Threadword began in 2018 with two translators working above a stationery shop. It now has six employees and contracts with additional translators for language pairs outside the staff's coverage. Under the changing workflow, their assignments can involve checking names and dates, identifying omitted qualifications and ensuring that a draft agrees with previously approved material.

That work does not fit neatly into the agency's ordinary pricing model. Threadword charges for conventional translation by the word but normally quotes draft review according to the estimated time needed for checking. The length of an existing draft does not establish how much scrutiny it requires or how quickly a translator can approve it.

Ravelle says some routine messages need limited revision, while unfamiliar exhibition terminology can require specialist review. The distinction makes the content of an assignment important to its scope, even when a client has already supplied a complete draft.

Sometimes, the obstacle is not deciding how to translate the source but establishing what the source should say. Naylen says the agency sometimes asks clients to clarify their original material before determining whether a draft has translated it correctly.

“I cannot check a confident sentence against an opening time the client has not settled,” Naylen said.

Threadword's client agreement requires approved source documents. It does not authorize publication of every first draft without review.

For museum client Keri Varden, consistency across visitor materials matters more than getting the first draft earliest. Varden says signs, tickets and emails need to carry a single consistent version.

Paid review accounted for 28% of Threadword's revenue in the first half of 2026, up from 11% in the preceding period, according to the agency. That marks a larger share of its business, but does not establish a productivity gain: Threadword has not supplied total revenue, matched job complexity or sufficient staff-hour data to calculate one.

Nor do the available figures establish a universal comparison of machine and human translation error rates. The reporting materials contain no machine-generated passages or outside system tests.

No employee has been dismissed under the current workflow change, according to Ravelle, though future staffing has not been guaranteed.

As of Sept. 8, Threadword is preparing a new client briefing explaining the scope of review, but has not announced a uniform fee for all draft types. For now, a document's arrival with its sentences already written leaves a separate question for the agency and its clients: how much work remains before those sentences are ready for visitors.
