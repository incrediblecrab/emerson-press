# At Threadword, machine drafts change the work — and how it is priced

A July job note at Threadword Services records translator Aro Naylen comparing a client's draft visitor guide with an approved list of place names and opening hours. The text had arrived written. Naylen's assignment was to check it against the material the client had approved.

That distinction is becoming a larger part of the agency's business. Threadword translates exhibition guides, booking messages and visitor information for museums and tour operators. Increasingly, clients bring machine-generated drafts for review instead of asking the agency to translate from the start.

In the first half of 2026, 32 clients submitted machine-generated drafts for review, compared with nine in the preceding half-year, according to the agency. The change puts more of Threadword's work into assessing what an existing draft needs, deciding who should check it and estimating the time before it can be approved.

"The document arrives already written. That is not the same as arriving already finished," founder Anya Ravelle said.

Threadword prices ordinary translation by the word but normally quotes draft review by estimated checking time. The length of a draft does not establish how much checking it requires or how quickly a translator can approve it.

Review can include confirming names and dates, checking for omitted qualifications and making sure the wording is consistent with earlier approved material. Ravelle says some routine messages need limited revision, while unfamiliar exhibition terminology can require specialist review. Those differences matter when the agency estimates the checking time for a quote.

Threadword has six employees and contracts with additional translators for language pairs its staff does not cover. It began in 2018 with two translators working above a stationery shop. Under the changing workflow, the checking can also require another conversation with the client before a translator can make a decision.

Naylen says the agency sometimes asks clients to clarify the original source before deciding whether a draft has translated it correctly.

"I cannot check a confident sentence against an opening time the client has not settled," Naylen said.

Threadword's client agreement requires approved source documents and does not authorize publishing every first draft without review. For museum client Keri Varden, having one consistent version across signs, tickets and emails matters more than receiving the first draft earliest.

The agency reports that paid review accounted for 28% of first-half revenue, up from 11% in the preceding period. That establishes a larger share for review, not a measured productivity gain. Threadword has not supplied total revenue, comparable job-complexity figures or enough staff-hour data to calculate such a gain.

The client counts also have limits: They track clients requesting review, not every document handled or the quality of every machine draft. They do not establish how reliably the drafts perform.

No employee has been dismissed under the current workflow change, according to Ravelle, though future staffing has not been guaranteed. As of Sept. 8, Threadword is preparing a new client briefing on the scope of review. It has not announced a uniform fee for all draft types.

The July job note records a specific part of that work: Naylen comparing a visitor guide with the approved place names and opening hours.
