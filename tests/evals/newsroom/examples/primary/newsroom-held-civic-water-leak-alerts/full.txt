Moorhaven Water to Flag Unusual Usage in New Alert Program

MOORHAVEN — Starting with its next quarterly meter-reading cycle in October, Moorhaven Water will mail notices to customers whose water use has roughly doubled from the same period a year earlier, city officials said.

Under the program, the utility will consider a notice only when consumption for a newly completed billing period is at least twice the amount recorded for the comparable period the previous year, and only if that prior-year amount was positive. Properties without a positive comparable reading from a year earlier will not receive an alert under this first version of the rule.

The comparison is meant only as an initial screening tool, not a determination that a pipe or appliance is leaking, according to the utility. Staff will check readings for obvious transcription errors before a notice goes out, but the process does not include inspecting a property. The program relies on existing quarterly readings rather than new continuous-monitoring equipment installed in homes.

"The letter means something changed in the reading; it cannot tell us what changed inside a home," Utility Director Enna Rook said.

The notice will invite customers to discuss possible explanations with the utility and to review a supplied history of their readings. Rook's office cited an extended household stay or additional garden watering as examples of possible explanations, not as findings about any specific customer.

Getting a notice will not automatically change a customer's bill or make them eligible for an adjustment. The utility's existing adjustment process requires a separate request with supporting information, and decisions are made case by case, officials said.

The city has allocated $19,500 for printing, staff training and evaluation of the program's first two reading cycles. No estimate of water savings or number of repairs expected to result from the program has been provided.

A tenants group has asked that the notices explain how renters can raise a concern when they do not control repairs to the property they occupy. Notices will go to the account holder, and the utility has not created a system for sending copies to other occupants of a property.

"Knowing there may be a problem and being able to authorize a repair are different things," said Sol Danner, an organizer with the tenants group.

The utility said it will keep its ordinary channels for reporting an obvious fault, such as a visible leak, rather than asking customers to wait for a quarterly alert.

Officials said the first evaluation will track only how many notices were sent and how many recipients contacted the utility — not assume that every notice uncovered a leak. The screening rule itself could change after the first two cycles if staff find it misses useful cases or produces confusing notices, Rook said.

Each notice will include a diagram showing how consumption for a billing period is calculated — by subtracting the period's valid opening cumulative meter reading from its closing cumulative reading — with both billing periods labeled for comparison.

The utility's customer-service lobby still displays a mechanical meter removed from the town's first pumping station, a fixture unrelated to the new notification effort.
