# At Aster Fold, shared costumes reshape school theater budgets and backstage work

A temporary hem and a production’s color plan met in Aster Fold’s costume room in June. As coordinator Omi Savren pinned the garment, a drama teacher compared it with the planned palette — a small negotiation between what a school wanted onstage and what a shared wardrobe could provide.

That negotiation is part of the work at the costume bank, which serves 14 member schools from a rented room in an arts center. Borrowing gives schools access to garments without requiring them to acquire every piece themselves. But it also makes reservations, flexible designs and timely returns part of preparing a production.

Each school pays $180 a year. If all current memberships are paid, that generates $2,520 in annual membership receipts. Schools also owe a separate $2 cleaning contribution for each garment returned.

Those charges establish the price of access, not a measured amount of savings. Aster Fold has not calculated a uniform per-school saving: Without the bank, schools would buy, hire, make or reuse different items. There is no single alternative wardrobe bill against which to compare membership.

The service recorded 1,200 garment checkouts last year, a measure of borrowing that includes repeated loans of the same clothing. It does not mean the bank holds 1,200 distinct costumes or served 1,200 student performers. The reporting materials provide no annual return count or total cleaning receipts.

Membership allows schools to request garments, subject to availability. It does not guarantee a complete, matching cast wardrobe for every show.

The reservation ledger shows popular sizes and neutral coats being booked earlier than unusual costume pieces. Drama teacher Nera Vossel changed one production’s color choices after another school had already reserved the requested coats.

“Borrowing changes the budget, but it also asks the production to work with what is actually on the rail,” Vossel said.

That flexibility extends to fitting. Schools may make reversible alterations, such as the temporary hem Savren was pinning in June, but must get approval before cutting or permanently changing a garment. The rules allow clothing to be adapted for one production without automatically sacrificing its usefulness to the next.

Scheduling matters, too. A late return can shorten another school’s fitting period even if the garment comes back undamaged. The shared resource is not just the clothing, but the time available to prepare it.

Behind each loan is work that membership fees and cleaning contributions do not fully fund. Savren is paid for eight hours a week, while volunteers sort returns and carry out simple repairs. Arts-center support and donations supplement receipts, which alone do not cover all staffing and room costs.

Donations also require judgment. Some garments need more cleaning or alteration than their likely future use warrants, and the bank does not accept every offer.

“A free coat can still arrive with an expensive amount of work inside it,” Savren said.

The collection began with costumes from a dissolved amateur theater company in 2019. Its loan records track garment identifiers and school contacts, rather than pupils’ personal measurements.

As of Sept. 8, a proposal for additional paid coordinator hours remains unfunded, and the service cannot promise to take on more member schools. For its current members, the bank offers a shared wardrobe with practical limits: Costumes can move between productions, but the fitting, cleaning, repairs and coordination still have to be done.
