Key Aquifer Feeding Four States Is Shrinking 40% Faster Than Expected, Federal Survey Finds

A federal geological survey released Thursday found that the Meridian Plains Aquifer, a critical groundwater source beneath four states, is depleting at a rate nearly 40% faster than previously estimated, raising new concerns about the future of farming and drinking water supplies across the region.

The survey found groundwater levels are falling by 1.54 meters per year, up from the 1.1-meter annual rate estimated in the agency's last assessment in 2015. The aquifer underlies 2.8 million acres of irrigated farmland and supplies drinking water to more than 400,000 rural residents. Groundwater levels in the study area are already 18 meters below where they stood in 1990.

The findings are based on data from 1,200 monitoring wells collected over the nine years since the last assessment.

"The revised rate reflects both increased extraction and reduced natural recharge," said Dr. Sarah Kimani, the survey's lead hydrologist. "Some portions of the aquifer won't recover on any human timescale."

According to the survey, the aquifer's natural recharge rate — the pace at which precipitation replenishes it — averages just 0.03 meters per year, less than 2% of current extraction. Irrigation accounts for 91% of the water pumped from the aquifer, with the remainder drawn for municipal and residential use.

At current depletion rates, the survey estimates that economically viable irrigation in the aquifer's shallowest zones could end within 20 to 35 years. The survey also found a 22% increase between 2019 and 2024 in the number of wells reporting declining yield, compared with the previous five-year period.

The aquifer underpins a major share of the region's economy. The U.S. Department of Agriculture estimated that crops grown in its service area generated $14.2 billion in farm sales in 2023.

Bruce Holt, president of the Meridian Plains Growers Association, disputed the survey's findings, saying it overstates the extraction rate, and called for an independent review of the monitoring well methodology. Holt did not dispute the overall downward trend but said the rate calculation relied on wells concentrated in heavily pumped areas, which he said could skew the estimate.

Some producers are already adjusting. Dryland farming, which requires no irrigation, has expanded to about 8% of the study area over the past decade as some growers anticipate water scarcity.

Several states in the region have imposed extraction limits, though enforcement varies and the rules are set independently by state water authorities rather than through a coordinated federal program. Some economists have proposed tradeable water rights markets to reduce overall extraction, but no state in the region has adopted such a system. Critics also point to federal water assistance programs that subsidize irrigation infrastructure, arguing the subsidies reduce incentives for farmers to conserve water.

At least three cities in the region are studying alternative water sources, including pipeline imports and wastewater recycling, as they weigh long-term supply risks.

The aquifer was first systematically surveyed in the 1950s, when large-scale irrigation expanded into the region. The agency said depletion is uneven across the formation and did not estimate when the aquifer as a whole might be exhausted.

Kimani said the agency plans to release zone-specific depletion maps in early 2025 to give state water planners more detailed data to guide future policy decisions.
