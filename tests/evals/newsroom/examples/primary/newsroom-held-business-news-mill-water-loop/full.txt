# Sedge Row Paper approves $5.6 million water-reuse project

Sedge Row Paper's board approved a $5.6 million water-reuse installation Friday that its equipment supplier projects will cut fresh-water use by 40% at unchanged paper output.

Construction at the Dovewick mill is scheduled to begin in November and take eight months. The budget includes a $1.2 million manufacturing-efficiency grant and $4.4 million in company borrowing.

The projected water savings have not been measured at the installation, which has yet to be built. The project will also increase electricity use, and the company has not published a net energy or emissions calculation or disclosed a verified payback period.

The mill currently draws an average of 900,000 gallons of fresh water per production day, according to its metered annual report. The supplier projects that the new equipment will reduce that figure to 540,000 gallons at the same paper output, a decrease of 360,000 gallons per production day.

That comparison is central to judging the project's performance: The target is to use less fresh water to make the same amount of paper, rather than to reduce water use by making less.

The system will filter process water so it can circulate again, but the mill will still need fresh water and a controlled discharge stream. Company engineer Teri Holt said dissolved salts limit how often the same water can circulate.

"Reusing more water is not the same as never needing another gallon," Holt said.

The mill expects its fresh-water purchase bill to fall. But that expected saving is only part of the project's financial picture. New pumps will increase electricity use, and the absence of a verified payback period leaves unanswered how long savings would take to cover the investment.

For households, the Dovewick Water Authority has confirmed that the project does not itself require higher water charges. The authority has not promised that industrial water savings will lead to a cut in residential rates.

River-users association chair Inna Cole supports reduced withdrawals but wants public comparisons of water use at similar production levels.

"A quieter mill uses less water too. We need to know what it took to make the same amount of paper," Cole said.

The company's plan provides for monthly water-use reporting during the first operating year. Those reports would provide operating figures to compare with the supplier's projection, though assessing the same-output target would also require corresponding production information.

The mill's existing water authorization remains in place. The environmental office has requested updated discharge-design details. Water reuse will not eliminate the discharge stream, even as the system is intended to reduce the amount of fresh water drawn.

Employees will receive training for three new water-treatment operator assignments, all to be filled from existing staff. The mill makes packaging paper from recovered fiber and replaced its pulping equipment in 2016.

The projected reduction in withdrawals is not evidence of an environmental or health improvement already achieved. As of Aug. 28, the reporting record contains no measured change in river wildlife, water quality or neighborhood health attributable to the project.
