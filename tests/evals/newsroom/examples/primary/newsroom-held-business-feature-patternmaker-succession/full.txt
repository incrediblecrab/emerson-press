# Merrin Patterns works to preserve the knowledge behind the shapes

A wooden pump-housing pattern and a paper drawing carried different parts of the same job at Merrin Patterns. An Aug. 4 workshop note records owner Ila Merrin checking the form against the drawing, where handwritten allowances accompanied the dimensions.

Those allowances help distinguish a pattern from the metal component it will eventually help produce. Merrin Patterns makes forms that foundries use to create molds for casting replacement pump parts and other industrial components. The forms’ dimensions must account for later casting and machining requirements.

Merrin, who opened the workshop in 1988, plans to reduce her working week from five days to three in 2027. With no guaranteed successor or completed sale, the business faces a question that scanning alone cannot settle: How much of the knowledge needed to make a usable pattern can move into a file, and what must be recorded alongside it?

“The shape can go into a file. The reason for that extra lip still needs to go with it,” Merrin said.

The workshop occupies two rooms behind a former agricultural dealership. Customer names are painted on storage racks holding 120 active customer patterns. A separate shelf holds retired patterns kept for reference.

Moving that collection into digital files involves both technical decisions and customer permission. Some customer agreements require permission before a pattern or drawing can be supplied to an outside scanning service.

A pilot scanned 14 permitted patterns. Ten could be used with existing written instructions, while four needed further annotations. The missing information concerned casting allowances and machining references, rather than simply the visible outside shape.

The results give the workshop a limited basis for planning. They do not establish that every stored pattern can be reproduced accurately from a scan alone. Even the 10 usable scans depended on written instructions already available.

Some of the work of preserving that context is happening in a job notebook. Toma Venn, one of the workshop’s two assistants, links customer drawings to changes made after trial castings. He joined Merrin Patterns from a general machining job 18 months ago.

“I am not learning a secret by watching once. I am learning which question to ask before cutting,” Venn said.

For foundry customer Keri Calden, dependable reproduction can outweigh a quick turnaround. Calden said reliable replacement patterns can matter more than speed when maintaining equipment whose original maker no longer supplies parts. Calden has not committed to buying the business or financing all its conversion costs.

The financial arrangements also remain unsettled. A quoted $18,000 digitization package includes software access for the first year, with renewal quoted separately at $2,000 annually. Customers have not agreed on how much they will pay through revised orders or storage fees.

Merrin said she intends to keep taking orders during the transition rather than announce a closing sale. That leaves the workshop preparing for a shorter working week for its owner while continuing to make patterns for customers.

The pump-housing pattern checked in August already had more than its wooden shape to guide the work: a paper drawing with allowances written by hand. Those annotations are part of what the workshop must carry forward.
