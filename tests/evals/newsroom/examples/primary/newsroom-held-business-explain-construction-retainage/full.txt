Roof Payment Snub Explained: Why a City-Approved Invoice Still Left $10,000 Unpaid

LODEN — Wickmere Roofing finished the main roof at the city's municipal workshop in June under a $200,000 contract, and the city certified the invoice for the full amount. But Wickmere received only $190,000. The other $10,000 — 5% of the contract — is being held back under a retention provision written into the deal, and part of it will stay unpaid until a small, unfinished piece of the job is done.

The gap between certification and payment confuses people because it sounds like the city is disputing the work. It isn't. City contracts manager Iri Keld said the two steps measure different things.

"Certification records the work's value," Keld said. "Release of the last money has another set of checks."

Retention is a standard tool in this contract, though not necessarily in every construction contract the city or anyone else signs. Under its terms, half the withheld amount — $5,000 — is released after a completion inspection. The other half follows a six-month defect-correction period, provided the contract's conditions are met. That clock does not start when Wickmere submitted its original invoice; it starts at the completion stage the contract defines, and the city says that stage has not yet arrived.

The holdup is specific: a city inspector found an unfinished gutter connection that must be completed before the first $5,000 can be released. Wickmere does not dispute this. Owner Edda Marsh said the fitting the job requires is on order and has not arrived.

"The work can be on the building while part of the payment is still on somebody else's calendar," Marsh said.

The city says the retained money is sitting in its budget, not withheld because of any funding shortfall. Keld said the arrangement is meant to work as an incentive — Wickmere has a financial reason to finish the gutter connection and close out the six-month window cleanly.

For Wickmere, the timing still matters. The firm employs five people, with combined regular gross payroll of about $4,800 a week. The $10,000 retained exceeds two weeks of that payroll, though it is not necessarily the firm's profit on the roofing package — Marsh has not disclosed the job's full material, labor and overhead costs, so the retained amount cannot be read as money the company was counting on keeping.

Marsh said suppliers and workers on the job were paid on shorter schedules than the final contract releases, meaning the firm covered those costs before its own final payments arrived. Partly because of that gap, Wickmere has asked other public clients it works with whether smaller roofing packages could carry a lower retention percentage than 5%. No client has changed a contract in response, and the city has not proposed any change to how it structures retention on this job or others.

The city said it will inspect the gutter connection promptly once the fitting is installed, but has not set a date because delivery of the part remains uncertain.

The municipal workshop, which houses road-maintenance equipment, also had separate electrical and door work performed by other contractors under different contracts not affected by this dispute.

Nothing in the record indicates either the city or Wickmere breached the roofing contract, and no citywide change to procurement or retention rules has been announced.
