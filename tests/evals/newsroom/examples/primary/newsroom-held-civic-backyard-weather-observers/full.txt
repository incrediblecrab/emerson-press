# Around Harrowmere, weather observers keep the notes behind the numbers

Pella Dorne removes a leaf from the rim of a rain gauge and notes the obstruction beside that day's entry. The rainfall measurement will stay in the record, but so will the detail that may affect how she reads it later.

Dorne checks the gauge at a consistent morning time and writes the date before emptying it. Keeping that routine also means recording when something is out of place.

"The note about the leaf belongs beside the number. Otherwise I will trust the number more than I should when I read it next winter," Dorne said.

That attention to how a reading was taken guides the Harrowmere Weather Circle, whose members keep small weather instruments at homes, gardens and a community allotment. At monthly meetings, residents compare observations and recording circumstances rather than issue an official public forecast. Now the circle plans to put those recording practices on display in an exhibition at the community center.

Members bring paper notebooks as well as downloads from newer instruments. Both have a place in the meeting, where a figure is considered alongside information about the instrument and its surroundings.

A wall, tree or paved surface can affect what an instrument experiences, so members record where they place their equipment. One older notebook includes a sketch showing where a greenhouse stood before its owner's instrument was moved. The drawing preserves a detail about the setting that the readings alone cannot show.

Retired technician Orin Saye helps members compare their recording routines and identify maintenance questions.

"A row of figures gets more useful when somebody can tell you how each one arrived on the page," Saye said.

There are limits to those comparisons. The instruments are not all identical and have not all undergone the same calibration process. The circle does not treat differences between readings from differently placed instruments as automatic proof that neighborhoods have different long-term climates.

The records also preserve gaps. When a reading is missing, members leave the space blank rather than estimate it from a nearby observer's record. An absence can leave a gap even when the instrument itself continues operating.

Selected dated observations go to a regional community-science project, which applies its own acceptance checks. Submission does not guarantee that an observation will become part of the project's final dataset.

For members comparing particular gardening seasons, accounts of frost timing and heavy rain provide material to revisit. Those personal comparisons are not advice predicting the next season's weather.

The planned exhibition will focus on recording practices, not serve as a live emergency-warning display. Official weather alerts remain separate from the circle's voluntary observations and social-media updates.

Members pay for their own instruments. The community center provides the monthly meeting room without charge, giving residents a place to compare what their equipment recorded and what they wrote down themselves.

In Dorne's notebook, the note about the leaf stays beside the number.
