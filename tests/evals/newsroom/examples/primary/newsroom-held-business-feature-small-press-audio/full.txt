**Small Press Bets on Audio, One Backlist Title at a Time**

By [Staff Writer]

Loom Lantern Press has spent 2026 turning a handful of its 14 print titles into audiobooks, and the numbers behind the first finished one show both the appeal and the arithmetic of the format for a small publisher.

The independent house selected three backlist titles for what publisher Mara Quill calls an audio pilot. Audio permission has been agreed with the authors of two of them; negotiations for the third are unfinished. That is partly because the company's print contracts do not all include the same audio rights, according to a contract summary supplied by the publisher — a gap Quill said has to be closed title by title before any recording begins.

"A book we already printed still needs a new set of work before somebody can listen to it," Quill said.

The first title completed under the pilot is "Tide Keys," an original novel read by narrator Iri Venn and edited by a contracted sound engineer. Production — narration, editing and quality checks, but not the publisher's general overhead — cost $6,000.

Under the terms the publisher has set for individual retail downloads, Loom Lantern keeps $10 per sale after the retailer's cut and the author's share. At that rate, recovering the $6,000 production cost would require 600 paid downloads, before other costs are counted.

A retail report covering the title's first six sales weeks, July 20 through Aug. 30, lists 120 paid downloads and $1,200 in publisher receipts under those terms — one-fifth of the downloads needed to break even on production alone. Quill and the report both stop short of drawing a conclusion from that: the six-week window does not establish lifetime sales or the profitability of the audio program as a whole, and the publisher has not measured how many of those 120 buyers would otherwise have bought the print edition.

Separate library and subscription-platform deals under discussion do not necessarily pay the same $10 per-sale contribution as an individual retail download, meaning the eventual revenue mix could look different from the six-week snapshot.

The work behind the recording is granular. A production note from July records Venn stopping mid-session to check a place-name pronunciation with the press's editor. Venn keeps a working script marked with breaths, character-voice notes and pronunciation changes rather than recording each page successfully in a single pass.

"A quiet recording is not just a quiet room," Venn said. "It is all the decisions you stop and check."

A second authorized title has not yet finished recording, so its length and production cost are not yet known.

The pilot has already changed at least one plan on the ground. A launch event originally set for a bookshop was moved to a community listening room after the shop could not accommodate headphones for the size of group expected to attend.

Loom Lantern started in 2016 publishing local travel essays and has since expanded into fiction and memoir. Quill said the appeal of audio cuts both ways among readers: some who already own the print editions want an audio version for commuting, while other listeners may never buy a physical copy at all.

The company has not announced an outside investor, a guaranteed audio-sales contract or any plan to stop printing books. For now, Quill said, the audio program remains a title-by-title experiment layered onto an existing backlist rather than a settled second business line.
