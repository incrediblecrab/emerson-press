**Small Publisher Finds Its Voice, One Pronunciation Check at a Time**

By [Staff Writer]

Loom Lantern Press has spent a decade turning local travel essays, fiction and memoir into printed books. This year it is trying something new: teaching those same books to speak.

The independent publisher, which began in 2016, has picked three titles from its 14-book backlist for an audio pilot program, according to publisher Mara Quill. Audio rights have been agreed with the authors of two of those works; negotiations for the third remain unresolved, a complication rooted in the press's own paperwork. A contract summary supplied by the publisher shows its print agreements do not uniformly include audio rights, meaning each backlist title must be checked individually before a recording can begin.

"A book we already printed still needs a new set of work before somebody can listen to it," Quill said.

That work is evident in the studio, where narrator Iri Venn reads from a working script marked with breaths, character shifts and pronunciation notes rather than attempting to record clean pages in a single pass. A production note from July records Venn interrupting a session to confirm a place-name pronunciation with Loom Lantern's editor — the kind of small stoppage that recurs throughout narration.

"A quiet recording is not just a quiet room," Venn said. "It is all the decisions you stop and check."

The first title to complete that process is "Tide Keys," an original novel narrated by Venn and edited by a contracted sound engineer. Its finished production cost $6,000, covering narration, editing and quality checks but not the publisher's general overhead. Under the terms governing individual retail downloads, Loom Lantern keeps $10 per sale after the retailer's cut and the author's share — meaning that, at an unchanged contribution rate, the press would need 600 paid downloads just to recover the production cost, before any other expenses.

Six weeks of retail data, from July 20 through Aug. 30, show 120 paid downloads and $1,200 in publisher receipts under those terms — a fifth of the way to covering "Tide Keys'" production cost. Quill and the publisher caution that the early figures do not establish lifetime sales or the profitability of the broader audio program. Separate discussions over library and subscription-platform licensing could bring in revenue on different terms; those deals are not guaranteed to pay the same $10-per-sale contribution as an individual download.

The press has also not measured how many audiobook buyers might otherwise have purchased the print edition, an unresolved question for a company betting that audio will expand its audience rather than simply divide it. Quill said some print readers want an audio option for commuting, while other listeners may never buy a physical copy at all.

The second authorized title has not finished recording, so its length and cost remain unknown.

The pilot's public debut has already required its own adjustment: a planned launch event was moved from a bookshop to a community listening room after the bookshop could not accommodate headphones for the expected crowd.

Loom Lantern has not announced outside investment, a guaranteed audio-sales contract, or any plan to stop printing books. For now, the audio venture remains what it has been since July — a title-by-title experiment in whether a small press's backlist can find new readers by finding new listeners, one checked pronunciation at a time.
