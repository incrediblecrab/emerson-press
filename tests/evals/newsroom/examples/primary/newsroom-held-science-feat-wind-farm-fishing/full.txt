**Off Eastport Sound, a Wind Farm's Cables Cross a Fisherman's Livelihood**

EASTPORT SOUND — Sam Callahan has fished these waters for 28 years. In September, Atlantic Ridge Wind is scheduled to begin building a 180-turbine array here, and the project's cable route runs through the most productive lobster grounds in the region.

"They're building something that'll be there for decades," Callahan, 52, said. "We're fishing something that's been here since before any of us were born. Those things don't automatically coexist."

The cable installation phase, projected to take 14 months, will close 22,000 acres of Callahan's traditional fishing area, he said.

Atlantic Ridge hired marine liaison Divya Singh to coordinate with the fishing fleet. Singh said the company has redesigned the cable route three times in response to fishermen's input.

"The route isn't perfect," Singh said. "But it avoids the peak season grounds between April and July. What's left is a real compromise. It doesn't fully satisfy anyone."

Callahan, who took part in the consultations, said "compromise" does not adequately describe the outcome. His GPS vessel tracking data, he said, shows the avoided grounds contain his most productive traps.

The project's environmental review found no evidence that operational turbines harm lobster populations. Studies from European offshore wind sites have found lobster populations stable or increasing around turbine foundations, though those studies were conducted in different water conditions.

Callahan said his primary concern is not the operating turbines but the construction itself. High-pressure jetting used to bury the cables disturbs the seabed, he said, and disrupts lobster molting cycles.

Atlantic Ridge has offered a $4.2 million compensation fund for fishermen affected during construction. Callahan said the fund's methodology significantly underestimates his losses. Three Eastport Sound fishing families have agreed to the compensation terms; Callahan and two others have not.

Singh said the company remains willing to keep negotiating but cannot delay construction past August without losing its installation vessel window, which would bring $18 million in penalties.

For Callahan, the dispute reflects a deeper problem: a regulatory process that treats fishing grounds as an environmental consideration rather than an economic asset.

"My trap license is worth $280,000," he said. "The process didn't put a dollar figure on that."

The regional marine fisheries council has no formal role in offshore wind siting. It can submit comments but cannot veto or condition approvals.

A federal coastal fisheries attorney said the legal standard for compensating commercial fishermen displaced by offshore construction is unclear and varies by circuit court. Two pending cases in other regions may establish precedent.

Singh said Atlantic Ridge intends to pursue a long-term fishing access agreement for the operational phase, allowing trap fishing between turbine foundations. Callahan said he would not commit to that until he sees how construction plays out. European operators who allow such fishing report that rebuilding trust takes years; one Norwegian case study found 80% of displaced fishermen returned to the grounds within four years.

Callahan said he has no objection to offshore wind itself. "I'm not against clean energy," he said. "I'm against doing it in a way that destroys something else without accounting for it properly."

Singh said the company is funding a two-year seabed monitoring study tracking lobster density before, during and after construction, with results to be published. Callahan called the study welcome but too late to affect the cable route decision.

Singh and Callahan met face to face for the first time in April, after 18 months of correspondence through lawyers and industry liaisons. Callahan said the meeting changed nothing but felt like the conversation they should have had two years earlier.
