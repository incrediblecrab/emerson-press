**Old classroom now rents quiet, not certainty, to remote workers**

CELANDINE — Tara Veyne left her seat at a shared desk in August, walked past rows of laptops and into one of four enclosed booths, and closed the door before her scheduled customer-service call began.

The booth is one reason Veyne comes to Celandine Workroom, a coworking space built inside a former school classroom. Her employer permits customer calls only from an enclosed space, and repairs at her home have made it unsuitable for that work. The room gives her somewhere else to be. It does not give her everything an office would.

"I am not buying another office identity," Veyne said. "I am buying a room where I can finish this day's work."

The workroom offers 12 desks and four call booths under the same roof, at a former school building where a blackboard still hangs on one wall, now used for room notices rather than clients' work. A day pass costs $12; a monthly pass costs $80 for up to 12 visits, not unlimited attendance. Forty-six people currently hold monthly passes — more people than the room can seat at once.

That gap between passes sold and seats available shows up in the numbers. In one full working week, the room booked 210 of 600 available desk-hours, a booking rate of 35%. The average obscures busier stretches on Tuesdays and Thursdays, and it does not measure how often the four booths are full, since booth use is tracked separately from desk bookings.

Operator Omi Lerren said that gap between desks and booths matters more than the overall rate suggests.

"A free chair does not solve a booking problem when everybody needs to talk at once," Lerren said.

The room's other selling point — reliability — has limits of its own. A backup power unit can keep the essential network equipment running for about 45 minutes, not the desks and appliances throughout the room, and it does nothing for an outage on the outside broadband line it depends on. Lerren recorded three service interruptions in the first quarter of the year but has not compared that record against the reliability of the home connections its members are trying to replace.

Money is a comparable open question. A town start-up grant is covering part of the workroom's rent through its first year, and that support ends in December. Lerren has not published a full break-even calculation or lined up replacement funding for after the grant lapses.

None of that has slowed who shows up. The room's roster is not one type of worker: employees working remotely, freelancers between projects, and people handling administrative tasks for small businesses all use the same desks and the same booths, often on the same day.

What the room is selling, in the end, is a version of quiet that comes with conditions — a booth that has to be reserved, a pass that runs out after 12 visits, a backup system built for minutes rather than hours, and a rent subsidy with an end date already on the calendar. There is no approved plan to convert the rest of the former school into offices or add housing on the site. For now, it remains one classroom, repurposed, doing what it can for the people who need a door to close.
