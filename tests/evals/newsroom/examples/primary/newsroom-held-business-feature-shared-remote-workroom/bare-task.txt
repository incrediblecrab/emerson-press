Remote Workers Find a Quiet Desk in an Old Schoolroom — and the Limits of Buying One

The desks in what used to be a classroom are arranged the way desks always are, in facing rows, but the people sitting at them are not students. They are customer-service agents, freelancers and small-business bookkeepers who have paid for a few hours of quiet, and on a recent afternoon Tara Veyne needed more than a desk. She needed a door that closed.

Veyne, who works in customer service, had been sharing a table at Celandine Workroom before a scheduled call came up on her calendar. Under the rules of her job, she cannot take a customer call anywhere but an enclosed space, so she moved from the open floor into one of the room's four reserved booths, according to an August room-use note. Repairs to her building have made her home unsuitable for calls lately, she said, and the workroom has become her fallback.

"I am not buying another office identity," Veyne said. "I am buying a room where I can finish this day's work."

That distinction — a room, not an identity — is close to the business model at Celandine, which occupies a former school classroom fitted with 12 desks and the four call booths. A day pass costs $12; a monthly pass, at $80, allows up to 12 visits rather than unlimited access. Forty-six people currently hold monthly passes, more than the room could seat at once if they all showed up together.

In practice, they don't. A full week of bookings recorded 210 desk-hours out of 600 offered, a booking rate of 35%. But that average flattens a pattern operator Omi Lerren says matters more than the overall number: desks fill unevenly, with Tuesdays and Thursdays busier than the rest of the week, and the figure says nothing about how often the booths are occupied.

That gap is the workroom's central strain. With only four enclosed booths against 12 open desks, demand for private space can outstrip supply even when the floor looks half-empty.

"A free chair does not solve a booking problem when everybody needs to talk at once," Lerren said.

Other limits are harder to advertise. Lerren estimates the site's backup power unit can keep essential network equipment running for about 45 minutes during an outage — not the desks, lights or other appliances — and cautions that backup power does nothing for an outage on the external broadband provider's end, which the workroom cannot control. The operator logged three service interruptions in the first quarter but has not compiled a comparison showing the room is more reliable than any given user's home connection.

The business itself faces a countdown. A town start-up grant covering part of the first year's rent runs out in December, and no complete break-even calculation or replacement funding has been made public.

Still, the room draws a mixed clientele — employees like Veyne, independent freelancers, and people handling administrative tasks for small businesses — suggesting the appeal isn't tied to one occupation but to a shared need for a place to sit that isn't home. The old blackboard is still bolted to one wall, though these days it holds notices about parking and pass renewals rather than lessons. There is no plan, so far, to convert the rest of the former school into offices or housing; for now, it remains one repurposed classroom, oversubscribed on its busiest days and uncertain about what comes after its grant runs out.
