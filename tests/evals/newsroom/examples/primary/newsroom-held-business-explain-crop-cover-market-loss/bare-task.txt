**Headline: When the Crop Comes In But the Buyer Doesn't: A Carrot Harvest Shows the Limits of Yield Insurance**

FEN ORCHARD FARM — Bela Orr's carrots came out of the ground on schedule in July, all 36 tons of them. But shortly before harvest, a restaurant supplier that had committed to buying ten tons scaled back its purchasing, leaving Orr scrambling to place vegetables that were already in the field.

The episode illustrates a distinction that trips up many growers: crop insurance built around yield does not necessarily cover lost sales.

Fen Orchard Farm carries a policy with Field Lantern Mutual that pays out for specified weather-related reductions in yield — not for every drop in revenue. The policy sets an insured reference yield of 40 tons and a covered-yield threshold of 75%, or 30 tons. If the farm's actual harvest falls below that threshold because of a covered weather event, a payment can follow.

Fen Orchard's harvest came in at 36 tons — comfortably above the 30-ton threshold. That means no payment was triggered, regardless of what happened afterward with the buyer.

"A policy has to name the event it is covering, not just the fact that a season was expensive," said Neri Selt, a representative for the mutual.

The buyer's cancellation, Selt said, is not itself a covered yield event under this contract. The policy is not revenue insurance, and it is not insurance against a customer's business troubles. The canceled buyer, a restaurant supplier, reduced its purchasing schedule; there is no finding that the company became insolvent or ceased operating.

The underlying logic is that unsold carrots do not become unharvested carrots just because nobody buys them. The crop existed; the market for part of it did not.

Orr put it plainly: "We had the carrots. What vanished was the place they were supposed to go."

That distinction mattered financially, even if it didn't matter for the policy. Of the ten tons the canceled buyer had been expected to take, Orr's farm managed to resell three tons to other outlets, donated four tons to Cedar Spoon Kitchen Network for its meal programs, and composted the remaining three tons after failing to find any buyer. The farm has not released a full accounting of what those transactions meant for its bottom line — including resale prices, the value of the donation, disposal costs or savings from deliveries that never had to be made.

Cedar Spoon accepted the four donated tons for its own scheduled meal operations, not at the price the farm's original contract had specified. No tax valuation or reimbursement for that donation appears in the farm's records.

The other 26 tons Orr harvested went to customers who kept their original orders, according to the farm.

Growers facing similar gaps between harvest and market are beginning to organize. A regional growers' association is collecting members' accounts of canceled orders as it explores shared cold-storage arrangements and backup buyer networks. No such storage project has secured financing yet, and organizers caution that even available storage would not guarantee a later sale for crops that lose their intended buyer.

Field Lantern Mutual's officials noted that their explanation applies only to this account and this policy's specific terms, not to agricultural insurance generally; other products may define covered events differently. No regulator or court has weighed in on how the mutual handled Fen Orchard's claim.

Fen Orchard Farm, despite its name — a holdover from an earlier fruit-growing operation on the same land — has grown vegetables, including carrots, for years.
