**When the Well Runs Dry: Prospect Valley Families Face a Water Crisis Without a Fix**

By [Staff Writer]

PROSPECT VALLEY — Nkechi Okonkwo was washing dishes on a Tuesday morning in June when the water stopped.

There was no warning, no slow trickle to signal trouble. The pump that had drawn from her family's well for 31 years simply ran dry.

"I had to ask a neighbor to bring water in a tank," said Okonkwo, 58. "We have a house and a yard and we're living like it's 1890. You don't expect that in this county."

Okonkwo's well is one of a growing number failing across Prospect Valley as the Prairie Basin Aquifer's upper layers decline. In her zone of the aquifer, the water table has dropped 9 meters in eight years.

The county has 340 registered domestic wells drawing from the Prairie Basin. Water district records show 28 reported yield problems or failure in 2024 — up from four in 2019.

Mark Dahl, an engineer with the water district, said the failures are concentrated in wells drawing from the upper 40 meters of the aquifer, where the water table is dropping fastest.

"These aren't agricultural wells," Dahl said. "These are people's drinking water. When they fail, there is no backup. They were never designed to have a backup."

Drilling a new, deeper well costs between $18,000 and $35,000. Okonkwo received a $12,000 emergency assistance grant from the county, but said the remaining gap is beyond her immediate means.

The county's well assistance fund totals $1.4 million a year. It has received 47 applications in 2024 but has capacity to fund only about 20 at average grant levels. Okonkwo applied in August and, as of December, has not received a decision. The county said applications are reviewed in the order received, though some require additional documentation.

Three of Okonkwo's neighbors, all with shallow wells, have already drilled deeper and recovered water. One neighbor said her total drilling cost was $29,000 and the work took seven weeks.

Dahl said deeper aquifer zones are also declining, though more slowly, and warned that without reductions in agricultural extraction, those zones will eventually follow the shallow ones into failure. Irrigated corn farms are the dominant users of the aquifer in Prospect Valley. A 2023 state-commissioned study found that cutting irrigated corn acreage by 20% would meaningfully slow the aquifer's decline.

No such reduction program exists. The state legislature has twice declined to expand the water district's authority to regulate domestic well zones. Dahl said the district's authority is currently limited to distributing surface water and helping fund well repairs; regulating groundwater extraction would require legislative authorization.

A state water policy attorney said domestic well users in most western states are the least protected class of water right holders, with no priority over agricultural or municipal users during shortages.

Okonkwo said she attends county water board meetings and often hears the same word. "They say 'complex,'" she said. "I say I have no water. Those are not the same conversation."

Dahl called the 28 failures this year a turning point. Before 2024, he said, failures were isolated enough to handle case by case. At current rates, he said, the problem demands a systemic response.

A hydrologist under contract with the county projects that, absent policy changes, domestic well failures could climb to 60 to 80 per year by 2027.

Okonkwo said she is now considering moving from the home she and her family have occupied for three decades. "We own this house. We've been here thirty years," she said. "But a house without water is not a home."

Still, she said she isn't ready to blame local officials entirely. "They're trying," she said. "They just don't have what it takes to actually fix it."
