# Before Harrow Quay opens, five porters get its produce moving

At 4:45 a.m., as the first vegetable truck reaches Harrow Quay's loading edge, crew leader Daro Venn checks the chalked pitch numbers. The public market will not open until 9, but the work of getting deliveries to traders' stalls is beginning.

Venn leads a five-person paid porter crew that moves goods from the loading edge to traders' pitches. Using wheeled carts, the porters work through shared lanes that must be clear of delivery vehicles before customers enter. Under the current arrangement, routine vehicle unloading ends at 7:30 a.m.

The job depends on knowing both the market's layout and the equipment that carries its produce.

"You learn the sound of the carts. A loose wheel announces itself before the trader comes looking for it," Venn said.

The porters are employed through the traders' joint service agreement, with traders contributing through the market's published operating charge. It is a shared service, not an unpaid volunteer effort or five separate delivery businesses.

That distinction matters when deliveries fall out of sequence. A late truck can affect several stalls because the crew shares carts and access lanes. The market manager has asked suppliers to give notice of unusual loads, since a standard cart is not suitable for every delivery.

For fruit trader Selma Rill, having someone move the crates leaves time to prepare her pitch. She lays out empty trays while her delivered crates wait at the marked unloading point.

"If I spend the first hour chasing boxes, I have not spent it getting ready for the people who buy from me," Rill said.

The arrangement separates the work of moving deliveries from the work of making a stall ready for customers. Gradually, the market changes from stacks of closed crates to signs, price cards and open customer aisles.

Learning how to help make that change takes more than following a numbered plan. Older pitch names remain in everyday speech, even where the current layout uses numbered positions. New porters spend their first shifts alongside an experienced worker, learning pitch locations and the routine for sharing lanes.

The crew records damaged carts and broken paving in the market manager's maintenance log. A planned paving repair will require a temporary unloading arrangement, though the manager has not announced a change in public opening hours.

The service also has defined limits. Its agreement covers movement within the market, not the condition of produce before arrival or every trader's road transport. Individual porter pay rates have not been disclosed in the supplied reporting, nor has a monetary value been calculated for the time each stall saves.

This morning offers a view of the routine, not a measurement of every day's workload. Even breaks follow the delivery sequence: The crew keeps a kettle in a small room beside the cart store and takes time out according to the arriving loads.

After closing, the same lanes serve waste removal under a separately timed part of the day's operation. For the coming season, traders have retained the shared porter service rather than shifting all unloading to individual stallholders. Before the signs and price cards are ready, the carts will still have produce to move.
