# Short leases give repair shops a start in Ambercourt mall's vacant anchor space

In a corner of Ambercourt mall's vacant former department store, upholsterer Rena Selt showed a customer two replacement chair fabrics in a unit once used to display luggage. The August scene offered a different use for space left behind when the department store closed in 2024: helping customers repair something they already own.

The mall has divided part of the former anchor space into eight temporary workshop-retail units. Five were occupied at the end of July, with three still available. Some display rails and old fitting-room mirrors remain from the department store.

For Selt, the arrangement provides a place to meet customers, cut fabric and make small repairs. For the landlord, it brings rent and occupied frontage while the search for a long-term anchor tenant continues. Neither side has the certainty of a permanent shop.

The units cost $150 a week in rent, plus $35 for shared utilities — a total of $185 before materials, labor and other business expenses. Agreements renew in 28-day periods for up to six months, with 14 days' notice if the landlord ends the arrangement.

“A shop window lets people bring a question in. A four-week lease makes me careful about what I leave behind,” Selt said.

Her work illustrates both the opportunity and the limits of fitting a repair business into temporary retail space. Customer meetings and fabric work can happen in the mall, but dusty stripping work stays at a separate workspace. The mall's temporary-use rules prohibit large saws and spray finishing inside the units.

That division means the mall unit does not replace all of Selt's working space or its costs. She completed eight paid repair jobs in her first month and made three additional quick adjustments without charge. Those figures show work being done, but they do not establish a profit after rent, materials, labor and the expense of maintaining the separate workspace.

For customer Oren Bell, the storefront helped answer a practical question: whether to repair chairs rather than replace them. Seeing fabric samples in person helped him decide, he said.

But Bell also asked how he could reach Selt if the mall unit closed before he needed another repair. Selt's written work orders list her business contact details separately from the temporary unit address, giving customers a way to contact her beyond that location.

Mall manager Ceri Marel said the arrangement generates some rent and occupied frontage while negotiations with prospective anchor tenants continue. The landlord has not promised that the workshops will become permanent.

“We can offer a beginning here without pretending we have already found a permanent ending for the building,” Marel said.

The mall counted 320 visits to the workshop area during one observed week. It has no comparable count for the former department store, however, and the visits were not a tally of repair purchases. They also do not establish that the entire mall has recovered commercially.

As of Sept. 8, no permanent anchor lease, tenant profit survey or mall-wide employment recovery had been announced. The workshops offer a place for repair businesses to meet customers now, while leaving both their longer-term economics and their place in the building unresolved.
