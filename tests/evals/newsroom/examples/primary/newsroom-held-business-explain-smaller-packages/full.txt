Grocery Explainer: Same Price, Smaller Bag — What Shoppers Are Actually Paying

MEDFORD — The price tag on Marshglen Foods' Orchard Grain cereal still reads $4. But the bag behind that tag now holds 400 grams instead of 500, and the unit price has climbed from $8 to $10 per kilogram — a 25% increase that an unchanged sticker does not show.

The change, which took effect in July, is what analysts call "shrinkflation": a package gets smaller while its shelf price stays the same. Marshglen's case, examined in a shelf check of three stores in August, shows both what that kind of change reveals and what it leaves out.

**What the price alone doesn't tell you**

A steady price can mean a steady product, or it can mean less product for the same money. In Marshglen's case, the bag now contains 20% less cereal than before — a figure that does not match the 25% rise in price per kilogram, because the two percentages measure different things: one is a change in quantity, the other a change in cost per unit of that quantity. Shoppers scanning only the $4 tag would see no change at all.

The new weight is printed on the front label, and old and new bags were still both on shelves during the check, meaning a shopper could see two different unit prices under one package price. Store manager Omi Venn said staff had updated unit-price labels for the new stock but kept separate labels where both sizes remained.

**What the company says**

Lora Dain, Marshglen's purchasing director, said the company's contracted costs for grain and dried fruit rose over the past year. "We chose a smaller pack at the familiar ticket price rather than a larger jump on the shelf label," Dain said. The company says its recipe is unchanged, though no independent laboratory comparison was available to confirm that. Marshglen has not released a full cost breakdown or the profit margin on either bag size, and it has sold the cereal under the same name since 2017.

**What one product does not show**

Marshglen's cereal is one entry in a wider check. The Household Basket Observatory, a local group that tracks packaged goods, looked at 10 products chosen after shopper complaints: three had gotten smaller, five stayed the same, and two got larger. The group does not present that sample as representative of inflation generally, and the reporting record contains no finding that Marshglen or any other manufacturer broke labeling rules.

Promotional pricing complicates comparisons further. The stores sometimes run temporary multi-buy discounts on cereal, though none were active during the August check. A lower promotional price can shift the unit-price math entirely, which is why the observatory records price and quantity side by side rather than price alone.

**What shoppers notice**

Rella Pike, who buys the cereal by how many breakfasts it covers rather than by the box, said the change caught her mid-week. "Four dollars looks familiar until the bag runs out a day earlier," Pike said. Her experience reflects one household's use, not a fixed serving count — different families divide a bag differently, so her timeline won't match everyone's.

Taken together, the unchanged sticker price told shoppers nothing about the smaller bag; the printed weight and unit-price label did. Comparing the two, not the tag alone, is what showed the real cost.
