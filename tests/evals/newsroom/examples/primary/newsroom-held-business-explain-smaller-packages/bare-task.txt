**Same Price Tag, Smaller Bag: What Marshglen's Cereal Swap Shows About Reading the Shelf**

By [Staff Writer]

Shoppers scanning the cereal aisle this month may notice a familiar $4 price tag — and an unfamiliar bag. Marshglen Foods began shipping a 400-gram bag of its Orchard Grain cereal in July, replacing the previous 500-gram bag, according to a shelf check at three participating stores conducted through Aug. 31. Both sizes were selling for $4.

The episode illustrates a point that price-watchers say shoppers often miss: an unchanged sticker price does not mean an unchanged cost per bite.

At $4 apiece, the old 500-gram bag cost $8 per kilogram, while the new 400-gram bag costs $10 per kilogram — a 25% rise in unit price. That is a different number from the 20% reduction in package weight, and shoppers comparing only the percentages, rather than the underlying price-per-kilogram figures, could come away with the wrong impression of how much more they are paying.

The new weight is printed on the front label, but the bag's shape and main color remain similar to the earlier packaging, meaning a quick glance at the shelf may not reveal the change.

Marshglen purchasing director Lora Dain said the company's contracted costs for grain and dried fruit rose over the past year. "We chose a smaller pack at the familiar ticket price rather than a larger jump on the shelf label," Dain said. The company says its recipe is unchanged, though the reporting record includes no independent laboratory comparison confirming that, and Marshglen has not disclosed a full cost breakdown or the profit margin on either bag size.

Because both the old and new bags were still on shelves during the shelf check, shoppers could encounter two different unit prices under the same $4 tag. Store manager Omi Venn said staff had updated unit-price labels for the new stock but were keeping separate labels posted where both sizes remained in stock — a reminder, price-watchers say, that unit-price labels are the more reliable comparison tool during a transition like this one.

For shopper Rella Pike, who tracks the cereal by the number of breakfasts a bag supplies rather than by package count, the change registered at the pantry level. "Four dollars looks familiar until the bag runs out a day earlier," Pike said. Her experience doesn't establish a standard serving size or the same breakfast count for every household, but it captures how a stable price can mask a shorter supply.

The Household Basket Observatory, a local group that tracks package changes, examined 10 products chosen after shopper inquiries — three became smaller, five stayed the same, and two became larger. The group does not describe its list as a representative sample of overall inflation, and it notes that temporary multi-buy discounts, not in effect during this comparison, can also shift the unit-price picture, which is why it records price and quantity together rather than price alone.

Nothing in the record suggests Marshglen violated labeling rules, and there is no evidence every manufacturer is shrinking package sizes; Marshglen has sold the cereal under the same name since 2017. The lesson the Marshglen case offers, price-watchers say, is narrower but still useful: a steady shelf price answers the question "What will I pay today?" It does not answer "How much am I getting?" For that, shoppers need the unit price — and, increasingly, the fine print on the front of the bag.
