# Agency denies Granite Ridge rare earth mine permit over river contamination risk

The Environmental Resources Agency on Monday denied Pacific Minerals Corp.'s application to mine rare earth minerals at Granite Ridge in Cascadia County, citing an inadequate plan to prevent acidic runoff from contaminating a river that supports protected steelhead trout.

The June 30 decision blocks a project that would become the nation's second operating commercial rare earth extraction site if permitted. Pacific Minerals President Grant Thomas said the company would appeal and submit a revised drainage management plan within 90 days.

At issue is acid rock drainage, a chemical process in which sulfide minerals exposed during excavation react with water and air to produce sulfuric acid. Uncontrolled drainage from the Granite Ridge ore body could acidify about 24 miles of the Coldwater River.

“The ore body is economically significant, but the applicant has not demonstrated that it can prevent contamination of the river to a scientifically defensible standard,” agency Director Helen Waters said.

The agency's scientific review panel found that Pacific's proposed passive treatment system would be effective for 40 to 60 years, but that the site would require perpetual active maintenance after the mine closes. The company has not fully addressed the resulting cost and liability, the panel found.

Thomas said Pacific had offered to post a $120 million environmental performance bond and fund a long-term treatment trust. The panel found those terms insufficient.

A coalition of fishing and conservation organizations had also challenged the drainage proposal, arguing that it relied on models calibrated for ores containing less sulfide and was inappropriate for the Granite Ridge formation. Acid rock drainage is a well-documented problem in mining sulfide-bearing ores and has contaminated rivers at multiple historic mining sites in the western United States.

The denial followed a 34-month environmental review. Pacific has explored Granite Ridge since 2017 and invested about $340 million in exploration and permitting. Its stock fell 14% on the day of the decision.

The deposit contains an estimated 480,000 metric tons of rare earth oxides, including concentrations of neodymium and dysprosium. Those elements are used in permanent magnets for electric vehicle motors and wind turbine generators.

The United States imports more than 80% of its processed rare earth materials, primarily from China, and currently has only one operating commercial rare earth extraction site.

State legislators from Cascadia County said the denial would cost the region an estimated 1,200 construction jobs and 380 permanent positions.

Waters acknowledged the strategic importance of a domestic rare earth supply but said the agency's mandate was environmental protection, not supply chain policy.

A critical minerals advisory board designated rare earth deposits as national priority extraction targets in a 2022 report. That designation, however, does not override environmental permitting authority. A related federal bill to streamline rare earth permitting on federal lands has passed the Senate but has not advanced in the House.

The agency's decision leaves Pacific a path to seek approval again: It may reapply within 24 months with a revised environmental management plan.
