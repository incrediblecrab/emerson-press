# Riverdale's cooling effort brings relief — but leaves neighborhoods waiting

For 44 years, James Abubakar has lived on Elmore Street in Riverdale's South District. When he arrived, he said, the street had no shade trees. Now it has 14, and during hot weeks, he waters the young trees himself.

“Nobody asked me to,” said Abubakar, 71. “But I watched them plant those trees. I'm not going to watch them die.”

His block is part of a cooling effort that has produced measurable temperature reductions — and questions about when neighborhoods still without coverage will benefit.

Between 2019 and 2024, Riverdale planted 18,000 trees and installed cool roofing on 340 municipal buildings through its Cool Streets Initiative. Monitoring found an average temperature reduction of 1.8 degrees Celsius, or about 3.2 degrees Fahrenheit, within 200 meters of intervention sites.

“A degree and a half sounds small until you understand what it means during a heat wave,” said Maria Fontaine, the city's urban climate coordinator. “It means the difference between dangerous and survivable for people who can't access air conditioning.”

The temperature findings are clearer than the evidence of specific health benefits.

Abubakar said he counted four people from his block who went to the hospital for heat in summer 2021. As of July 8, he knew of none this summer.

“I can't prove the trees did that,” he said. “But I believe it.”

Naomi Wheeler, the urban climatologist who designed the monitoring program, said access to cooling centers and individual health conditions make it difficult to attribute health outcomes to cooling interventions.

“We measured the temperature. The temperature went down,” Wheeler said. “The physiological effect of that reduction is real physics. The specific health outcomes in specific individuals are much harder to attribute to the trees.”

The city's heat emergency plan credits the initiative with reducing emergency cooling center activations from 11 in 2019 to four in 2024. Emergency management officials said the correlation is plausible but not causally established.

Using 160 distributed temperature sensors and satellite thermal imagery, the monitoring program is one of the most detailed urban heat island intervention studies in the United States. Wheeler said its five-year span captures trees maturing into meaningful shade. The data has been shared with eight other cities.

The most important methodological finding, she said, was that combining trees and cool surfaces amplified cooling beyond either intervention alone. Surface temperatures fell 2.4 degrees Celsius, or about 4.3 degrees Fahrenheit, in the highest-intervention blocks.

But areas without interventions — predominantly lower-income, higher-density neighborhoods — showed no significant reduction.

Clara Adjei, 38, said her Northgate block is waiting for the second phase.

“I read about the temperature results in South District,” she said. “We have the same pavement, the same sun, no trees. We've been waiting.”

Expanding the program means confronting costs and upkeep. The $28 million initiative drew on federal grants, state health department funds and private foundation support, with the city contributing $6 million.

Fontaine said maintenance was the hardest part: Soil compaction and inadequate watering during drought killed 12% of planted trees in their first two years. The city invested an additional $800,000 in replacements.

Cool roofs were simpler to maintain, Fontaine said, but participation by building owners remained necessary in neighborhoods dominated by private buildings. Uptake on the target list was 67%.

The city plans to seek another federal grant for 6,000 trees and 80 cool-roof installations. Approval is uncertain, and the federal program is up for reauthorization.

Fontaine said uncovered neighborhoods will take priority: “We showed it works. The equity question is where we do it next.”

For Adjei, the request is simpler.

“I want shade,” she said. “I want my kids to play outside without getting sick. Give me the trees and I'll let you study me for free.”
