# Microplastics rise in Clearwater River as researchers weigh risks to fish

Microplastic concentrations in the Clearwater River’s main channel rose 34% from 2019 to 2024, researchers say, while particles are turning up in spawning beds where fish eggs develop. Whether that exposure harms reproduction remains an unanswered question.

The findings come from the River Sciences Institute at Northview University, which has monitored the watershed since 2019. Its five-year dataset is one of the most comprehensive for a midsized North American river, offering a baseline that many waterways lack.

Lead researcher Elena Vasquez said the steepest increases occurred in agricultural stretches downstream of irrigation return water, which carries water from fields back toward the river.

“We can trace the plastic to its sources in many cases. Agricultural mulch film and drip tape fragments dominate the farm sections. Fiber fragments dominate the urban sections. The river is a mirror of the landscape,” Vasquez said.

For fishery biologist Kenji Yamamoto, who is collaborating on the study, a central concern is where those particles settle. He has found microplastics in 92% of water samples taken from spawning gravel beds — the shallow, oxygenated zones where salmon eggs develop.

Laboratory studies show that particles smaller than 50 micrometers can enter developing fish eggs, Yamamoto said. But ongoing field studies have not produced statistically conclusive results on whether exposure reduces hatching success in the Clearwater.

“We’re finding the particles where the eggs are. We don’t yet know if that matters to the fish. That uncertainty is not reassurance. It’s a gap we need to close,” he said.

The distinction matters in a basin with a federally protected steelhead trout population. Any confirmed impact on steelhead reproduction would trigger additional regulatory review under the Endangered Species Act.

The population is recovering from a historic low, Yamamoto said. Any new stress on spawning success would be identified through population monitoring, but with a lag of two to three years.

Meanwhile, some efforts to reduce agricultural plastic waste are underway. Irrigation district manager Frank Olsen said a voluntary pilot buyback program for agricultural plastic film reached 18% of district members in its first year.

“We’re trying. But we’re asking farmers to add a cost with no direct benefit to them. That’s a hard sell every time,” Olsen said.

Vasquez said one pathway could be addressed with simple filtration: fragments washed from fields into drainage ditches and carried to the river. Filters at irrigation return points would cost an estimated $8,000 per site, or $272,000 for all 34 registered return points in the Clearwater.

No regulatory mechanism currently requires that filtration. The basin’s clean water permit system predates recognition of microplastics as a monitoring priority.

Olsen said he would support a modest cost-share program if the state provided 70% of the funding. But the state program that might cover the work does not currently list microplastics as a priority pollutant. The state environmental agency is reviewing the institute’s data as part of an ongoing water quality assessment; no regulatory action has been triggered.

For Louis Renard, 71, who farmed the Clearwater’s upper reach for 40 years before retiring, plastic was not among the water quality concerns he considered.

“I thought about fertilizer runoff. I thought about pesticides. Nobody was talking about plastic film going in the river,” Renard said.

Researchers now plan to examine nanoplastics, particles smaller than 1 micrometer. Current detection methods require weeks of sample preparation for each sample, Vasquez said.

For now, the monitoring has established something essential: a record of what is present and how it is changing.

“We know what’s there now. Before 2019, nobody did. That’s the first step. It’s not the last,” Vasquez said.
