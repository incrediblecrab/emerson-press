# At Clay Window, glaze tests and broken pots stay within reach

A beginner at Clay Window compares a dull green test tile with a glossy sample while studio technician Hara Venn looks up their different firing-batch notes. The pieces offer something to examine side by side, and the notes give the comparison a starting point.

At the community pottery studio, labeled glaze tests and selected broken pieces sit beside communal worktables. Finished pots are not the only objects kept on view. Several metal biscuit tins hold small fragments, with written indexes showing where particular tests have been stored.

The test-shard archive began after instructors found themselves describing the same past experiments without being able to show them. It gives users physical references for comparison and discussion, with records that preserve what is known about how a piece was made. It does not promise that following a label will produce an identical object.

Venn links each retained test to notes about its clay, glaze and firing batch when those details are known. Some older pieces have gaps in their documentation. Those are marked as incomplete records rather than supplied with invented firing information.

“The piece that went wrong can be the one people spend longest looking at, provided we know enough to ask a useful question,” Venn said.

That qualification shapes what the shelves can offer. A retained piece may give users a question to pursue without providing a complete account of what happened. The archive includes ordinary repeated tests as well as striking failures, so the comparisons extend beyond the pieces whose problems are most visually dramatic.

For member Dera Cole, a cracked bowl is a reference for a later version, not a finished exhibition piece.

“I remember being annoyed with it. Now I can point to the part I changed instead of pretending the next bowl arrived from nowhere,” Cole said.

Members can contribute a test or failed piece, but shelf space limits what stays. Venn selects what can be stored and documented. The studio does not retain every member’s work, and no one is required to display a personal piece they would rather take home.

Access to the reference shelves is included for current users of the workspace, who pay the studio’s ordinary membership or class fees. Studying the samples does not qualify a visitor to operate a kiln. Designated trained staff handle kiln operation under the studio’s procedures.

The labels also have a narrower purpose than a safety certification: They are learning records, not assurances that a glaze or finished pot is suitable for every food or household use.

The studio is planning a small public display about experimentation. Named personal work will require member permission, and as of Sept. 18, neither the display’s date nor its final selection has been announced.

Meanwhile, photographs are being added to the archive so a record remains available when a fragile piece can no longer be handled. A photograph can preserve an appearance, but it cannot preserve everything users compare in a sample held in their hands: its weight and texture. For now, the small fragments remain in their biscuit tins, located through written indexes and available to examine again.
