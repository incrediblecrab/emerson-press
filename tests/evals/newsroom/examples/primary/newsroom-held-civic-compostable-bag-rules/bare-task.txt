Why 'Compostable' Bags Still Get Rejected From Elmharbor's Food-Scrap Bins

ELMHARBOR — For weeks, some residents have opened their kitchen cabinets to find shelves stocked with an assortment of liners marketed as compostable, biodegradable or eco-friendly, alongside ordinary plastic garbage bags. The mixed labeling has led many to wonder which bags actually belong in the city's food-scrap bins — and why some marked "compostable" are turned away anyway.

The confusion prompted residents to seek clarification from the city, which sends its collected food scraps to a single contracted processing site. That facility works from a specified list of accepted materials, and the list is narrower than shoppers might expect: loose food scraps, plain paper liners and the uncoated paper bags distributed through the city program are permitted. Plastic-like bags carrying a general compostability claim are not currently accepted, even when their packaging says otherwise.

The reason, according to the processor's operating note, is not that such claims are false. Rather, the facility's screening and processing schedule are not built to distinguish among the many certified bag formulations on the market and handle each one correctly. City officials stressed that the acceptance rule describes what this particular contracted system can process — it is not a finding that every excluded bag's label is fraudulent.

"Our crews need a rule they can apply to the material in the bin, not a packaging claim they have time to research at the curb," said Enna Pell, the city's collection manager.

To reduce confusion, the city and participating shops plan to display the local acceptance list together, rather than let a bag's retail shelf category imply it qualifies for curbside collection.

What happens to a bin that includes an unaccepted bag? City officials said it may receive a notice asking the resident to remove the item before the next ordinary collection. A rejected bin does not automatically trigger a fine, and the city has not announced any new financial penalty as part of this clarification.

The notice system poses a particular challenge in apartment buildings with shared bins, where an individual resident may not be the one who added a contaminating item. "A notice on the lid reaches the next person who opens it, not necessarily the person who put the bag there," said apartment manager Roven Dale. Apartment managers can request a demonstration session for shared-bin users to help address the issue. The processor reports contamination by incoming load rather than by household, so a rejected load does not identify a specific resident or establish habits for an entire building.

Residents whose bags are turned away should not simply toss them in the recycling bin — the food-scrap program is separate from ordinary recycling, and excluded liners aren't automatically recyclable. The city's waste guide lays out the current disposal route for excluded packaging and will be updated alongside the bin notices. Households that would rather skip liners altogether can use the city's loose-scrap collection method; the paper liners remain optional.

The city said it will revisit its accepted-material list if the processor's equipment or contract changes, and manufacturers may submit product information for that review — though a submission doesn't immediately change what collectors will take.

The processor turns finished material into contracted landscaping work after standard quality checks; it is not returned as a finished product to individual households.

Community gardens, meanwhile, often post their own composting rules, since many rely on smaller-scale methods different from the municipal system. This clarification does not require gardens to adopt the processor's list.

The city has not released a percentage of bins currently sorted correctly citywide or a quantified estimate of how much this clarification might improve outcomes.
