# For rooftop mushroom grower, short deliveries still carry cooling costs

Before office tenants began arriving, insulated delivery boxes from Flint Roof Mushrooms were moving down a shared freight lift, an August dispatch note records. The mushrooms had been grown on the commercial building’s roof. Getting them to customers still meant coordinating packing, cooling and access to the building below.

The business grows mushrooms in controlled rooms on a roof that formerly held an unused staff terrace. An old doorway now opens into the packing room. Founder Omi Carlen rents the growing space from the building owner and employs three part-time workers, supplying restaurants and a weekly market.

The location puts production close to city customers, but proximity does not eliminate the equipment, scheduling or costs involved in delivering food.

“Being close removes some miles. It does not remove the fridge, the lift or the clock,” Carlen said.

Most restaurant deliveries travel by cargo cycle. A hired van handles a weekly order beyond the cycle route. The shared freight lift is another link in that system: An outage in July delayed one dispatch and forced staff to rearrange a restaurant’s delivery window.

For restaurant buyer Rella Haver, small, frequent deliveries are valuable because her kitchen has limited cold storage. The benefit depends on matching deliveries to what the kitchen needs, rather than simply having mushrooms growing nearby.

“Freshness is useful when the order fits the service we are actually about to cook,” Haver said.

That requires advance decisions. Restaurant orders must be confirmed two days before delivery, and Flint Roof Mushrooms does not guarantee every variety on every date. Customers have a nearby supplier, but they still have to plan around its ordering schedule and available crop.

The supplied trading week shows how the harvest divided among customers and other destinations. Of 80 kilograms harvested, 60 went to restaurants and 12 were sold at the market. Five kilograms were donated and three composted.

Restaurant sales at $12 per kilogram brought in $720. Market sales at $18 per kilogram added $216, producing $936 in gross sales from the 72 kilograms sold. The donated and composted mushrooms generated no sales revenue in that week’s record.

Those figures do not establish profitability. The business pays $800 a month for its space and reported a $1,300 electricity bill for its latest full month. Neither monthly bill can be set against a single week’s sales as though the figures covered the same period. Nor do those bills represent every operating cost. Carlen has not provided a complete profit statement.

Electricity also complicates any simple environmental conclusion about growing close to customers. Temperature control and cooling require power even when delivery journeys are short. Flint Roof Mushrooms has not published a full energy-per-kilogram or emissions comparison with mushrooms delivered from outside the city.

The business uses separate lots and dispatch records to trace its own batches. Those practices document its output; they do not establish broader food-safety superiority.

Expansion is not assured, either. The landlord has approved the current use of the space, but there is no agreement to double the farm’s area. Carlen has provided no plan to supply all of the city’s mushroom demand.

For now, the operation’s reach is defined by its growing rooms, confirmed orders and delivery routes. The rooftop shortens some journeys. The August dispatch still began with insulated boxes and a shared lift.
