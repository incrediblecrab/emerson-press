# For Flint Roof Mushrooms, a short delivery route still needs cooling and a working lift

Before office tenants began arriving, Flint Roof Mushrooms was moving insulated delivery boxes down a shared freight lift, according to an August dispatch note. The mushrooms had been grown in controlled rooms on the commercial building's roof. Getting them downstairs was one step on a short route to customers.

The roof formerly held an unused staff terrace. An old doorway now opens into the packing room, part of a business that serves restaurants and a weekly market. Founder Omi Carlen rents the growing space from the building owner and employs three part-time workers.

Flint Roof's operation shows the practical decisions behind growing food close to city customers: how much to harvest and sell, how to keep it cool and how to deliver it when a kitchen needs it. Proximity shortens customer journeys, but temperature control still requires electricity, and dispatch depends on the building's shared equipment.

"Being close removes some miles. It does not remove the fridge, the lift or the clock," Carlen said.

Most restaurant deliveries are made by cargo cycle. A hired van handles a weekly order beyond the cycle route. In July, a lift outage delayed one dispatch and required staff to rearrange a restaurant's delivery window.

For restaurant buyer Rella Haver, small, frequent deliveries are valuable because her kitchen has limited cold storage. The usefulness of a nearby grower depends partly on matching an order to the kitchen's immediate work.

"Freshness is useful when the order fits the service we are actually about to cook," Haver said.

That requires advance decisions. Restaurant orders must be confirmed two days before delivery, and Flint Roof does not guarantee every variety on every date. Customers have a short delivery route, but they still need to plan around the grower's ordering deadline and availability.

The trading record also distinguishes what the farm grows from what it sells. In the supplied week, Flint Roof harvested 80 kilograms of mushrooms. Restaurants bought 60 kilograms and market customers bought 12. The business donated five kilograms and composted three.

Restaurant sales at $12 per kilogram brought in $720. Market sales at $18 per kilogram added $216, for gross sales of $936 on the 72 kilograms sold. The donated and composted mushrooms generated no sales revenue.

Those figures do not establish a profit. Flint Roof pays $800 a month for its space and reported a $1,300 electricity bill for its latest full month. The weekly sales record and monthly bills cover different periods, and the bills do not represent every operating cost. Carlen has not provided a complete profit statement.

Nor does the short delivery route establish an overall energy or emissions advantage. Cooling and temperature control use electricity even when customers are nearby. The business has not published a full energy-per-kilogram or emissions comparison with mushrooms delivered from outside the city.

As of Sept. 8, expansion remained unresolved. The landlord had approved the current use of the space, but there was no agreement to double the farm's area. Carlen had not provided a plan to supply all of the city's mushroom demand.

For now, the operation serves its restaurant orders and weekly market from rooms reached through the former terrace doorway. The August dispatch record follows its harvest out in insulated boxes, down the shared freight lift before the office tenants arrive.
