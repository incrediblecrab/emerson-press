RILLHAVEN — Oren Venn labeled floor panels as a crew hauled them from storage, fitting each one into place along a sequence the caretaker keeps in his head as much as on paper. Above the stacks of folded tables, the roller rink's skate rail was still bolted to the wall. Workers moved past it with tape measures, marking off the aisles where market stalls will stand.

The Rillhaven community rink is partway through its scheduled winter conversion, the seasonal switch that turns the skating floor into an indoor market for the colder months. The building isn't becoming permanent retail space — the skates and rink equipment are going into storage, not out the door, and the trust plans to bring them back when the season turns again.

Converting the room means more than laying down tables. A removable protective floor covers the skating surface. Entrances are being revised, and the stall layout has to keep agreed public routes clear. The building's access assessment is being reviewed specifically for the market configuration, because a route that works around skating spectators isn't the same as one through rows of stalls.

"The room changes jobs, but it is still the same room," Venn said. "We have to leave ourselves a way to turn it back."

That means discipline about where things go. Traders have to use their assigned pitches and equipment arrangements rather than set up wherever floor space looks open. Venn's conversion checklist covers more than the floor itself — public signs and toilet access are on the list too. Storage is tight enough that rails, panels and tables all have to be inventoried, so this season's equipment doesn't end up buried behind next season's supplies.

The trust hasn't set a final opening date. The first market day follows completion of the conversion checks, Venn said, not simply the delivery of the last table.

Money is part of the calculation. Market stall fees contribute to the building's winter operating costs under the community trust's budget, though the record doesn't show those fees covering every annual cost or making skating sessions self-supporting on their own. The trust keeps separate booking lists for seasonal skating and market use rather than treating every booking as the same kind of activity.

Elin Raye, a regular skater, has been helping move portable barriers during the conversion. She'd like a longer indoor skating season, and the trust has discussed occasional winter skating sessions — though none has been promised within the current market layout.

"Thursday is still skating night in my head, even when the room has tables in it," Raye said.

The market will include food and craft stalls under the trust's existing vendor arrangements, not a supermarket run by the caretaker's office. Above what used to be the skate hire counter — now the market's information point — a row of old skate sizes is still painted on the wall.

When the season turns again, the reverse conversion will need its own preparation schedule, Venn said, with the panels coming out just as carefully as they went in.
