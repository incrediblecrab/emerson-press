**Why Keeping Vaccines Cold Is a Global Health Equity Problem**

Every vaccine that reaches a child's arm has traveled through a system called the cold chain — the network of refrigerated storage and transport that keeps vaccines at the right temperature from the factory to the clinic. When that chain breaks, the consequences are often invisible, and they fall hardest on the world's poorest countries.

Most conventional vaccines must stay between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius. Some, including varicella and other live-attenuated vaccines, must be frozen at minus 20 degrees. mRNA vaccines are far more demanding, requiring storage between minus 20 and minus 80 degrees — colder than most low-income health systems can reliably sustain.

The danger of a broken cold chain is that it doesn't announce itself. A vaccine exposed to too much heat, or frozen when it shouldn't be, can lose potency partially or completely while still looking normal.

"A vaccine that has been heat-exposed is not obviously damaged," said Dr. Adaeze Okonkwo, a global health researcher at International Health Solutions. "It injects and the child shows no reaction. You only discover the failure at the population level, when outbreaks occur."

The World Health Organization estimates that up to half of vaccines worldwide are wasted because of temperature failures, expired stock and broken vials, with the highest losses in countries where electricity supply is unreliable. Cold storage units in many low-income facilities depend on unstable power grids. Solar-powered equipment has extended cold chain coverage into remote areas, but it requires maintenance capacity that isn't always available.

To catch damaged doses before they're used, many vials carry vaccine vial monitors — small heat-sensitive labels that darken permanently when a vial gets too hot, giving health workers a way to discard compromised doses. Data loggers, electronic devices that track temperature over time, are also increasingly deployed, though reading and acting on that data requires infrastructure many clinics lack.

The riskiest stretch is what's known as the last mile: the trip from a district health facility to a village clinic or a home. Health workers rely on passive cold boxes and insulated carriers for that leg, but these have limited duration before their contents warm.

Freezing poses a less-recognized threat than heat. Aluminum-adjuvanted vaccines form aggregates when accidentally frozen, reducing their effectiveness — a failure mode that gets less attention than heat damage but is no less real.

The stakes go beyond wasted doses, Okonkwo said. When a compromised vaccine fails and a vaccinated child later develops the disease, parents may conclude the vaccine itself failed. "That's the hidden cost," she said, describing an erosion of public trust that can outlast the immediate outbreak.

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed the inequity built into cold chain infrastructure. Early mRNA vaccines' ultra-cold storage requirements meant low-income countries received mainly vaccines with conventional cold chain needs, reinforcing existing disparities in access.

Some vaccines sidestep part of the problem already. Freeze-dried, or lyophilized, formulations — used for measles, yellow fever and meningitis vaccines — are inherently more heat-stable than liquid vaccines, though they require a diluent to be mixed in before use. Researchers are also developing thermostable formulations for diseases including polio and measles, designed to tolerate higher temperatures for longer stretches, though regulatory approval is still pending.

The WHO's Expanded Program on Immunization requires countries to maintain cold chain capacity at national, regional, district and facility levels. The GAVI Vaccine Alliance has invested more than $800 million in cold chain equipment in low-income countries over the past decade, an investment credited with expanding vaccine coverage across sub-Saharan Africa. In conflict zones, where refrigerators have been destroyed or fuel has run out, vaccination campaigns are often compressed into short windows timed to how long a cold box can hold its temperature.

The cold chain was built for a world of conventional vaccines, Okonkwo said. The arrival of mRNA platforms has exposed how fragile that infrastructure is for newer biologics — a gap that, without investment in thermostable technology, will keep shaping who gets protected and who doesn't.
