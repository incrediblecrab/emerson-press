# Why seas are rising — and why some coasts face greater danger

Sea level is rising as ocean water warms and melting land ice adds water to the seas. But the threat facing a coastal community depends on more than the global average: Whether its land is sinking, how exposed it is to storms and what resources it has to adapt all matter.

As of Jan. 22, 2025, the reporting snapshot shows a rise that is accelerating, with consequences that can arrive well before a shoreline is permanently underwater.

## How much, and how fast?

Global average sea level has risen approximately 21 to 24 centimeters, or about 8 to 9 inches, since 1880. The current pace is approximately 3.7 millimeters a year, up from about 1.5 millimeters annually in the early 20th century — more than twice as fast.

Two processes account for that increase. Water expands as it warms, a process called thermal expansion that contributes approximately 40% of the observed rise. Melting glaciers contribute roughly 30%, while losses from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets account for roughly another 30%.

Measuring the change requires combining different perspectives. Yemi Adeyinka, a coastal oceanographer at Atlantic Research Center, said scientists must reconcile tide gauges, which measure water levels relative to land, with satellite measurements of absolute ocean surface height.

## What lies ahead?

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s most likely projection is a global average rise of 0.3 to 1 meter — roughly 1 to 3.3 feet — by 2100, compared with 2000 levels. Where the result falls within that range depends on greenhouse gas emissions.

Under high-emissions scenarios, some projections include a low-probability but high-impact possibility of up to 2 meters, or about 6.6 feet, if instability in Antarctica triggers faster-than-expected ice sheet collapse. That is a risk, not the central forecast.

Greenland holds enough ice to raise global sea level approximately 7 meters if it melted completely; Antarctica holds enough for roughly 58 meters. Those are not projections for this century. Complete melting would take centuries to millennia even under worst-case warming. The nearer-term concern is faster movement of outlet glaciers into the ocean.

Because ice sheets respond slowly to warming, some rise caused by past emissions cannot be avoided even if emissions stopped today.

## Why does location matter?

“Global averages obscure the local reality. Some coasts are rising relative to sea level due to land uplift; others are sinking due to subsidence, doubling or tripling the effective rate of rise,” Adeyinka said.

Groundwater extraction, sediment compaction and the slow adjustment of Earth’s crust after past glaciation can cause land to sink. Delta cities including New Orleans, Jakarta and Bangkok are subsiding faster than global sea level is rising.

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration projects 0.3 to 1.5 meters of rise along U.S. coastlines by 2100, with the Southeast and Gulf Coast at the higher end of local projections.

## Who is most at risk?

In most coastal areas, the most immediate flood threat is storm surge: temporary, extreme water rises during hurricanes and typhoons. A higher starting sea level lets surge reach farther inland. Salt water can also enter coastal freshwater aquifers, threatening drinking water before permanent inundation.

Low-lying atoll nations such as Tuvalu and Kiribati face existential risks. Both have begun negotiating legal status for their populations if their islands are submerged.

Resources shape vulnerability, too. Wealthy nations can invest in seawalls and adaptation, while low-income coastal communities have fewer options, Adeyinka said. Restored mangroves, salt marshes and barrier reefs can reduce wave energy and erosion, but warming and rising seas threaten those ecosystems. Planned relocation offers a long-term option, though it has faced resistance in most countries where proposed.
