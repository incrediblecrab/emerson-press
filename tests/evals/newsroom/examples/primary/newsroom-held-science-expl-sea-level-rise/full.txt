# Why seas are rising — and why some coasts face greater danger

Sea level is rising as warming ocean water expands and melting land ice adds water to the seas. But the danger along any coastline depends on more than the global average: whether the land is sinking, how storms push water ashore and what people can afford to do about it.

As of January 2025, global average sea level has risen approximately 21 to 24 centimeters, or about 8 to 9 inches, since 1880. The pace has accelerated to approximately 3.7 millimeters a year, up from about 1.5 millimeters annually in the early 20th century.

That yearly increase can sound small. Over time, however, a higher ocean gives storm surges a higher starting point and allows salt water to reach coastal freshwater supplies before land is permanently submerged.

## What is driving the rise?

Thermal expansion — water expanding as it warms — accounts for approximately 40% of the observed increase. Melting glaciers contribute roughly 30%, and losses from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets account for roughly another 30%.

The ice sheets contain enormous amounts of water. Complete melting of Greenland’s ice sheet could raise global sea level approximately 7 meters; Antarctica holds enough for roughly 58 meters. Those are not projections for this century. Complete melting would take centuries to millennia, even under worst-case warming. The nearer-term concern is that glaciers carrying ice from the sheets into the ocean will speed up.

Ice sheets respond slowly to warming. Some future sea level rise is already locked in by past emissions and could not be avoided even if emissions stopped today.

## How much higher could the ocean get?

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s most likely projection puts global average sea level rise by 2100 between 0.3 and 1 meter above 2000 levels — approximately 1 to 3.3 feet — depending on greenhouse gas emissions.

Some high-emissions projections include a low-probability, high-impact possibility of up to 2 meters by 2100 if instability in the Antarctic ice sheet triggers faster-than-expected collapse. That possibility is distinct from the most likely range.

Local projections differ. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s U.S. technical report projects 0.3 to 1.5 meters of rise along U.S. coastlines by 2100, with the Southeast and Gulf Coast at the higher end.

## Why are some places more vulnerable?

“Global averages obscure the local reality. Some coasts are rising relative to sea level due to land uplift; others are sinking due to subsidence, doubling or tripling the effective rate of rise,” said Yemi Adeyinka, a coastal oceanographer at Atlantic Research Center.

Subsidence means the land is sinking. Groundwater extraction, sediment compaction and the slow adjustment of Earth’s crust after past glaciation can contribute. River delta cities including New Orleans, Jakarta and Bangkok are sinking faster than global sea level is rising.

For most coastal areas, the most immediate flood threat is storm surge: temporary, extreme increases in water level during hurricanes and typhoons. Higher seas allow that water to penetrate farther inland. Salt water entering freshwater aquifers can also threaten drinking water before permanent flooding occurs.

Low-lying coral atoll nations such as Tuvalu and Kiribati face threats to their continued existence. Both have begun negotiating the legal status of their populations if their islands are submerged.

Resources also shape risk. Adeyinka said wealthy nations can invest in seawalls and adaptation, while low-income coastal communities have fewer options. Restoring mangroves, salt marshes and barrier reefs can reduce wave energy and erosion, though warming and rising seas threaten those ecosystems too. Planned relocation, known as managed retreat, is a long-term option that has faced resistance in most countries where it has been proposed.
