Artist, Neighbors Return to Lark Ford Underpass Mural for Annual Touch-Up

Painter Hessa Noll and a small group of neighborhood volunteers spent part of this week examining the mural beside the Lark Ford pedestrian underpass, part of an annual maintenance session meant to keep the artwork intact without altering its original design.

The mural, which depicts river birds and working boats along the underpass wall, has drawn periodic upkeep since it was completed, and this year's session followed the same pattern: assessment first, repair second, and no changes without the artist's sign-off.

Noll stood near a faded painted wing on a recent afternoon, holding an older reference sheet as volunteers marked sections of the wall that appeared to need attention. The work required distinguishing between ordinary surface dirt, genuine paint loss and marks that had been added to the wall sometime after the mural was finished — categories that call for different responses, according to those involved. Not every visible difference, they said, is treated as a reason to repaint an entire section.

"We have a record of the colors, but the wall has also had several winters," Noll said. "Matching one little patch is not always a simple act of going back."

The wall itself remains city property, but the maintenance agreement governing the mural requires Noll's approval for any change to its design. The agreement permits the kind of upkeep performed this week — cleaning, patching, careful color-matching — but does not authorize volunteers to add new characters, slogans or other elements to the artwork.

Resident Orin Vale, who helped paint one of the original boat shapes when the mural was created, brought an old project photograph to this year's session to help with the reference work.

"I recognize the part I worked on even when I can no longer remember the exact shade that came out of the tin," Vale said.

Organizers said the photograph is being used as a general reference rather than treated as proof that every color currently on the wall exactly reproduces the original paint. The original project drew on drawings submitted by local residents before Noll finalized the design, which volunteers said is part of why so many people recognize small individual elements in the finished work.

A small neighborhood fund is covering the cost of materials approved for this year's work, while city staff arranged temporary barriers around the work area. The pedestrian route through the underpass has remained open throughout, routed along a marked alternative path. Organizers said they planned the session around the underpass's busiest school and commuting hours rather than assuming the area would be empty during the work.

By the end of the session, the team had recorded which repairs were completed, leaving any unresolved questions about materials or damage for Noll and the city's building-maintenance staff to address later. Organizers noted that the record does not draw conclusions about who made various marks on the wall over time or why.

Some residents have floated the idea of adding a new panel to the mural as a future project, but organizers said that idea remains separate from — and outside the scope of — the current maintenance work. No expanded design or permanent repainting schedule has been approved beyond this year's arrangement, and the maintenance notes from this session will be kept alongside the mural's original design records for reference in future years.
