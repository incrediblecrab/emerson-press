# What an fMRI scan measures — and why it cannot read minds

A brain region that “lights up” on a scan is not displaying a thought. Functional magnetic resonance imaging, or fMRI, measures changes in blood flow associated with brain activity. It gives researchers a way to map where activity changes, but it does not directly record neurons firing or establish what someone is thinking.

The distinction matters when interpreting claims about behavior, mental illness or differences between groups. Cognitive neuroscientist Yuna Breckinridge of Westfield University describes fMRI as a powerful tool for mapping functional brain organization at the population level, but not a mind-reading technology.

## A signal one step removed

Active neurons need more oxygen and draw increased blood flow. An fMRI scan detects a signal related to blood oxygen levels, known as BOLD, short for blood oxygen level dependent. That signal is a proxy for neural activity rather than a direct measurement of neurons’ electrical signals.

The timing differs considerably. Neurons fire on a scale of milliseconds; the BOLD response unfolds over seconds. Compared with electroencephalography, or EEG, fMRI provides excellent spatial resolution — showing where activity changes — but poorer temporal resolution, meaning it is less precise about when those changes happen.

Even its detailed images cannot show individual neurons. A typical voxel, the smallest imaging unit, covers approximately 1 to 3 cubic millimeters of tissue and contains millions of neurons.

## Activity does not establish cause

“When someone says 'the brain region X lit up,' what they mean is the BOLD signal in that region increased under those conditions. That is not the same as saying X causes or produces the behavior,” Breckinridge said.

A scan can show an association between activity in a region and a task. By itself, it cannot demonstrate that the activity causes the person’s performance.

Nor does observing activity uniquely identify a mental process. Many brain regions are active during multiple tasks. Working backward from a region’s activation to a claim about someone’s thoughts is a problem known as reverse inference.

Other methods can help test cause. Transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS, can temporarily disrupt function in specific brain regions, providing causal evidence to complement fMRI’s correlations.

## The study matters as much as the scan

Head movement creates artifacts in scan data. Correction algorithms reduce that problem but cannot eliminate it. Participants also must lie still in a noisy, confined scanner, a setting that may affect the social or emotional behaviors researchers want to understand.

Small samples have been another systematic weakness. Many classic studies tested fewer than 30 participants, leaving results vulnerable to false positives.

Breckinridge said the field has substantially improved since 2016 through larger samples enabled by consortium data sharing, registration of hypotheses before studies begin and replication across research sites.

## Patterns are not individual verdicts

Machine learning tools have identified fMRI patterns that distinguish groups of people. Their reliability for individual clinical decisions remains limited, and they are not generally approved as diagnostic tools.

Comparisons between groups also require careful controls for factors such as education, socioeconomic status and scanner type.

Even when no specific task is assigned, fMRI has identified networks of regions that consistently become active together. What these resting-state networks do is still being established. Mapping a pattern is a useful finding; determining what it means requires further evidence.
