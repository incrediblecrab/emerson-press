HEADLINE: Behind the Register: Why a Thrift Shop's Donations Don't Become Instant Cash for Music Bursaries

MORROW — A bag of clothing left at the door of Morrow Cupboard looks, to most donors, like a bag of money waiting to happen. To the volunteers who open it, it looks like a question.

"A bag at the door is a question about what is inside, not a price tag we can already count," said Omi Nelden, one of 14 volunteers who help run the nonprofit shop, which raises money for adult music-study bursaries and employs a part-time manager.

An April stock note illustrates the point. Volunteers sorting one donation found saleable jackets mixed in with incomplete bedding sets and damaged clothing — items that had to be separated before anything could go on a rack.

That sorting work shapes the shop's whole operation. According to its April intake ledger, the shop took in 1,200 donated items that month. Of those, 740 were priced for sale, 260 were accepted by a recycling partner, and 200 were sent for disposal. Not all 740 priced items had sold by month's end, so the figure is not a record of cash sales — only of what volunteers judged fit to display.

Manager Cera Veylan said condition, completeness and seasonal demand all factor into whether a donated item can be priced and put out. A stained coat, a bedsheet missing its matching pillowcase, or a swimsuit arriving in December may not clear that bar, even if the same item would sell easily at another time of year.

The shop's costs don't pause for the sorting, either. It pays rent, the manager's wages, utilities and waste-handling charges, despite receiving its stock at no purchase price. "The donation arrives once," Veylan said. "We still have to sort it, find room for it and sometimes pay to take it away." Items the recycling partner won't accept are not guaranteed a home elsewhere; the partner takes only specified textiles, and some rejected goods go to disposal.

Volunteer hours vary too. The 14 volunteers contribute different amounts of time, not a fixed shift each, which is part of why an unfamiliar item can take longer to evaluate than a familiar garment does. "It's not that we don't want it," Nelden said. "It's that judging it takes time somebody has to have."

Despite those costs, the shop's published annual account shows an $18,000 transfer to its bursary fund after operating expenses — money that funded six $3,000 bursaries rather than a payment to every person taking local music classes. The record does not track what happened to recipients' careers or earnings afterward.

Customers, meanwhile, can walk in and buy ordinary clothing without applying for assistance or disclosing household income; the shop's charitable purpose runs through where the proceeds go, not who is allowed to shop there.

To manage the volume, the shop has begun limiting donation drop-offs to staffed hours, so volunteers can explain in person what it currently accepts. Whether that has changed donation levels, disposal costs or overall income has not yet been evaluated. The board is weighing additional storage space but has not signed a lease or approved more paid hours.

The shop still occupies a former music-supply store, its long glass-fronted display cabinet intact — a quiet reminder, staff say, that even the building's past life required someone to decide what belonged on the shelf.
