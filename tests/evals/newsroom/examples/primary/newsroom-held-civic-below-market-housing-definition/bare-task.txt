Headline: What "Below-Market" Really Means in Stoneharbor's New Rental Program

Stoneharbor officials this month began enrolling apartments in a new rental program built around a single idea: participating units must be offered at a rent capped below a published benchmark. But housing officials and tenant advocates are cautioning residents not to read too much into that label, saying a discount from a reference number is not the same as an income-based affordability guarantee.

**How the cap works**

Under the program, the stated rent of a participating home cannot exceed 80% of a reference rent the city publishes each year for a defined category of housing. That reference figure is a program benchmark tied to a type of unit — it is not calculated from any individual tenant's income or personal finances.

The city's own illustration shows how the math functions: a $1,000 monthly reference rent produces an $800 cap on a participating unit. Officials used that example only to demonstrate the formula, not to describe an actual apartment or tenant.

The illustration also included a hypothetical household earning $2,000 a month in gross income, for whom an $800 rent alone would represent 40% of income — a widely used threshold for rent burden. But city officials stressed that example is instructional. It does not reflect a real tenant's income and should not be treated as typical of what program participants will pay relative to their earnings.

Housing director Vera Dall said the discount percentage should not be read in isolation. "The label needs its number beside it," Dall said. "A reduction from a benchmark does not tell us what every household can manage."

**What the number leaves out**

The reference-rent comparison covers only the base rent — it does not include utilities or any separately listed service charges, which can add to a household's actual monthly cost.

A local housing advice group has asked the city to list total expected housing charges in program listings, rather than just the discounted base rent. "People need the amount they will actually have to find each month, including the lines below the headline rent," said advice worker Perrin Moss.

**Scope of the first agreement**

The initial agreement covers 70 privately managed apartments spread across three existing buildings with differing layouts and service-charge arrangements — not all new rental housing in the city. The units remain under their private owners' management and do not become city-owned public housing, nor does the program grant tenants a right to purchase their apartments.

Applicants must meet the program's published eligibility conditions, but the city has not set a universal income cutoff, and meeting eligibility rules does not guarantee an offer of housing.

The rent restriction applies for five years under each participating agreement; any extension beyond that requires a new agreement, and the city has not promised any property will remain enrolled indefinitely. The benchmark itself can change with each annual revision, and individual lease terms and notice arrangements must still be checked against program documents. The city has said it will publish the benchmark source and the date it covers, and will check landlords' submitted rent schedules against the cap.

The advice group notes the rental program is separate from the city's emergency accommodation and direct rent-assistance programs.

Officials have not yet reported occupancy figures, average tenant incomes or any citywide effect on rents from the new agreement. City officials also emphasize that this program's specific definition of "below-market" applies only locally and is not a universal standard for affordable housing.
