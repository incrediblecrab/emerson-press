# Keeping Ternhaven bus station's last payphone ready for the next caller

Station attendant Jorin Fell wipes the receiver of Ternhaven bus station's last public payphone as part of his opening routine. He also checks that the displayed number for reporting a fault is still legible.

The phone is mounted beside the staffed information window, not in a separate street booth. Fell keeps spare paper and a pencil at the window for travelers who need to write down a number before they call. A nearby bench gives callers somewhere to set their luggage.

Those small provisions help keep the phone usable alongside the maintenance supplied by its owner, Northline Telephone cooperative. As of Sept. 18, the station has no approved plan to remove it during current renovation work. Its continued place in the concourse depends on practical support, though the cooperative has made no indefinite maintenance commitment.

"Most days I walk past it carrying something else. Then somebody needs it and the whole point of keeping it becomes obvious," Fell said.

One such need appears in the supplied station record: A traveler who had missed the last bus to a nearby village used the payphone to arrange a ride. The traveler had a mobile phone, but its battery was exhausted.

That account offers a specific example of what the payphone can do. It does not establish why other callers use it, or how many people depend on it. The cooperative records completed calls and equipment faults but does not collect a public list of callers or their conversations. The supplied records contain no count of distinct users.

For community worker Eda Rill, the phone fits into the practical services she helps new residents identify: things they can use without an existing online account.

"It is one of the few things here that does not begin by asking you to log in," Rill said.

Access still has limits. The phone accepts coins and a prepaid calling card sold at the station kiosk, but the kiosk and information window have different opening hours. Callers can reach the phone while the concourse is open, not at every hour of the night. And while the bench offers room for luggage, the surrounding concourse is no private, soundproof space for a conversation.

Keeping the equipment working is the cooperative's responsibility, rather than Fell's or the bus operator's. The station pays a small site-service charge under its agreement with Northline. The supplied material does not disclose whether this particular phone makes a profit or a loss.

Two other public phones were removed during earlier concourse renovations because their equipment was no longer supported. Replacement parts remain available for the surviving unit. Northline says a review of the phone would consider use, repairability and the service agreement, rather than call volume alone.

When the phone fails, station staff report the fault and put up a dated notice. They do not leave a disconnected receiver looking ready for someone who needs to make a call.

A faded rectangle on the wall marks where an older printed telephone directory rack once hung. At the information window, Fell keeps the spare paper and pencil.
