# At Stitch Hour Exchange, repair time is currency — and a constraint

In an August session note from the Stitch Hour Exchange, Leda Marron was shortening trousers while another member repaired a bag strap at the next table. The two jobs illustrate the neighborhood group's premise: Repair work can be traded in hours rather than paid for in cash.

But keeping the hours balanced is not the same as making every repair possible — or accounting for all the work needed to bring people together.

The exchange has 28 registered members and meets twice a month in a rented community room. It began after a one-day coat-mending event in 2024 and stores its supplies in two wheeled cabinets.

For each completed job, the worker receives a credit and the requester takes a debit for the same agreed amount of time. An hour earns an hour, regardless of what the person doing the work would ordinarily charge commercially.

That puts members' time on equal terms in the ledger, without making their skills interchangeable. Credits cannot be redeemed for cash, and having a balance does not guarantee that a particular skilled member will take a request. Four requested jobs were declined because no available member had the necessary skill or equipment.

Marron, who formerly altered clothes commercially, says teaching a beginner is different from promising a shop's turnaround time.

“I can offer the skill, but I still need the freedom to say that a job is beyond this table,” Marron said.

Members agree on the expected time before a repair begins and discuss changes if the work becomes more involved. The exchange does not guarantee a manufacturer's finish and does not accept repairs to safety-critical equipment.

Those limits distinguish the arrangement from simply buying a service. A credit records time contributed; it is not a promise that any repair can be delivered on demand.

Nor does participation eliminate cash expenses. A $3 attendance charge helps pay for room rental and shared thread, rather than buying an hour of another member's labor. Members provide unusual fabrics, replacement zippers and other materials specific to their jobs.

Newcomers start with a zero balance and may owe up to two hours while arranging work they can contribute. Sewing is not the only way to earn credits: Some members organize materials or set up the room.

Yet one recurring contribution remains outside the accounting. Coordinator Omi Veylan spends about three hours a week arranging requests and updating the ledger, receiving neither cash nor time credits for that work.

“The hours can balance on the page while the person matching them is still running out of time,” Veylan said.

The ledger recorded 46 credited service hours from April through June, excluding Veylan's coordination time. It attached no cash value to those hours. Organizers also have not measured how many purchases the repairs prevented or whether participants' household finances changed.

The records therefore show time exchanged, not a documented amount of money saved. They also leave a gap between the labor members trade and the labor that keeps those trades moving.

As of Sept. 8, the group's current room booking ends in December. A proposed permanent workspace has no funding commitment. For now, the exchange's hours depend on a rented room, available skills and coordination that earns no hours at all.
