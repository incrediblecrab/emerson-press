# At Stitch Hour Exchange, repair time is the currency — and the constraint

Leda Marron shortened trousers while another Stitch Hour Exchange member repaired a bag strap at the next table, an August session note records. The repairs were different, but the rule for crediting the work was the same: One agreed hour earned one hour in return.

Marron formerly altered clothes commercially. At the exchange, her usual commercial wage does not determine what her time is worth in the ledger. Nor does a credit buy an unconditional claim on her skills.

The 28-member exchange brings neighbors together twice a month in a rented community room to trade repair time instead of paying one another for labor. Its records show how that arrangement makes room for different contributions — and where an accounting system based on equal hours reaches its limits.

For every completed job, the worker receives the same agreed amount of time that the requester owes. Members settle the expected time before a repair and discuss changes if the work proves more involved. Credits cannot be redeemed for cash, and having a balance does not guarantee that a particular skilled member will accept a request.

New members start at zero but can owe up to two hours while arranging work they can contribute. Sewing is not the only way to earn credits: Some members organize materials or set up the room.

The arrangement still needs money and supplies. A $3 attendance charge helps pay for room hire and shared thread; it does not purchase an hour of someone else's labor. Members provide unusual fabrics, replacement zips and other materials specific to their jobs.

The ledger records 46 credited service hours from April through June 2026. It assigns no cash value to those hours and excludes the work of coordinator Omi Veylan, who spends about three hours a week arranging requests and updating the ledger without cash or time credits.

“The hours can balance on the page while the person matching them is still running out of time,” Veylan said.

That coordination sits outside the exchange's otherwise matched entries: A repair adds credit to one account and takes the same amount from another, but arranging those transactions earns Veylan nothing in the ledger.

Available skills also set a limit. Four requested jobs were declined because no available member had the relevant skill or equipment. The exchange does not guarantee a manufacturer's finish or accept repairs to safety-critical equipment.

Marron says teaching a beginner is different from promising a shop's turnaround time. Her experience is available to the group, but it comes with boundaries.

“I can offer the skill, but I still need the freedom to say that a job is beyond this table,” Marron said.

The recorded hours establish how much credited service passed through the exchange, not how much money members saved. Organizers have not measured how many purchases were avoided or how participants' household finances changed.

The group began after a one-day coat-mending event in 2024. As of Sept. 8, its current room booking ends in December, and a proposed permanent workspace has no funding commitment. For now, its supplies fit in two wheeled cabinets.
