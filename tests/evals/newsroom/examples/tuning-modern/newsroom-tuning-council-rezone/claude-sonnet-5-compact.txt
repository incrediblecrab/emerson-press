Meridian Heights Council Approves Rezoning for Former Rail Yard, Setting Up Fight Over Water Costs

MERIDIAN HEIGHTS — The City Council voted 6-3 Tuesday night to rezone a 28-acre former rail yard for mixed-use development, clearing the way for as many as 1,200 housing units on a site that has sat empty since the railroad shut it down six years ago.

The vote on the Northline Site, at the council's regular Tuesday session, allows residential and commercial buildings up to 14 stories on the property in the city's northeast corridor. Meridian Central Railroad closed the rail yard in 2019.

The developer, Anchor Point Development Corp., must submit a final site plan within 18 months. Under the city's inclusionary housing ordinance, at least 20% of the units — about 240 — must be priced for households earning 80% or less of the area median income.

Council President Diane Vasquez, who has represented the northeast district since 2014, voted for the plan and called it the product of years of work. "This is a decade of negotiation finally bearing fruit," she said. A previous attempt to rezone the same site failed on a 4-5 vote in 2021.

Three members — Marcus Okafor, Rhonda Tran and Eli Suárez — voted no, citing concerns that the city's water infrastructure cannot support the added density. Planning Director Rebecca Lim told the council that a water main capacity study completed in January found the corridor would need $14 million in upgrades to serve the project as planned. That cost is not part of the rezoning approval and would have to be addressed separately, through the city's capital budget process.

"We are approving density without a clear answer on whether the pipes can handle it," Okafor said.

An Anchor Point representative told the council the company would pay for the water upgrades if the city granted a 10-year tax abatement on the project. Rather than deciding the question Tuesday, the council voted to table the abatement discussion until its June budget cycle, leaving the infrastructure financing unresolved for now.

The rezoning drew opposition from the Meridian Residents Coalition, which submitted a petition against the plan signed by 2,300 residents. Some residents have also raised concerns about transit capacity: the corridor is currently served by a single bus line, and city estimates suggest the development could add 2,000 to 2,400 residents to the area.

Tuesday's meeting ran nearly four hours and drew 47 public speakers, 31 in favor and 16 opposed.

Supporters of the rezoning have pointed to the city's broader housing shortfall. The regional planning authority has estimated Meridian Heights needs 8,500 additional housing units to meet demand, a figure council members on both sides of Tuesday's vote cited during the meeting.

The rezoning now moves forward without a resolved funding source for the water main upgrades the city's own study says are necessary. Whether Anchor Point's tax abatement request is approved in June, rejected, or renegotiated will determine how — or whether — the $14 million infrastructure gap gets closed before construction can begin. Anchor Point has 18 months to file its site plan; the council has not set a hearing date for the abatement item.
