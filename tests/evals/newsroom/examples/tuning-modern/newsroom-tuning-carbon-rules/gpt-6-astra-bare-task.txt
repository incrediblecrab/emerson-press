# Agency orders power plant carbon cuts, drawing pledge of legal challenge

The Environmental Compliance Agency issued a rule Wednesday requiring about 340 coal and natural gas power plants to meet a 40% carbon dioxide reduction requirement by May 2029 or cover emissions above that limit with approved offset credits.

The Interim Clean Generation Standard immediately drew a pledge of a legal challenge from the American Power Producers Alliance, which argues the agency exceeded its statutory authority. The rule also faces criticism over its expedited adoption process and potential costs.

The standard takes effect May 9, 2025, with covered plants required to comply by May 9, 2029. It measures reductions against each facility’s 2024 emissions and applies to coal and natural gas generation facilities with capacities of at least 100 megawatts. Nuclear, solar and wind facilities are not covered.

“The climate math is simple: we have run out of time for voluntary measures,” agency Administrator Thomas Beckwith said.

The agency projects that full compliance will reduce annual carbon emissions from covered facilities by approximately 280 million metric tons.

Plant operators have four compliance options: reduce emissions, purchase approved carbon offset credits, retire a facility, or demonstrate financial hardship and apply for an 18-month extension. The rule sets an initial offset credit price of $45 per metric ton, subject to annual revision.

Key implementation details remain unfinished. The agency has not published its list of approved third-party offset certifiers, though it said that list will appear in a supplemental Federal Register notice before the May 9 effective date. An additional notice addressing the enforcement framework is expected by July.

Independent energy analysts at the Meridian Policy Institute estimate compliance costs of $18 billion to $31 billion over four years, depending on offset credit prices.

Alliance President Roger Holt said the rule “bypasses congressional intent and will raise electricity rates for millions of families.” The industry group said it would challenge the standard in the 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals.

That planned lawsuit would be separate from a pending case filed in February by Harwick, Dunmore and Selby challenging the agency’s use of emergency rulemaking authority.

The agency invoked that authority to replace its ordinary 180-day consultation schedule with an expedited process. It conducted a 45-day comment period earlier this year and received approximately 12,000 written comments.

Political criticism included a joint statement from two Democratic senators representing coal-producing states, who called the rule “procedurally flawed and economically reckless.”

Environmental organizations welcomed the action. The Clean Energy Now coalition, which represents 34 environmental groups, called the standard “a necessary and overdue first step.”

The previous standard, adopted in 2018, required an 18% emissions reduction from 2014 levels by 2030. The new rule moves the compliance deadline to 2029 and uses a 2024 baseline. Because the standards measure reductions from different years, their percentage targets are not directly comparable without additional emissions data.
