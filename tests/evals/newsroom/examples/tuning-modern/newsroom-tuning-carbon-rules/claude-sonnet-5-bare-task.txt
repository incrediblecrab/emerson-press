Agency Unveils Sweeping New Power Plant Emissions Rule, Setting Up Legal Fight

The Environmental Compliance Agency issued a new regulation Wednesday requiring coal and natural gas power plants to slash their carbon dioxide emissions by 40 percent within four years, a move that drew immediate opposition from industry groups and some lawmakers while environmental advocates praised it as overdue.

The Interim Clean Generation Standard applies to roughly 340 coal and natural gas facilities with a generating capacity of 100 megawatts or more. Under the rule, covered plants must cut emissions 40 percent by May 9, 2029, compared with their 2024 levels. The rule takes effect May 9, 2025, 30 days after its publication.

"The climate math is simple: we have run out of time for voluntary measures," Agency Administrator Thomas Beckwith said in announcing the standard.

The regulation replaces a 2018 standard that required an 18 percent emissions cut from 2014 levels by 2030. The agency did not provide a comparison between the two baselines.

Plants have four ways to comply: reduce emissions directly, purchase carbon offset credits, retire the facility, or apply for an 18-month extension by demonstrating financial hardship. Facilities that rely on offsets will pay an initial credit price of $45 per metric ton, a figure subject to annual revision. The agency said the list of approved third-party certifiers needed to verify offset credits has not yet been published but will appear in a supplemental Federal Register notice before the rule takes effect.

The agency said it expects the rule, if fully complied with, to cut annual carbon emissions from covered facilities by about 280 million metric tons. Nuclear, solar and wind facilities are not subject to the rule.

Details of how the agency will enforce the standard are still pending; a supplemental notice on enforcement is expected by July.

The agency moved unusually fast to finalize the rule, invoking emergency authority to shorten its typical 180-day consultation process. It held a 45-day public comment period earlier this year, receiving about 12,000 written comments, before issuing the final rule Wednesday.

That expedited process has already drawn legal scrutiny. Three states — Harwick, Dunmore and Selby — filed a lawsuit in February challenging the agency's use of emergency rulemaking authority. That case remains pending.

The American Power Producers Alliance, an industry trade group, said Wednesday it plans to file its own legal challenge in the U.S. Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals, arguing the agency exceeded its statutory authority in issuing the rule.

"This rule bypasses congressional intent and will raise electricity rates for millions of families," Alliance President Roger Holt said.

Two Democratic senators from coal-producing states also criticized the rule in a joint statement, calling it "procedurally flawed and economically reckless."

Environmental groups took a different view. The Clean Energy Now coalition, which represents 34 environmental organizations, called the regulation "a necessary and overdue first step."

Independent analysts are divided on the rule's economic impact. The Meridian Policy Institute, an energy research group, estimated compliance costs for the industry could range from $18 billion to $31 billion over four years, depending on how offset credit prices move.

With legal challenges already mounting on two fronts — from industry and from the states that oppose the agency's emergency authority — the rule's fate beyond its May 9 effective date remains uncertain. The agency has not indicated whether it will seek to expedite any court review or how it plans to respond to the pending litigation.
