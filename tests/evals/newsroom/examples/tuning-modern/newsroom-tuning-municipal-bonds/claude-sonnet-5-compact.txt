**Illinois city's bond default puts spotlight on municipal debt risks**

*[Editor's note: This is a synthetic exercise. Portfield, Illinois; Galena County, Texas; the Meridian Credit Rating Agency; the National Municipal Guarantee Corp.; and Harwick University are fictional, created for this drafting exercise.]*

The City of Portfield, Illinois, filed for Chapter 9 bankruptcy protection April 1, weeks after missing an $18.4 million interest payment on its general obligation bonds — a default that is drawing new attention to risks in the municipal bond market.

Portfield carries $340 million in general obligation bond debt. The city's Chapter 9 petition asks a federal court to adjust its municipal debts, but no ruling on Portfield's eligibility for that protection has been issued, and no plan or recovery estimate for bondholders has been approved.

Municipal bonds are debt securities issued by cities, counties, states and other local governments to pay for capital projects such as roads, schools and water systems. Two common types are general obligation bonds, backed by a government's general credit and taxing authority, and revenue bonds, backed by specific revenue streams; the exact repayment pledge depends on the individual bond.

Interest on most municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax and, in many cases, from state income tax for in-state investors. That tax treatment makes the bonds attractive to people in higher income brackets, for whom tax-exempt interest can be competitive after taxes, depending on their tax status and the bond's terms, according to a background sheet from the Meridian Credit Rating Agency. Meridian's sheet uses a scenario estimate of about $4 trillion in outstanding U.S. municipal debt as of December 2024, and it assigns 25% of that debt to mutual funds and money market funds combined.

Portfield is not the only issuer in distress. Galena County, Texas, defaulted on $87 million in general obligation bonds in February after its commissioners court declined to raise property tax rates. Combined, the two issuers' general obligation debt totals $427 million — a figure that spans two separate defaults, not one.

Meridian downgraded Portfield's bonds from an A rating to below investment grade in March. The city has roughly $1.2 billion in unfunded pension obligations against annual revenues of about $280 million, and its population has fallen from 94,000 in 2010 to an estimated 71,000 in 2024, according to U.S. Census figures. Portfield's bonds were partially insured by the National Municipal Guarantee Corp., which covered 40% of the outstanding principal. A company spokeswoman declined to discuss the Portfield policy specifically but said the insurer's exposure to below-investment-grade municipal bonds was "within our risk management parameters."

Linda Chai, who teaches municipal finance at Harwick University, said defaults remain uncommon overall but obscure wide differences among issuers. "The headline default rate is low, but it masks enormous variation between large, stable issuers and small cities running structural deficits," she said. Meridian's data show a 0.1% issuer-default rate among its rated municipal bonds in 2024, compared with 0.4% for its similarly rated corporate bonds — figures the agency describes as one-year comparisons, not long-term averages or predictions for any single bond.

Chai said investors should scrutinize pension disclosures in bonds issued by smaller cities. "The official statement tells you the debt, but the pension is what kills a city," she said. She pointed to pension obligations, tax revenue shortfalls and population loss as issues worth examining in Portfield's finances, without ranking their contribution to the default.

No federal guarantee or rescue has been announced for either Portfield's or Galena County's bonds. The Senate Finance Subcommittee on State and Local Finance has scheduled hearings on municipal debt distress for May.
