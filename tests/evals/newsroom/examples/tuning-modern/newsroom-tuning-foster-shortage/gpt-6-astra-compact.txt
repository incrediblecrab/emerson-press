# For Alcott County foster children, temporary placements stretch into months

Marcus is 9 years old and has been living in a hotel for four months. County staff say they have not found a foster family placement that meets his needs.

The child, identified in county records as Marcus, is one of 83 foster children in Alcott County, Texas, housed in hotels, county office suites and a staff member's private home. The hotels are commercial lodging properties, billed to the county at market rates.

These arrangements have become part of a temporary care system with no long-term solution identified, according to the county's child welfare director. A state citation in March has put the agency under pressure to correct its placements or risk losing control of its foster care operations.

"We are running a de facto shelter system inside a child welfare agency, and that is not what any of these children need," agency Director Beverly Chen said.

The Texas Department of State Health and Family Services cited the county for maintaining noncompliant placements and ordered it to bring the share of children in nonfamily settings below 10% within 90 days. State Regional Director Raymond Park, who issued the citation, said failure to comply could lead to state receivership of the county's foster care operations.

The county is already below that numerical threshold: The 83 children represent about 9.8% of its 847-child caseload. At that caseload, as many as 84 children would still fall below 10%. But meeting the percentage requirement does not resolve the citation over placement conditions.

The agency lists 312 licensed foster homes, though that figure alone does not establish how many children could be placed. A home can accommodate more than one child, and the available figures do not show total licensed beds or vacancies suitable for particular children.

Staffing and finances present additional constraints. The agency has 34 vacancies among 98 funded caseworker positions, roughly 35%. Starting salaries are $41,000 a year, compared with approximately $54,000 in neighboring states.

Foster parents receive $29 per child per day, below the state average of $38. About 28% of the county's foster families leave annually.

Elena Bell, a child welfare researcher at South Plains College, said recruitment alone would not resolve the county's problems. She recommended examining reimbursement and caseworker support.

A paid social media recruitment campaign launched in February had drawn 67 inquiries by early April, and the agency had approved 11 new foster families. Those figures do not establish the campaign's effect. Licensing a new foster home takes an average of seven months, down from a peak of 10 months in 2022.

The county cut its foster care budget by 12% in fiscal 2024 following a property-tax revenue shortfall. Chen said she had warned county commissioners of the crisis since mid-2023 but received no additional funding until the citation. Commissioners then allocated an emergency $750,000, which Chen called "helpful but not sufficient to resolve the structural problem."

A bill before the Texas Legislature would raise foster parent reimbursement to $52 a day statewide. House Bill 1402 has passed committee but has not been scheduled for a floor vote.

For now, the agency has an 18-month, $2.1 million contract with Bright Futures Partners, signed in January, to manage hotel and office placements. Chen called it a "stopgap."

For Marcus, the temporary arrangement has already lasted four months.
