**Three years after Keystone Motors closed, retraining program shows gains and gaps**

HARTWELL, Mich. — When Keystone Motors shut its assembly plant here on Dec. 31, 2021, it erased 2,800 jobs in a single stroke and, economists estimated, another 1,400 in supplier and service work that depended on it. More than three years later, a city-backed retraining program meant to answer that loss has moved roughly two-thirds of its graduates into new jobs — at wages that mostly fall well short of what the plant paid.

Hartwell Works, a partnership among Hartwell Community College, Vertex Systems and Clearline Manufacturing, began enrolling former Keystone workers in March 2022. By April 2025, about 1,100 of the plant's 2,800 former employees had signed up, and 780 had completed a certification program, a 71% completion rate. Of those completers, 499 — about 64% — found relevant employment within six months, according to the program's April report.

The $14.2 million effort, funded through Michigan Department of Labor and federal Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act grants, has drawn interest from other communities. The state labor department said Hartwell Works has the highest per-enrollee completion rate of any plant-closure program it has funded in the past decade.

Mayor Sandra Alvarado called the results mixed. "Sixty-four percent would be a good graduation rate at a lot of colleges," she said. "For a workforce-crisis response, I do not know if it is enough."

The wage gap is the program's most persistent problem. Completers who found work are earning an average of $22.50 an hour, compared with the $28.40 average Keystone paid before it closed — a difference of $5.90 an hour.

James Petrov, 52, felt that gap directly. He spent 19 years at Keystone earning $31 an hour, completed the program's CNC operator track in 2023, and was hired at Clearline Manufacturing at $23 an hour. "I am working. My house is paid for," he said. "But I will not make what I made at Keystone again."

Results vary sharply by track and by age. Manufacturing programs — CNC machining, industrial electrical and welding — posted an 83% completion rate and 72% placement rate. Information technology and computer-skills tracks completed at only 38%, which administrators attributed to workers lacking a computer background going in.

Workers over 50, who made up 40% of enrollees, fared worse after finishing: Their six-month employment rate was 51%, compared with 64% among all completers. Petrov said he urged two former colleagues to enroll and both declined. Of one, who was 58, he said: "He told me the math didn't work. I don't think he was wrong."

About 1,700 former Keystone workers never enrolled. Administrators estimate 400 left the Hartwell area, 300 retired early, and roughly 1,000 remain unemployed or in part-time work.

The city, meanwhile, is still absorbing the plant's absence. Combined property and income tax revenue fell 19% from 2021 to 2024, squeezing street maintenance and the fire department. The 65-acre plant site, demolished in 2023, has been listed for sale at $8.5 million with no buyers in 18 months.

Vertex Systems has hired 64 Hartwell Works completers. A company spokeswoman called the arrangement "mutually beneficial" but would not say whether its wages compare to Keystone's.

The program has been renewed for two more years, and director Carmen Ruiz said she expects to enroll 200 additional workers from other industries facing layoffs. Alvarado said the city has applied for a federal Economic Adjustment Assistance grant to fund a three-year study of the Hartwell Works model and publish a guide for other communities considering it.
