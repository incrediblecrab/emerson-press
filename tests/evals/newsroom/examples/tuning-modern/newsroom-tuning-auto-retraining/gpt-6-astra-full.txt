# After Keystone, retraining puts some Hartwell workers back on the job at lower pay

James Petrov spent 19 years at Keystone Motors, earning $31 an hour before the assembly plant closed. He completed a machining certification in 2023 and found work at Clearline Manufacturing. His new wage was $23 an hour.

“I am working. My house is paid for. But I will not make what I made at Keystone again,” said Petrov, 52.

His experience captures both the gains and limits of Hartwell Works, the city-supported retraining program for former Keystone employees. Its April 2025 report shows that nearly two-thirds of certification completers found relevant employment within six months. But average wages in the jobs completers obtained remained below the plant’s preclosure average, and most former workers never enrolled.

For Hartwell, a western Michigan city of approximately 43,000 residents, the question extends beyond how many workers earn certificates. More than three years after losing the employer around which it built its economy for over five decades, the city is still contending with lower tax revenue and an unsold industrial site.

Keystone closed Dec. 31, 2021, eliminating 2,800 direct jobs. Economists estimated another 1,400 supplier and service jobs disappeared across the region.

Hartwell Works began accepting enrollees in March 2022 through a partnership among Hartwell Community College, technology manufacturer Vertex Systems and Clearline. The program has cost approximately $14.2 million, financed through Michigan Department of Labor and federal Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act grants.

According to the program’s April report, approximately 1,100 former Keystone workers enrolled and 780 completed certification, a 71% completion rate. Of those completers, 499 — approximately 64% — found relevant employment within six months.

The Michigan Department of Labor said Hartwell Works had the highest per-enrollee completion rate of any plant-closure response program it had funded in the past decade. That measure tracks completion, however, rather than employment or restored earnings.

The report puts the average hourly wage in jobs obtained by completers at $22.50, compared with Keystone’s preclosure average of $28.40 — a gap of $5.90 an hour.

Outcomes also differed by training track and age. Manufacturing tracks in computer numerical control machining, industrial electrical work and welding recorded an 83% completion rate and a 72% placement rate. Information technology and computer-skills tracks had a 38% completion rate, which administrators attributed to gaps in prior skills among workers without computer backgrounds.

Workers over 50 accounted for 40% of enrollees. Among certification completers in that age group, 51% found relevant employment within six months, compared with approximately 64% overall.

The program’s figures do not capture a recovery for all 2,800 former workers. Approximately 1,700 did not enroll. Administrators estimate that about 400 left the area, about 300 retired early and the remaining approximately 1,000 are unemployed or working part time.

Meanwhile, Hartwell’s combined property and income tax revenue fell 19% between 2021 and 2024, straining street maintenance and the fire department. The plant was demolished in 2023. Its 65-acre site, listed at $8.5 million, has attracted no buyers in 18 months.

Hartwell Works has been renewed for two years with continued state funding. Program director Carmen Ruiz expects another 200 enrollees from other industries facing layoffs.

Petrov has encouraged two former colleagues to enroll. Both declined.

“He was 58. He told me the math didn't work. I don't think he was wrong,” Petrov said of one.
