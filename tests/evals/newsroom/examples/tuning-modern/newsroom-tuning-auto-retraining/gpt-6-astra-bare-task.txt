# After plant closure, Hartwell retraining program restores jobs, but not pay

HARTWELL, Mich. — James Petrov found his way back to manufacturing after the Keystone Motors assembly plant closed. What he did not recover was his paycheck.

Petrov, 52, spent 19 years at Keystone, earning $31 an hour. He completed a computer numerical control operator training track through Hartwell Works in 2023 and was hired by Clearline Manufacturing at $23 an hour.

“I am working. My house is paid for. But I will not make what I made at Keystone again,” he said.

His experience reflects both the promise and limits of Hartwell’s effort to rebuild after losing the employer that anchored this western Michigan city of approximately 43,000 for more than five decades.

Keystone closed the plant Dec. 31, 2021, eliminating 2,800 direct jobs. Economists estimated another 1,400 supplier and service jobs disappeared across the region.

Hartwell Works, a city-supported partnership among Hartwell Community College, technology manufacturer Vertex Systems and Clearline, began accepting enrollees in March 2022. According to its April 2025 report, approximately 1,100 former Keystone workers enrolled, and 780 completed certification programs — a 71% completion rate.

Of those completers, 499, or approximately 64%, found relevant employment within six months. That employment rate measures outcomes among graduates, not all enrollees or everyone displaced by the closure.

The Michigan Department of Labor said Hartwell Works had the highest per-enrollee completion rate of any plant-closure response program it had funded in the past decade. The program has cost approximately $14.2 million, financed through state labor department and federal Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act grants.

Mayor Sandra Alvarado questioned whether the results meet the scale of the crisis.

“Sixty-four percent would be a good graduation rate at a lot of colleges. For a workforce-crisis response, I do not know if it is enough,” she said.

The 64% figure is the program’s employment rate among completers; its graduation rate is higher.

Even for workers who found jobs, earnings lagged. Jobs obtained by Hartwell Works completers paid an average of $22.50 an hour, compared with $28.40 at Keystone before the closure — a $5.90 hourly gap.

Outcomes also varied sharply by training track and age. Manufacturing courses in CNC machining, industrial electrical work and welding recorded an 83% completion rate and a 72% placement rate. Information technology and computer-skills tracks had a 38% completion rate, a gap administrators attributed to workers’ lack of prior computer experience.

Workers older than 50 made up 40% of enrollees. Among certification completers in that age group, 51% found relevant employment within six months.

The program’s reach remains limited. Approximately 1,700 former Keystone workers never enrolled. Administrators estimate about 400 left the area, 300 retired early and the remaining 1,000 are unemployed or working part time.

Petrov encouraged two former colleagues to enroll. Both declined.

“He was 58. He told me the math didn't work. I don't think he was wrong,” Petrov said of one.

The city’s recovery extends beyond retraining. Combined property and income tax revenue fell 19% between 2021 and 2024, straining street maintenance and the fire department. The former plant was demolished in 2023; its 65-acre site, listed at $8.5 million, has attracted no buyers in 18 months.

Hartwell Works has been renewed for two years with continued state funding. Director Carmen Ruiz expects another 200 enrollees from other industries facing layoffs.

Alvarado said the city also has applied for a federal Economic Adjustment Assistance grant to support a three-year study and a replication guide for other communities — even as Hartwell continues to measure what retraining can, and cannot, replace.
