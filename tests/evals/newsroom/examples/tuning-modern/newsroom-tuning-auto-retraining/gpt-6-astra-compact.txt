# After Keystone, retraining gets Hartwell workers back to work — for less

James Petrov spent 19 years at Keystone Motors, earning $31 an hour before the Hartwell assembly plant closed. In 2023, he completed training to operate computer-controlled machine tools and found work at Clearline Manufacturing. His new wage: $23 an hour.

"I am working. My house is paid for. But I will not make what I made at Keystone again," said Petrov, 52.

His experience captures both the achievement and the limits of Hartwell Works, a city-supported retraining program renewed for another two years. Its April 2025 report shows that most participants completed training and nearly two-thirds of those completers found relevant work within six months. But their average wages remain below the plant's, and most former Keystone workers never enrolled.

For Hartwell, a western Michigan city of approximately 43,000 built around the plant for more than five decades, finding replacement jobs has not meant replacing what was lost.

Keystone closed the plant Dec. 31, 2021, eliminating 2,800 direct jobs. Economists estimated that another 1,400 supplier and service jobs disappeared across the region.

Hartwell Works began accepting enrollees in March 2022 through a partnership among Hartwell Community College, technology manufacturer Vertex Systems and Clearline. It has cost approximately $14.2 million, financed through Michigan Department of Labor and federal Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act grants.

According to the program's April report, approximately 1,100 former Keystone workers enrolled and 780 completed certification, a 71% completion rate. Of those completers, 499 — approximately 64% — found relevant employment within six months.

The Michigan Department of Labor said Hartwell Works had the highest per-enrollee completion rate of any plant-closure response program it had funded in the past decade.

Mayor Sandra Alvarado questioned whether the employment results were enough.

"Sixty-four percent would be a good graduation rate at a lot of colleges. For a workforce-crisis response, I do not know if it is enough," she said.

The 64% figure measures employment among certification completers, not graduation. The distinction matters: It does not describe outcomes for everyone who enrolled, much less all workers displaced by the closure.

The program report puts the average hourly wage in jobs obtained by completers at $22.50, compared with $28.40 at Keystone before closure — a $5.90 gap.

Results also differ by training track and age. Manufacturing tracks in computer-controlled machining, industrial electrical work and welding had an 83% completion rate. Information technology and computer-skills tracks had a 38% rate, which administrators attributed to gaps in prior skills among workers without a computer background.

Workers over 50 accounted for 40% of enrollees. Among certification completers in that age group, 51% found relevant employment within six months, compared with approximately 64% overall.

Approximately 1,700 former workers did not enroll. Administrators estimate that about 400 left the area, about 300 retired early and the remaining approximately 1,000 are unemployed or working part time.

The city's losses extend beyond paychecks. Combined property and income tax revenue fell 19% between 2021 and 2024, straining street maintenance and the fire department. The demolished plant's 65-acre site, listed at $8.5 million, has attracted no buyers in 18 months.

With continued state funding, program director Carmen Ruiz expects to enroll another 200 workers from other industries facing layoffs. Alvarado said the city also has applied for a federal grant to study the model over three years and publish a guide for other communities.

Petrov has encouraged two former colleagues to enroll. Both declined.

"He was 58. He told me the math didn't work. I don't think he was wrong," Petrov said of one.
