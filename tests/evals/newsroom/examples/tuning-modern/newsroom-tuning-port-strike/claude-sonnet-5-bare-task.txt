Port Strike Enters Third Day at Port Calloway as $180 Million in Cargo Sits Idle

CALLOWAY — Dockworkers at Port Calloway remained off the job Wednesday for a third straight day, leaving an estimated $180 million in cargo idle at one of the region's busiest shipping hubs as federal mediators worked to bring both sides to the table.

The strike began Monday when the International Longshore Workers Union Local 14 rejected a contract offer from port management and walked off the job. About 890 dockworkers are seeking a 28 percent wage increase over four years, while the port authority has offered 18 percent over the same period. The union's previous contract, which expired Dec. 31, provided a 12 percent raise over four years.

"We move every container that keeps this economy running, and we have been offered less than inflation in real terms," said Local 14 President Maria Reyes.

Port Calloway authority chief Donald Hsu said management wants to settle the dispute quickly. "We remain committed to reaching a fair agreement, but this disruption is costing everyone," he said.

A second point of contention involves automation. The port authority plans to install six new automated container cranes, which officials estimate will cut labor needs by about 18 percent per shift once operational. The union wants all new automation investment paused during negotiations, calling it a "red line." Hsu said the cranes are necessary for the port to stay competitive with neighboring facilities.

The standoff is having a mounting economic impact. About 2,100 containers are sitting at the port that would normally have been loaded or unloaded, including shipments of strawberries and lettuce, which account for roughly 12 percent of the stranded cargo. Port Calloway typically handles about $4.2 billion in cargo annually and serves as a principal entry point for electronics, auto parts and refrigerated agricultural products.

The Calloway Regional Chamber of Commerce estimated Wednesday that the strike is costing the regional economy about $60 million a day in delayed commerce and downstream supply chain disruptions. Three electronics importers and two auto parts distributors told reporters they had already missed delivery deadlines, though none agreed to be identified.

The port's terminal operator has redirected four arriving cargo ships to Port Westfield, about 80 miles up the coast, adding an estimated $2,300 per container in additional handling and trucking costs.

The Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service sent a team to Calloway on Tuesday. Team leader Gerald Watts said the two sides had agreed to talk but had not yet entered formal mediated sessions. California's governor's office also offered to assign a state mediator; the union said it was open to the idea, but port management said it preferred to continue with the federal process.

The port authority reported a $28 million operating surplus in fiscal year 2024, a figure Reyes has cited in pressing the union's wage demands.

The ILWU's national leadership has not declared a broader strike, and Local 14 is negotiating independently under its own collective bargaining agreement, meaning the walkout remains confined to Port Calloway for now.

The last major work stoppage at the port came in 2011 and lasted nine days before it was resolved through federal mediation. With mediators now on the ground and both sides describing themselves as willing to negotiate, officials on both sides expressed hope that this dispute could be resolved before it approaches that duration — though as of Wednesday, no formal talks had begun and the economic toll continued to climb.
