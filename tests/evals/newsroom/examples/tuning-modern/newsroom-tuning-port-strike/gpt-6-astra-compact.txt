# Port Calloway strike enters third day with $180 million in cargo idle

Dockworkers at Port Calloway entered the third day of a strike Wednesday, with an estimated $180 million in cargo sitting idle as the union and port authority remained divided over wages and automation.

The 890 striking workers are seeking a 28% wage increase over four years, compared with the port authority's offer of 18%. The union also wants a pause on all new automation investment during contract negotiations, while the authority plans to install six automated container cranes.

Federal mediators arrived Tuesday, but formal mediated sessions had not begun. Gerald Watts, team leader for the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service, said both parties had agreed to talk.

Members of International Longshore Workers Union Local 14 walked off the job Monday after rejecting management's offer. Local 14 President Maria Reyes said the proposed wages did not adequately compensate workers.

"We move every container that keeps this economy running, and we have been offered less than inflation in real terms," Reyes said.

The union's previous contract expired Dec. 31, 2024. It provided a 12% wage increase over four years. The current wage proposals are 10 percentage points apart, with automation posing a separate obstacle to an agreement.

Reyes called automation a "red line" for the union. The six planned cranes are estimated to reduce labor requirements by about 18% per shift once operational. That estimate concerns staffing needs per shift, not a specified number of layoffs.

Port authority chief Donald Hsu said the cranes were necessary for the port to remain competitive with neighboring facilities. The authority reported a $28 million operating surplus in fiscal 2024.

"We remain committed to reaching a fair agreement, but this disruption is costing everyone," Hsu said.

About 2,100 containers that would normally have been loaded or unloaded were sitting at the port Wednesday. Perishable cargo, including strawberry and lettuce shipments, accounted for roughly 12% of the stranded containers, according to port records.

The port handles about $4.2 billion in cargo annually and is a principal entry point for electronics, auto parts and refrigerated agricultural products.

The Calloway Regional Chamber of Commerce estimated Wednesday that the strike was costing the regional economy about $60 million a day in delayed commerce, including downstream supply chain disruptions. That estimate measures delayed economic activity, rather than establishing that the same amount has been permanently lost.

The port's terminal operator redirected four arriving cargo ships to Port Westfield, 80 miles up the coast. The diversions added an estimated $2,300 per container in handling and trucking costs.

The California governor's office offered to assign a state mediator. The union said it was open to that offer, but port management said it preferred the federal process.

The union's national leadership has not declared a formal national strike. Local 14 is acting independently under its own collective bargaining agreement.

Port Calloway's last major work stoppage, in 2011, lasted nine days and ended with federal mediation.
