# Port Calloway strike enters third day with $180 million in cargo idle

Dockworkers at Port Calloway entered the third day of a strike Wednesday with an estimated $180 million in cargo sitting idle, as a dispute over wages and automation delayed shipments and sent arriving vessels to another port.

The 890 members of International Longshore Workers Union Local 14 walked off the job Monday after rejecting management’s contract offer. They are seeking a 28% wage increase over four years, compared with the port authority’s offer of 18% over the same period.

Federal mediators arrived Tuesday, but the two sides had not begun formal mediated sessions as of Wednesday. Gerald Watts, leader of the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service team, said both parties had agreed to talk.

“We move every container that keeps this economy running, and we have been offered less than inflation in real terms,” Local 14 President Maria Reyes said.

Port authority chief Donald Hsu said management remained focused on a settlement.

“We remain committed to reaching a fair agreement, but this disruption is costing everyone,” Hsu said.

About 2,100 containers that would normally have been loaded or unloaded were sitting at the port. The Calloway Regional Chamber of Commerce estimated Wednesday that the stoppage was delaying approximately $60 million in regional commerce each day, including downstream supply chain disruptions. That estimate measures delayed commerce, not necessarily permanent economic losses.

Port Calloway handles about $4.2 billion in cargo annually and is a principal entry point for electronics, auto parts and refrigerated agricultural products.

The port’s terminal operator redirected four arriving cargo ships to Port Westfield, 80 miles up the coast. The diversions added an estimated $2,300 per container in handling and trucking costs.

Three electronics importers and two auto parts distributors told reporters they had missed delivery deadlines. None agreed to be named publicly.

Perishable cargo, including strawberry and lettuce shipments, accounted for roughly 12% of the stranded containers, according to port records.

Beyond wages, the sides remain divided over the port authority’s plans to install six automated container cranes. The union wants all new automation investment paused during contract negotiations.

The cranes are estimated to reduce labor requirements by about 18% per shift once operational. That estimate concerns staffing needs per shift, rather than a specified number of job cuts.

Reyes called automation a “red line” for the union. Hsu said the cranes were necessary to keep the port competitive with neighboring facilities.

The previous labor agreement expired Dec. 31, 2024. It provided a 12% wage increase over its four-year term. The port authority reported a $28 million operating surplus for fiscal 2024.

The California governor’s office has offered to assign a state mediator. The union said it was open to that offer, while port management said it preferred the federal process.

The stoppage is a local action under Local 14’s own collective bargaining agreement. The union’s national leadership has not declared a formal national strike.

Port Calloway’s last major work stoppage, in 2011, lasted nine days and ended with federal mediation.
