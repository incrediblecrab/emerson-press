**Blood network declares critical shortage of O-negative type, urges donors to act**

The nation's blood supply organization sounded its highest alarm Tuesday over a dwindling stock of O-negative blood, the type doctors reach for first in emergencies when there's no time to determine a patient's blood type.

The National Blood Reserve Network issued a Level 3 critical shortage alert — its top designation — for O-negative blood on July 15. The network said its O-negative inventory has fallen to 2.1 days' worth of supply, well below the five-day minimum it considers safe.

"O-negative blood cannot be manufactured. When donations drop, there is no alternative," said Dr. Patricia Osei, the network's chief medical officer.

O-negative blood is often used for emergency transfusions when a patient's blood type isn't yet known, because it's broadly compatible. But hospitals still run compatibility testing, and not every O-negative blood product can be used interchangeably.

The shortage stems partly from a seasonal dip. Network records from 2022 through 2024 show summer donations across all blood types running 15% to 25% below January levels, a pattern staff attribute largely to workplaces, schools and universities pausing blood drives. This year, June donations of O-negative blood came in 18% below the January baseline.

Only about 7% of the U.S. population has O-negative blood, meaning the pool of potential donors who can replenish that specific supply is limited.

The strain is visible across the network's infrastructure. Of its six regional collection centers, four reported inventory below 40% of target levels as of Tuesday.

At Calloway Medical Center, administrators said Tuesday they had postponed 14 elective surgeries requiring blood transfusions, while emergency and urgent procedures would continue using existing stockpiles. The hospital said it would consider directed donations from relatives for some elective patients, though it cautioned that processing would take at least 48 hours and availability isn't guaranteed even then. Those donations still must clear the same eligibility, compatibility and safety screening as any other.

Elsewhere, trauma surgeons at several hospitals contacted by the network said they had begun rationing O-negative blood, reserving it for cases where no other compatible type is available.

In response to the shortage, the network has activated emergency measures, including direct phone outreach to past donors and mobile collection units at high-traffic locations. Osei said the network expects to issue a formal appeal to regular donors by the end of the week and is working with major employers to organize workplace drives.

The network estimates it needs about 1,200 additional O-negative units per week nationally to restore safe inventory levels. Officials point to the summer of 2022, the network's last comparable shortage, when a Level 2 alert was resolved within four weeks after a coordinated donation push.

Part of the challenge may be public misunderstanding about who can give blood. In a survey commissioned by the network, 650 of 1,000 adults said they believed they were eligible to donate but had never done so — though the network noted this reflects respondents' own beliefs, not a clinical assessment of actual eligibility. Common reasons cited for not donating included fear of needles, uncertainty about eligibility and safety concerns.

Donors must wait at least 56 days between whole-blood donations and pass other screening requirements; having O-negative blood does not exempt anyone from those rules.

The Food and Drug Administration sets standards for how blood is collected and stored — red cells can be refrigerated for up to 42 days, while platelets have shorter shelf lives and are tracked separately — but it does not manage national inventory levels. That responsibility falls to nonprofit blood organizations like the network.

Donors can schedule appointments at regional centers or mobile events by visiting nbrn.example.org.
