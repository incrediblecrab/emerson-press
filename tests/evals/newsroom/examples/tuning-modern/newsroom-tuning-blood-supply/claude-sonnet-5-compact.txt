**Blood network declares highest-level shortage alert as O-negative supply falls to 2 days**

The National Blood Reserve Network on Tuesday declared a Level 3 critical shortage of O-negative blood, its highest alert level, saying the type's national supply had fallen to 2.1 days' worth of inventory. The network's operational safety minimum is five days.

O-negative red blood cells are the type most commonly used in emergencies when a patient's blood type has not yet been determined, though hospitals still perform compatibility testing before any transfusion. Only people who are themselves O-negative — about 7% of the U.S. population — can donate blood that adds to that supply, and donations to that pool cannot be replaced by any other type.

"O-negative blood cannot be manufactured," said Dr. Patricia Osei, the network's chief medical officer. "When donations drop, there is no alternative."

Four of the network's six regional collection centers reported Tuesday that their O-negative inventory stood below 40% of target. June's O-negative donations came in 18% below the January 2025 baseline, according to network data. The network has previously tied part of its broader summer slowdown — monthly donations across all blood types ran 15% to 25% below January levels in 2022, 2023 and 2024 — to workplaces, universities and schools suspending blood drives during summer months.

The strain is already reaching patients. Calloway Medical Center said Tuesday it had postponed 14 elective surgeries requiring blood transfusions, though it said emergency and urgent procedures would continue using existing stockpiles. The hospital said it would consider directed donations from relatives for some elective patients, estimating at least 48 hours to process such donations, without guaranteeing blood would be available even then; related donors and their donated units still must pass the same eligibility, compatibility and safety screening as any other donor.

Osei said trauma surgeons at several hospitals contacted by the network have told the network they are rationing O-negative blood, reserving it for cases where no other compatible type is available.

Under a Level 3 alert, the network activates emergency measures, including direct phone outreach to past donors and mobile collection units at high-traffic locations. Osei said the network expects to issue a public appeal to regular donors by the end of the week and is working with major employers to organize workplace donation drives. The network estimates it needs about 1,200 additional O-negative units per week nationally to rebuild to safe levels.

The last comparable shortage, a Level 2 alert in the summer of 2022, was resolved within four weeks after a coordinated donation push, though the network said the current shortfall is more severe.

The Food and Drug Administration sets standards for how blood is collected and stored, but it does not manage national inventory levels; that responsibility falls to nonprofit blood collection organizations such as the network.

A survey commissioned by the network found that 650 of 1,000 adult respondents believed they were eligible to donate blood but had never done so. The survey reflects respondents' own beliefs about their eligibility, not a clinical assessment, and the network cautioned against reading it as a fixed share of the U.S. population. Respondents who had not donated cited fear of needles, uncertainty about their own eligibility and concerns about donation safety.

Donors must wait at least 56 days between whole-blood donations and undergo standard eligibility screening; the network said that interval and screening requirements apply to O-negative donors as they do to everyone else. People interested in donating can schedule an appointment at the network's regional centers or at mobile collection events by visiting nbrn.example.org.
