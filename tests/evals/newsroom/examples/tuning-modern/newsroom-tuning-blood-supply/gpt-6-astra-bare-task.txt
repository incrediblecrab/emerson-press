# O-negative blood shortage triggers highest alert, delays elective surgeries

The National Blood Reserve Network issued its highest shortage alert Tuesday, July 15, with just 2.1 days of O-negative blood on hand — well below its five-day operational safety minimum.

The Level 3 alert comes as hospitals take steps to conserve supplies. Calloway Medical Center announced Tuesday that it had postponed 14 elective surgeries requiring blood transfusions. Emergency and urgent procedures will continue using existing stockpiles, the hospital said.

The network estimates it needs about 1,200 additional O-negative units each week nationally to restore safe inventory levels. Here is what the shortage means for patients and potential donors.

## Why is O-negative blood especially important?

O-negative red blood cells are commonly used in emergency transfusions when a patient’s blood type is unknown. That makes them particularly valuable when clinicians must act quickly.

But the designation does not mean every component of O-negative blood is universally compatible. Clinical protocols and compatibility testing still apply.

Only O-negative donors, who account for about 7% of the U.S. population, can contribute to the O-negative supply.

“O-negative blood cannot be manufactured. When donations drop, there is no alternative,” said Dr. Patricia Osei, the network’s chief medical officer.

## What is driving the shortage?

The alert follows a decline in donations. June O-negative donations were 18% below the January 2025 baseline, according to network data.

The network has also documented a broader summer pattern. In its 2022-2024 records, monthly summer donations across all blood types were 15% to 25% below January totals in the same year. Staff attributed part of that decline to workplaces, universities and schools suspending collection drives.

Those historical figures cover all blood types, not O-negative alone.

As of Tuesday, four of the network’s six regional collection centers reported inventories below 40% of their operational targets.

Blood also has a limited storage life. Under the methods described in the network’s background sheet, refrigerated red-cell units can be stored for up to 42 days. Platelet products have shorter labeled storage periods and are managed separately.

## How are hospitals and the network responding?

Trauma surgeons at several hospitals contacted by the network reported adjusting protocols to ration O-negative blood, using it only when no other compatible type is available.

The Level 3 designation activates emergency measures, including direct calls to donors and mobile collection units sent to high-traffic locations. Osei said the network expected to appeal to regular donors by the end of the week and was working with major employers on workplace drives.

Calloway said it would consider directed donations from relatives for some elective patients. Those donations are not an immediate or guaranteed solution: The hospital estimated processing would take at least 48 hours, without guaranteeing availability then. Donors and their units still require eligibility, compatibility and safety screening.

The network’s last comparable shortage, a Level 2 alert in summer 2022, was resolved within four weeks after a coordinated donation drive. That experience does not establish how quickly the current shortage will end.

## Who can help, and how?

Eligible O-negative donors can give at regional centers or mobile collection events. Appointments are available at nbrn.example.org.

The network requires at least 56 days between whole-blood donations, along with other eligibility screening. Having O-negative blood does not waive those requirements.

In a network-commissioned survey, 650 of 1,000 adult respondents said they believed they were eligible but had never donated. Those answers reflect respondents’ beliefs, not verified eligibility or an established share of all Americans. Non-donors cited fear of needles, eligibility uncertainty and safety concerns.

The Food and Drug Administration sets blood collection and storage standards, but it does not control national inventory levels. Supply management falls to nonprofit blood collection organizations.
