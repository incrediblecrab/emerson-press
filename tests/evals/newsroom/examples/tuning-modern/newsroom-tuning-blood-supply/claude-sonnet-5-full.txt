**National blood network declares highest-level shortage alert for O-negative type**

The National Blood Reserve Network declared its highest-level shortage alert Tuesday for O-negative blood, saying the type has fallen to 2.1 days of supply — well below the five-day minimum the network says it needs to safely meet hospital demand.

The Level 3 designation, the network's most severe, comes after a monthslong decline in donations. June donations of O-negative blood were 18% below the network's January baseline, according to network data. Four of the network's six regional collection centers reported inventory below 40% of their operational targets as of Tuesday.

O-negative blood is commonly used in emergencies when a patient's blood type is not yet known, though hospitals still follow compatibility testing and clinical protocols before transfusing it. Only people with O-negative blood — about 7% of the U.S. population — can donate units that count toward that specific supply.

"O-negative blood cannot be manufactured," Dr. Patricia Osei, the network's chief medical officer, said. "When donations drop, there is no alternative."

Summer slumps in donations are not new. Network records from 2022 through 2024 show monthly donation totals, across all blood types, running 15% to 25% below January levels each summer. Staff have attributed part of that pattern to workplaces, universities and schools suspending blood drives during the season.

The network's last comparable alert, a Level 2 shortage in summer 2022, was resolved within four weeks following a coordinated donation drive. The network said it now needs roughly 1,200 additional O-negative units per week nationally to return to safe inventory levels.

The shortage is already affecting patient care. Calloway Medical Center said Tuesday it had postponed 14 elective surgeries requiring blood transfusions, though emergency and urgent procedures will continue using existing stockpiles. The hospital said it would consider directed donations from relatives for some elective patients, estimating at least 48 hours to process such donations without guaranteeing blood would be available by then; related donors still must meet the same eligibility, compatibility and safety screening as any other donor.

Trauma surgeons at several hospitals contacted by the network said they have adjusted protocols to ration O-negative blood, using it only when no other compatible type is available.

In response to the shortage, the network said it is activating emergency measures, including direct phone outreach to past donors and mobile collection units at high-traffic locations. Osei said the network expects to issue an appeal to regular donors by the end of the week and is working with major employers to organize workplace donation drives.

The network's centers require a 56-day interval between whole-blood donations, along with other standard eligibility screening; having O-negative blood does not exempt a donor from that waiting period or from screening.

A survey commissioned by the network found 650 of 1,000 adult respondents believed they were eligible to donate but had never done so — a measure of self-reported belief rather than confirmed eligibility. Respondents who said they had not donated cited fear of needles, uncertainty about their own eligibility and concerns about donation safety.

The Food and Drug Administration sets standards for how blood is collected and stored but does not manage national inventory levels; that responsibility falls to nonprofit blood collection organizations such as the network. Under the network's storage guidelines, red-cell units may be refrigerated for up to 42 days; platelets, tracked in a separate inventory system, have shorter storage windows.

Donors can schedule appointments at the network's regional centers or mobile events through nbrn.example.org.
