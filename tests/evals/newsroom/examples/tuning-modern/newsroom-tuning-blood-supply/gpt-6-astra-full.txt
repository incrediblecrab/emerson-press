# O-negative blood shortage: Why supplies are low and what donors can do

The National Blood Reserve Network issued its highest-level shortage alert Tuesday, July 15, with O-negative blood inventory at 2.1 days’ worth, below its five-day operational safety minimum.

The Level 3 alert comes as Calloway Medical Center postponed 14 elective surgeries requiring blood transfusions. The hospital said emergency and urgent procedures would continue using existing stockpiles.

The network estimates it needs approximately 1,200 additional O-negative units per week nationally to restore safe inventory levels. Here is what the shortage means for patients and potential donors.

## Why is O-negative blood needed?

O-negative red blood cells are commonly used in emergency transfusions when a patient’s blood type is unknown. That makes the supply important when clinicians must act before they know which blood type a patient needs.

But that does not mean every component of O-negative blood is universally compatible. Clinical protocols and compatibility testing still apply.

Only people with O-negative blood, approximately 7% of the U.S. population, can donate blood that counts toward this supply.

“O-negative blood cannot be manufactured. When donations drop, there is no alternative,” said Dr. Patricia Osei, the network’s chief medical officer.

## What has happened to donations?

June O-negative donations were 18% below the January baseline, according to the network’s 2025 data.

Its records also show a broader summer pattern: From 2022 through 2024, monthly summer donation totals were 15% to 25% below January totals in the same year. Those figures combine all blood types, so they are not a measure of the seasonal decline in O-negative donations alone.

Network staff attributed part of that pattern to workplaces, universities and schools suspending collection drives.

As of Tuesday, four of the network’s six regional collection centers reported inventory below 40% of their operational target.

Blood also has storage limits. Under the methods described in the network’s background sheet, refrigerated red-cell units may be stored for up to 42 days. Platelet products have shorter labeled storage periods and a separate inventory system.

## How are hospitals and the network responding?

Trauma surgeons at several hospitals contacted by the network reported adjusting protocols to ration O-negative blood, using it only when no other compatible type was available.

A Level 3 shortage activates emergency measures that include direct phone outreach to donors and mobile collection units at high-traffic locations. Osei said the network expected to appeal to regular donors by the end of the week and was working with major employers on workplace drives.

The network’s last comparable shortage, a Level 2 alert in summer 2022, was resolved within four weeks following a coordinated donation drive. That history does not establish how long this shortage will last.

## Who can donate, and where?

The network accepts donations at regional centers and mobile collection events. Appointments can be scheduled at nbrn.example.org.

Its centers require at least 56 days between whole-blood donations and screen donors for other eligibility requirements. Having O-negative blood does not waive those requirements.

In a network-commissioned survey, 650 of 1,000 adult respondents said they believed they were eligible to donate but had never done so. Those answers reflect respondents’ beliefs, not confirmed medical eligibility or an established percentage of all Americans.

## Can relatives donate for a patient?

Calloway Medical Center said it would consider directed donations from relatives for some elective patients. These donations are not an immediate substitute for available inventory.

The hospital estimated processing would take at least 48 hours, without guaranteeing availability then. Related donors and their units must still undergo eligibility, compatibility and safety screening.
