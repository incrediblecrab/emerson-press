# After flood buyouts, a coastal county weighs what it saved — and lost

LOWLAND COUNTY, N.C. — Where flood-prone homes once stood, roughly 340 acres of restored coastal wetland now mark Lowland County’s decision to move people out of harm’s way. For Delia Marsh, 71, the offer to leave could not outweigh a lifetime at home.

“This is where I was born and this is where I intend to stay,” said Marsh, who inherited her house from her parents.

Six years after launching its buyout program, the county’s April 2025 review shows both the possibilities and limits of managed retreat: 240 completed purchases, neighborhoods cleared of buildings and residents whose attachment to place has survived repeated flooding. With no expansion planned, even some owners who now want to sell have no county program available.

The effort followed Hurricane Marion, a Category 3 storm that struck in September 2017. It flooded approximately 800 homes, many for the second or third time, and caused an estimated $340 million in property damage across the county of about 18,000 people.

In early 2019, the county launched an $80 million buyout program, funded by a $62 million Federal Emergency Management Agency hazard mitigation grant and an $18 million county match. Owners were offered 105% of their property’s pre-flood assessed value, with the additional 5% intended to help cover relocation.

“Six years later, the neighborhoods that moved do not flood. The ones that stayed do,” County Emergency Manager Robert Yates said.

The acquired properties have been cleared and converted to wetlands, removing buildings from exposure. County engineers also modeled lower peak flood levels behind the restored wetland than in a comparison without restoration. That finding does not establish a universal reduction in storm surge or guarantee protection in every storm.

Of the roughly 800 homes initially targeted, 112 owners declined to sell. Those properties flooded in both 2019 and 2022, with repair costs averaging approximately $34,000 per storm event. The review does not classify the initially targeted properties beyond the completed buyouts and refusals.

Marsh said she had flood insurance and had repaired her home twice since 2017. She declined to disclose her expenses or insurance payments.

Leaving carried costs beyond a change of address. Structures removed from bought-out land included the former Shoreline Community Elementary School, closed since 2016 because of flood damage, and the Shoreline Diner, a gathering place that operated for 38 years.

Maya Santos, a North Coastal University researcher who has studied the program for four years, said managed retreat’s long-term effectiveness depends heavily on where residents relocate and whether they find comparably affordable housing. Her research found roughly 60% of the 240 households stayed in the county; 40% moved to other regions, some outside North Carolina.

The program also took twice its projected three years, partly because title disputes involving 47 properties required legal resolution.

Yates estimated that disaster recovery, infrastructure repairs and flood insurance supplementation for the acquired properties would otherwise have cost about $112 million over six years. His comparison was with the $80 million authorized budget, not a final spending total, so it does not establish audited savings.

The county’s experience comes as Congress debates requiring managed retreat plans as a condition of renewed participation in the National Flood Insurance Program. Lowland officials have testified in favor. Neighboring Coastal Pines County instead spent $45 million on levee repairs and beach nourishment after Marion and suffered significant flooding again in 2022.

Lowland’s own buyout window has closed. Its remaining $3.1 million in unspent FEMA funds reverted to the federal government in January. Two owners who declined have since asked to sell, but the county has no current program to accommodate them.
