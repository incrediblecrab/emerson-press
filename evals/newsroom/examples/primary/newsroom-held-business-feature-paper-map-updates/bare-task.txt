# For Foldline Maps, keeping paper useful means knowing what to leave out

A path connection is coming off the next edition of the original Asterfen Footpath Map — not because mapmaker Neri Saven has established that it is permanently closed, but because the connection remains unconfirmed.

The decision, recorded in a June revision note, illustrates the work behind Foldline Maps’ seasonal walking-map business: deciding what a printed guide can reliably tell someone when routes, repairs and access information keep changing.

Foldline is a two-person business selling local walking maps through shops and visitor centers. It began with a hand-drawn festival-area leaflet in 2014 and now uses digital cartographic files to prepare its printed products. Digital production, however, does not make a map already sold any easier to revise.

“Paper does not need a signal, but it still needs a date,” Saven said.

The printed map identifies its revision month and directs readers to a dated corrections sheet available from stockists. That gives the business a way to circulate updated information, though the sheet cannot alter copies already in customers’ bags.

Before making proposed revisions, Foldline checks land-manager notices, direct permissions and its own recorded site observations. Those checks inform what goes into an edition without settling every question about a route.

Omitting a location because access is unconfirmed does not declare it permanently closed or legally private. Nor do the business’s current records provide authority to settle disputed public-access rights or guarantee every route’s future condition.

A footbridge under repair presents another test of what to print. As of June 30, the land manager had not announced a reopening date. Foldline is holding space for an update rather than printing a guessed reopening promise.

Such choices also carry financial consequences for a business that pays to produce maps before knowing how many will sell.

The current print run is 2,000 copies at $1.20 each, a $2,400 bill before fieldwork, design and other costs. The listed retail price is $3.50. Participating retailers retain 40%, or $1.40, leaving Foldline $2.10 per retail sale.

After the stated printing cost, that leaves 90 cents a copy before other expenses — not 90 cents in profit. Copies printed are not copies sold, and an unsold edition can become outdated before its paper wears out.

Retailers can return unopened bundles under negotiated terms, shifting some of that unsold-stock risk back to the publisher. The economics make keeping information current a stock-management issue as well as a cartographic one.

The appeal of paper does not necessarily depend on customers abandoning their phones. Visitor-center worker Omi Harlen says some customers want a printed overview while using a phone for other travel information.

“People are not always choosing paper instead of a phone. Sometimes they want the two things to do different jobs,” Harlen said.

Foldline has not measured a general decline in phone navigation or a universal preference for printed maps. Its business serves a use for paper without establishing a broader shift away from screens.

A larger regional edition remains a proposal, with no completed survey or committed print order. For now, the work includes checking the existing map, leaving uncertain connections out and keeping room for information that has yet to be confirmed.
