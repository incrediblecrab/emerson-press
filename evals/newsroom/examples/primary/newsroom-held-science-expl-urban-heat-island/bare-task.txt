# Why cities trap heat — and what they can do to cool down

Cities can be significantly warmer than surrounding rural and suburban areas, and the difference is not just a matter of weather. Buildings, pavement and human activity absorb, trap and generate heat, creating what researchers call the urban heat island effect.

The consequences extend beyond uncomfortable sidewalks and higher air conditioning bills. Heat islands increase dangerous exposure during heat waves, particularly in neighborhoods with fewer trees and less access to cooling.

“Heat islands are not just a comfort issue. They are a mortality issue,” said Naomi Wheeler, an urban climatologist at Clearview Urban Institute. “During heat waves, the gap between the city center and the outskirts can be the difference between a dangerous and a survivable heat exposure.”

## Why do cities stay hotter?

Dark surfaces such as asphalt and roofing absorb solar radiation rather than reflect it, then release that energy as heat. Dense clusters of buildings reduce wind speeds and trap heat, limiting cooling breezes.

Vehicles, industry and air conditioners add waste heat. Air conditioning thus provides relief indoors while contributing to higher temperatures outside.

City centers are typically 3.6 to 9 degrees Fahrenheit warmer than nearby rural areas during the day. On calm, clear nights, the difference can exceed 18 degrees.

That nighttime gap is especially consequential during heat waves. Rural areas cool more overnight, while city residents get less relief from the heat accumulated during the day.

The burden is uneven. Low-income neighborhoods tend to have less tree canopy, more hard surfaces that prevent water from soaking into the ground and less access to cooling infrastructure.

## What can cities change?

Trees cool the air through shade and evapotranspiration, the release of water vapor through leaves. A mature tree can cool its immediate shaded surroundings by up to 3.6 degrees Fahrenheit.

Wheeler said tree canopy is the most cost-effective single intervention, but it takes 10 to 20 years to mature enough to provide meaningful cooling. Reflective surfaces work immediately.

Cool roofs, painted white or covered with highly reflective materials, can lower roof surface temperatures by up to 54 degrees Fahrenheit and reduce building cooling costs by 10% to 30%. That surface-temperature reduction is not the same as an equivalent drop in neighborhood air temperature.

Vegetation-covered green roofs reduce heat absorption, improve insulation and slow stormwater runoff, though they require structural reinforcement and regular maintenance. Permeable pavements let water soak through, enabling evaporative cooling while reducing urban flooding risk.

Cities also can redesign streets flanked by tall buildings with building setbacks and street-level vegetation. Such retrofits are expensive and often require regulatory changes. Fountains and misting stations provide local relief, but they are not structural solutions and stop working in water-scarce conditions.

## How much difference can those measures make?

Between 2019 and 2024, Riverdale planted 18,000 trees and installed cool roofing on 340 municipal buildings. Monitoring found an average temperature reduction of about 3.2 degrees Fahrenheit within roughly 656 feet of intervention sites.

Wheeler said those findings show neighborhood-scale interventions work, but citywide results require sustained investment over decades.

Satellite thermal imaging now lets researchers map heat block by block. Several cities use those maps to prioritize tree planting and cool pavement programs in the hottest areas.

Economic modeling suggests that lowering a city’s temperature by 1.8 degrees Fahrenheit through trees and cool surfaces reduces air conditioning energy use by about 5%, with additional savings from fewer heat-related illnesses.

As urbanization increases exposure through 2050, cities being built or expanded have an opportunity to limit heat absorption from the start rather than face costly changes later.
