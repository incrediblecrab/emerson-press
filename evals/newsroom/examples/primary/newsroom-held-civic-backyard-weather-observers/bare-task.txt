# In Harrowmere, weather watchers look beyond the numbers

For Pella Dorne, checking the rain gauge means recording more than the rain. She checks it at a consistent morning time, writes down the date before emptying it and keeps track of anything that might complicate the reading.

When she removed a leaf from the gauge’s rim, she noted the obstruction beside that day’s entry. It was a small detail worth preserving for the next time she opened the notebook.

“The note about the leaf belongs beside the number. Otherwise I will trust the number more than I should when I read it next winter,” Dorne said.

That attention to how a measurement was made shapes the Harrowmere Weather Circle, a group of residents who keep small weather instruments at homes, gardens and a community allotment. At their monthly meeting, members compare observations and the circumstances behind them. They do not issue an official public forecast.

A reading comes with questions: Where was the instrument? What was nearby? Was anything obstructing it? Members record placement because a wall, tree or paved surface can affect what an instrument experiences.

The equipment itself also varies. Members’ instruments are not all identical, and they have not all undergone the same calibration process. Different readings from different locations are not treated as automatic proof that one neighborhood has a different long-term climate.

Retired technician Orin Saye helps members compare recording routines and identify maintenance questions. His focus is on making the figures understandable, not simply assembling more of them.

“A row of figures gets more useful when somebody can tell you how each one arrived on the page,” Saye said.

Sometimes, the record contains no figure at all. The circle leaves missing readings blank rather than estimating them from a nearby member’s observations. A member’s absence can leave a gap even when the instrument continues operating. Those blanks preserve a distinction between an observation someone recorded and a value that might merely seem plausible.

The meeting makes room for paper notebooks alongside downloads from newer instruments. An older notebook contains a sketch showing where a greenhouse stood before its owner’s instrument was moved — a record of the surroundings that helps put the measurements in context.

Members use accounts of frost timing and heavy rain to compare particular gardening seasons. Those personal comparisons are a way to examine what happened, not advice predicting the next season’s weather.

Selected, dated observations also go to a regional community-science project. That project applies its own acceptance checks, so submission does not guarantee that an observation will enter the final dataset.

Members pay for their own instruments, while the community center supplies the monthly meeting room without charge. The circle plans an exhibition there about recording practices, rather than a live emergency-warning display. Official weather alerts remain separate from its voluntary observations and social-media updates.

The planned exhibition reflects the work captured in Dorne’s note about the leaf: preserving not just a measurement, but enough information to judge it later. In the circle’s records, a blank space, a sketch or a few words beside a number can be part of understanding the weather, too.
