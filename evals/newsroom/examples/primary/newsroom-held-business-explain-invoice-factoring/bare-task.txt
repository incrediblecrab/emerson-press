# An order filled, a payment pending: How Hawthorn Gauge bridges the cash gap

Hawthorn Gauge finished and delivered a $40,000 order in July, but completing the work did not put the customer's money in its bank account. Payment was due 60 calendar days after the invoice date. Payroll for its 11 employees comes every week.

The fixtures maker bridged that gap by selling the invoice to Rillstone Factors for an immediate $39,000. The arrangement, known as factoring, gave Hawthorn cash before its customer had to pay, at a cost of $1,000.

“The invoice says we earned the money. Friday's payroll asks whether it is in the account,” Hawthorn owner Lena Brack said.

The transaction illustrates the difference between earning revenue and having cash available to meet bills. It also shows the limits of financing built around an unpaid invoice: Faster payment carries a charge, approval is not automatic and some nonpayment risk remains with the seller.

## Why is there a gap?

Hawthorn makes measuring fixtures to customer drawings in a converted service garage rather than carrying a large inventory of finished goods. For this order, it delivered a $40,000 batch to Pember Assembly and issued an invoice with 60-day payment terms.

Meanwhile, Hawthorn's material supplier requires payment within 30 calendar days of its own invoice date. That deadline runs from a different invoice than Pember's payment deadline, while Hawthorn's weekly payroll keeps coming.

Pember purchasing manager Daro Vale said the buyer's 60-day terms were published in its supplier agreement and did not change for this order. Pember has accepted the fixtures, and the supplied record contains no quality dispute.

As of Aug. 3, no late payment had occurred on this invoice. Hawthorn's need for cash therefore arose during the agreed waiting period, not because Pember had missed its deadline.

## What does Hawthorn get, and what does it give up?

By selling the invoice, Hawthorn received $39,000 immediately instead of waiting for the $40,000 payment. Under the agreement, Pember will pay Rillstone rather than Hawthorn when it settles the bill.

The $1,000 difference is a 2.5% charge on the invoice's face value. It is not a stated annual interest rate.

The benefit is timing: Hawthorn can access cash while its customer's payment is still pending. The cost is accepting less than the full amount billed. The sale does not give Rillstone an ownership stake in Hawthorn, and no public subsidy or guaranteed loan is part of the transaction.

## Who carries the risk?

The invoice sale does not remove all of Hawthorn's exposure. Once 75 calendar days have passed since the invoice's issue date, the agreement allows Rillstone to return an unpaid invoice to Hawthorn.

Rillstone also checks the invoiced customer's payment record and may reject an invoice even if the selling business wants immediate cash.

“We can advance the payment date, but we do not turn a disputed order into an undisputed one,” Rillstone manager Omi Keel said.

## Is factoring the cheapest option?

The available figures cannot answer that question. Brack said a conventional credit-line application was still under review when this payroll period arrived. The bank had issued neither a final credit decision nor a rate quote for comparison.

Hawthorn used factoring on two of its last 14 invoices but has not provided the fee for the other financed invoice. Repeated use would add transaction costs, though the figures do not support an annual companywide financing total.

For this order, the trade-off is clear: Hawthorn gave up $1,000 to receive $39,000 immediately. That establishes the price of closing this cash gap, not that factoring is cheaper than every loan or available to every small business.
