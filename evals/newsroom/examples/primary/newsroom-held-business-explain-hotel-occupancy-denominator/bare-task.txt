Wren Coast Occupancy Rose Even as Hotels Sold Fewer Rooms and Earned Less Revenue, Report Shows

By [Staff Writer]

A closely watched measure of hotel business along the Wren Coast climbed in April, but a closer look at the numbers shows the improvement came from a shrinking pool of available rooms, not from more travelers booking stays.

The Wren Coast Lodging Association's August 2026 report compares five participating hotels in April 2025 and April 2026. It found the group's occupancy rate rose from 80% to 85% even though the hotels sold 360 fewer room nights — a 15% drop — and brought in $15,600 less in room revenue, a 6.5% decrease.

The explanation lies in the report's denominator. In April 2025, the five hotels offered 3,000 available room nights and sold 2,400 of them, for the 80% occupancy figure. A year later, renovations took 20 rooms out of service for all 30 days of April, cutting available room nights to 2,400. The hotels sold 2,040 room nights that month. Divide 2,040 by 2,400, and the result is 85% — a higher rate, even though the actual number of rooms sold declined.

"A higher occupancy rate can describe fuller remaining capacity rather than more total business," said Ceri Venn, an analyst with the association who worked on the report.

"A smaller hotel can look fuller on the percentage line while its reception desk sells fewer nights," Venn said.

Room revenue tells a related but distinct story. The average daily rate the hotels charged rose from $100 to $110 per room night, according to the report. That increase offset some of the loss from selling fewer rooms, so total room revenue fell only 6.5%, to $224,400, rather than dropping in step with the 15% decline in room nights sold.

A third figure in the report, revenue per available room night, rose sharply, from $80 to $93.50. But the report cautions that this jump also reflects the smaller pool of available rooms rather than a genuine increase in business, since the same total revenue is now divided across fewer available nights.

The report notes that renovations affected different room types unevenly, so the higher average rate does not mean every room's listed price rose by 10%.

Local business owners say the hotel figures are difficult to translate into their own foot traffic. Tavin Holt, who owns a restaurant near the waterfront, said hotel guests are only one source of his customers, alongside local residents and day visitors.

"I cannot turn a hotel percentage into a count of people coming through my door," Holt said.

The association cautions against reading too much into any single figure in the report. Room nights are not the same as individual guests, since one room can house more than one person and a single stay can span several nights. The revenue totals exclude food, parking and event income, and they do not measure profit.

The report also has narrower limits. It covers only five hotels that volunteer to participate in the association's data collection — two housed in converted boardinghouses, three purpose-built — not every lodging business on the coast. Short-term rentals and campsites are not included. And the report does not attempt to isolate how much of the changes stemmed from the renovations, shifts in demand, pricing decisions or the mix of rooms sold.

The association said it expects the renovated rooms to return to service this autumn, but it has not published confirmed reopening dates.
