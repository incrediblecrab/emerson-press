# Small cafe orders give Plover Reed Flowers a measure of certainty

Nessa Sorn separated shorter flower stems for cafe tables from taller ones reserved for a weekend event on Aug. 12. At Plover Reed Flowers, those lengths were headed toward two kinds of business: standing restaurant orders and occasional event work that can fetch twice the price per stem.

The farm grows decorative cut flowers on two rented acres, with Sorn and two seasonal workers doing the work. Six cafes have standing orders totaling 900 stems a week during the agreed summer season, giving Sorn a recurring order to fill alongside less predictable sales.

The choice is more complicated than taking the highest price. Cafe orders bring a measure of certainty about demand, but small deliveries take time. Event orders can bring higher receipts, but require more sorting and are not guaranteed each week. The farm has not completed a margin comparison showing what remains from cafe, event and farm-gate sales after costs.

At 60 cents per delivered stem, the cafe orders produce $540 in weekly gross receipts when the farm supplies all 900 contracted stems. Event orders can pay $1.20 per stem. Both prices are receipts before growing, labor, packing and delivery costs, not profit per stem.

“The regular order tells me something about next week. It does not tell me what every bed will produce,” Sorn said.

The cafe agreements leave some room for that uncertainty. They allow seasonal substitutions within a stated color range instead of requiring the same flower variety every week. When a crop bloomed later than expected, the farm recorded two weeks of substituted varieties. All six cafes accepted them.

That experience shows how the agreements have worked during a delayed bloom. It does not establish that the cafes will accept every future substitution, or that event customers will offer similar flexibility. Event orders require more sorting by color and length.

For cafe owner Omi Holt, a shorter stem suits the space where the flowers will go. Holt prefers arrangements that let customers see across small tables.

“The arrangement has to leave space for lunch, not just look good before anyone sits down,” Holt said.

Meeting those table needs involves work beyond cutting flowers. Plover Reed Flowers makes two cafe delivery rounds a week, with each round taking approximately three hours. Handling many small bunches adds work that a single large collection order may not require. The seasonal workers are paid for packing and delivery preparation as well as cutting.

The standing orders also have limits. Cafe customers can change quantities with seven days’ notice, and the agreements cover the summer season, not guaranteed year-round income. The recurring business gives Sorn some knowledge of the coming week without settling demand for the rest of the year.

Additional mixed bouquets go on sale at the farm gate, but passersby have made no commitment to buy surplus stems. That leaves another outlet for flowers without the advance quantities of the cafe agreements.

For now, the farm is filling small recurring orders while reserving taller stems for an event. In the former potting shed used as a packing room, buckets are marked for each cafe.
