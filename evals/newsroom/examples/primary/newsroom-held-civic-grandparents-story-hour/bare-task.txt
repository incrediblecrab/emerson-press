# Grandparents help bring two languages to Fellmere library story hour

At the Fellmere library, volunteer reader Laila Venn repeats a picture book character's action with her hands while a librarian holds the illustration where children can see it. The story moves through English and Arabic, with pictures and gestures offering ways to follow along.

Venn is among the volunteer grandparents who pair with staff readers for the library's monthly bilingual story hour. Families are welcome whether or not they speak both languages. The gathering is a shared reading activity, not an assessed language course, and parents and caregivers stay with their children.

For Venn, the exchange between languages has a familiar place in family life.

"My grandson sometimes explains a word to me in English, and I give him a word he has heard at our table. Here that exchange belongs in the room."

The readers do not translate every sentence twice in a fixed sequence. Instead, they alternate passages and discussion, keeping the story at the center while giving families different ways to engage with it.

"People join in through the picture, the movement or the sound," librarian Orla Reed said. "There is more than one way to follow what is happening."

The public reading begins with preparation out of view of the audience. Venn discusses the chosen picture book with the librarian before the session. Volunteers have asked for time to examine unfamiliar books in advance rather than being handed any title immediately before they read.

That preparation also establishes a common basis for the two readers. The library checks that both are working from the agreed story and that their explanations do not change its basic events. For the session featuring the picture book "The Tin Boat," the library arranged permission for the planned public reading under its program arrangements.

The grandparents contribute their reading voices and experience. Their role does not make them certified translators for every language task.

Questions sometimes extend beyond the book. Adults at some sessions ask about pronunciation or expressions used in families. The readers discuss those as particular uses, rather than presenting one answer as a rule for every speaker.

The format leaves room for the practical needs of families with children. Sessions last about 40 minutes, and families can step out and return when a child needs a break. A low display offers related books to borrow afterward through the library's ordinary borrowing process.

The reading corner shares space with the regular children's collection. After each session, the cushions are packed away. The library covers volunteers' local travel costs from the program budget.

The program's scope remains modest. The library has not supplied a study of children's vocabulary gains or a comparison with formal language teaching. What it offers is an opportunity for families to share a story in a setting where both languages are part of the activity.

The library plans to keep English and Arabic together through this term before considering other volunteer-led sessions. It has made no commitment to offer every requested language or move to a weekly schedule. For now, the grandparents' voices are part of a monthly gathering built around a book, its pictures and the discussion they prompt.
