# At Relay Yard, salvaged doors need records — and room to wait

Before a reclaimed door could leave Relay Yard for a builder’s project, employee Neri Havell had to match its tag to its dimensions and removal record. The check, recorded in a July yard note, captures a less visible part of the reclaimed-material business: Getting an item out of one building does not make it ready for the next.

Relay Yard buys or receives materials from authorized building strip-outs and sells reusable stock to builders and household customers. Inside its former seed warehouse, separate aisles hold measured items and stock still awaiting inspection. Between arrival and collection come measurements, listings, questions about suitability and, often, a wait.

The latest intake ledger tracks 160 accepted doors. Of those, 90 have sold, 30 are reserved and 40 remain available. The distinctions matter: A reservation is not a completed sale, and a canceled order can put a door back on the available list.

The yard’s standard reservation period is 14 days. After that, storage arrangements must be agreed separately — another step between choosing an item and putting it to use.

Owner Tavin Lorne says arriving materials and outgoing construction schedules rarely match exactly.

“The bargain has to wait somewhere between the building coming down and the next one being ready,” Lorne said.

That wait has a cost. Relay Yard pays for storage and handling even when donated material carries no purchase price. The business employs four people, including one who spends most working hours measuring, photographing and listing stock.

Covered storage is nearly full. A demolition contractor has offered another batch of doors, but the yard has not accepted it. As of Sept. 8, Lorne has no signed agreement to expand into the neighboring building.

For buyers, the appeal can begin with the price. A common interior door is listed at $40, compared with one local supplier’s $140 quote for a new door of similar size. But those figures do not establish a universal saving once a door is installed. Condition, hardware, refinishing and fitting costs differ.

Price and dimensions also do not answer every question about where an item belongs. The yard records known dimensions and origins, but it does not certify every salvaged product for structural or fire-rated use. Suitability is assessed for the proposed use, not inferred solely from age or appearance.

Architect Cera Mossen says specified performance records can be necessary before her clients can use an item for a particular purpose.

“A beam can look sound in the yard and still need evidence before it belongs in the design,” Mossen said.

Unidentified coatings and missing test records can limit where materials are offered. No chemical test results for those items were supplied in the reporting materials.

The business has not released a net-profit figure or a verified carbon-saving total for its stock. Its ledger offers a narrower measure: doors accepted, sold, reserved or still waiting.

Havell’s tag check sits within that practical work. A reclaimed door may have a buyer and an attractive price, but its path to another building still depends on matching the item to its records, establishing its suitability and arranging collection. Until then, it needs a place in the warehouse.
