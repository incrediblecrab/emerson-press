# What CRISPR can change — and where gene editing meets its limits

CRISPR gene editing gives researchers a way to target particular stretches of DNA and change how genes work. It has moved from a laboratory tool to a treatment approach for some blood disorders, while opening possibilities in agriculture, disease research and diagnostics.

But as of Feb. 14, 2025, its capabilities remain narrower than the idea of rewriting DNA might suggest. Making an edit is only part of the challenge. Getting the machinery into the right cells safely, controlling unintended changes and manufacturing personalized treatments remain significant obstacles.

## How does it work?

CRISPR-Cas9 was adapted from a bacterial immune system that cuts and destroys invading viral DNA. Researchers turned that defense mechanism into a two-part editing tool.

The first component is a customized guide RNA, which directs the tool to a particular DNA sequence. The second is Cas9, an enzyme that cuts both strands of DNA at that location.

The cell then tries to repair the break. One repair pathway can disable a gene. Another can insert new genetic information if researchers provide a template. The outcome therefore depends not just on the cut, but also on how the cell repairs it.

“CRISPR is not magic and it is not science fiction,” said Dr. Yusuf Abebe, a geneticist at Clearview Research Institute. “It is a very precise molecular scissors, and like scissors, it cuts where you point it — and can cut off-target if you point badly.”

Those unintended cuts are a documented limitation. Their frequency depends on the guide RNA’s design, the delivery method and how unique the targeted sequence is within the genome.

## What can it do for patients?

CRISPR therapies can work inside the body or on cells removed from a patient.

Editing inside the body, called in vivo editing, requires a delivery vehicle — typically a lipid nanoparticle or a modified virus — to carry the machinery into target cells.

With ex vivo editing, researchers remove cells, edit them in a laboratory and return them to the patient. This is more controllable because the edit can be verified before the cells are reinfused.

Two CRISPR-based therapies for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassemia received Food and Drug Administration approval in December 2023. Both use ex vivo editing of patients’ own blood-forming stem cells.

The approach does not make CRISPR a universal treatment. Abebe said it is most effective when one gene or a small number of genes is the causal target, rather than conditions controlled by dozens or hundreds of genes.

He said the biggest constraint on expanding treatment is delivering the tool safely and efficiently to tissues beyond the liver and blood. Personalized cell manufacturing also costs substantially more than producing a mass-market drug, contributing to high list prices.

## What else is possible?

A guide RNA can target any species with a compatible DNA sequence. Researchers have used CRISPR to engineer fungal disease-resistant crops, cattle with more muscle mass and mosquitoes unable to transmit malaria. None of those applications has been fully released into the U.S. environment.

Refinements called base editing and prime editing can change individual DNA letters without cutting both strands, reducing off-target effects in some applications. Researchers are also developing CRISPR-based diagnostics to detect specific viral sequences.

Other efforts remain preliminary. Animal experiments aimed at eliminating latent HIV from infected cells have shown early success; human trials are in early stages.

A separate boundary is germline editing: changes to sperm, eggs or embryos that pass to future generations. It is banned in most countries.

CRISPR’s practical promise is targeted, not limitless: changing particular genetic instructions where researchers can identify a useful target and deliver the tool safely.
