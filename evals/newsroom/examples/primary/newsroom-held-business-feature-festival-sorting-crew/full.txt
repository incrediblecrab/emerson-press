# Sorting Grey Orchard's Trash Reveals the Real Work Behind "Recycled"

By the time Cairn Sorting Services finished checking the material collected after the Grey Orchard fair, the numbers told a more complicated story than a simple recycling rate. Food residue clung to cups. Packaging labeled "biodegradable" sat outside what the local composter would actually take. And a crew of eight paid workers had to pull apart, piece by piece, what fairgoers had tossed together.

A cleanup note dated Monday, July 13, records what that crew found after the July 10-12 fair: 7.2 metric tons of material in all, with four tons sent out as residual waste, 1.8 tons accepted for recycling and 1.4 tons accepted for composting. Those figures describe what a processor agreed to take in, not a measured amount of finished recycled product or finished compost — an early step in a chain, not its end.

Crew leader Omi Farden said much of the sorting difficulty traces back to decisions made well before a visitor reached a bin.

"The person choosing the cup and the person emptying the bin are making parts of the same decision," Farden said.

That showed up directly in the composting stream. Some packaging marked biodegradable did not appear on the receiving composter's accepted-material list, meaning it could not go into that ton and a half regardless of what its label promised. Cairn sorted by the processor's actual rules rather than assuming every package carrying an environmental label belonged in the same bin.

Food vendor Ceri Hollen said switching to compostable containers is not simply a matter of willingness. Suppliers approved for fair use often sell only in bulk minimums, she said.

"I can buy the right container and still be left with a year's supply after one weekend," Hollen said.

The fair's reusable-cup program produced a cleaner result, at least on paper. Organizers issued 4,000 cups under a $2 return-deposit system and recorded 3,800 returned, a 95% return rate. But the 200 cups that did not come back are not confirmed as discarded — some may simply have been kept — and their eventual fate was not tracked. The cups that were returned still required washing, transport and storage before another event could reuse them, work that does not appear in a return-rate figure.

Money offers a similar caution. The organizer's waste contract combines a fixed service fee with charges tied to what processors actually take in, so a lighter load of residual waste this year would not by itself eliminate cleanup costs, according to the contract terms described in the cleanup record.

Attendance adds another limit on what the tonnage can prove. The fair recorded 12,000 entries over the three days, a count that includes people who came more than once. Without a count of distinct visitors, that figure cannot be used to calculate waste generated per person.

The record also does not support a comparison with how the fair handled waste, product mix or attendance in prior years — that data was not part of the reporting package reviewed for this account. Nor does anything in the available records support a claim that the fair achieved zero waste or an independently measured drop in emissions.

What the record does show is a supply chain running in both directions: purchasing choices made by organizers and vendors shape what a sorting crew can and cannot recover, and a sorting crew's daily work shapes what numbers organizers can later report. Farden's crew finished the post-fair job in one day. The fair's next set of purchasing decisions, still to come, will determine how much of that work has to be repeated.
