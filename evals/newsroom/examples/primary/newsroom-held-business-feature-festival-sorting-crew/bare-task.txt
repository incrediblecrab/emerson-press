**After the Fair: Eight Workers, 7.2 Tons and the Fine Print Behind Grey Orchard's Recycling Claims**

By [Staff Writer]

GREY ORCHARD — The morning after this year's Grey Orchard fair closed, the cleanup crew's job was only beginning.

According to a cleanup note dated Monday, July 13, Cairn Sorting Services checked material collected after the three-day fair, which ran July 10-12 on a former orchard field where organizers hire temporary stalls and barriers from several suppliers. The contractor assigned eight paid workers to the collection and sorting operation.

The tally: 7.2 metric tons of material, with 4 tons sent as residual waste, 1.8 tons accepted for recycling and 1.4 tons accepted for composting.

Those numbers describe what processors agreed to take in — not what ultimately became new products or finished compost. Material accepted by a recycler or composter is not a measured final output, and the reporting record does not track what happened after acceptance.

The gap between "accepted" and "recycled" showed up quickly on the sorting line. Some packaging labeled biodegradable did not appear on the receiving composter's accepted-material list, according to the contractor. Rather than assume every package carrying an environmental label belonged in the same stream, the crew followed that specific processor's rules — meaning some ostensibly compostable items were redirected.

Crew leader Omi Farden said the sorting problems often start well before a visitor drops something in a bin. Food residue and mixed materials meant workers had to physically separate items that attendees had placed together in the same container.

"The person choosing the cup and the person emptying the bin are making parts of the same decision," Farden said.

That decision chain extends to vendors. Food vendor Ceri Hollen said switching to more sortable or compostable packaging is harder than it sounds when approved suppliers sell only in bulk minimum quantities.

"I can buy the right container and still be left with a year's supply after one weekend," Hollen said.

One initiative did show a clean number. The fair issued 4,000 reusable cups under a $2 return-deposit program and recorded 3,800 returned — a 95% return rate. But those returns didn't end the cups' environmental accounting: they still required washing, transport and storage before they could be redeployed at another event. The fate of the 200 unreturned cups is unknown; some may simply have been kept by attendees, and their later disposal or reuse was not tracked.

Organizers' broader claims about the fair's environmental footprint also come with caveats. The waste-hauling contract carries a fixed service fee plus actual processor charges, meaning a lower residual-waste weight this year does not eliminate every cleanup cost. The fair recorded 12,000 entries over three days, but that figure includes repeat visits by the same people, so it cannot be used to calculate waste generated per distinct attendee without more detailed attendance data. And there is no complete comparison available with the previous fair's product mix, attendance or waste arrangements, making year-over-year claims of improvement difficult to verify from the current record.

Nothing in the available documentation supports an independently measured emissions reduction or a zero-waste designation for the event.

What the record does show is more modest: a paid crew making judgment calls, tonnage by tonnage, based on what individual processors will and won't take — work that happens largely out of view, long after the last cup is returned or left behind.
