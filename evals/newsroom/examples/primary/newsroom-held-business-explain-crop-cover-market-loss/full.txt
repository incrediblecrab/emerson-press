**Carrots Were Harvested. A Sale Wasn't. Why That Combination Left One Farm Without a Payout**

Fen Orchard Farm pulled 36 tons of carrots from its fields in July, more than enough to clear the threshold on its crop insurance policy. But a lost buyer still cost the farm money — and the policy still didn't pay.

The gap between those two facts explains a distinction that trips up growers more often than insurers publicize: a yield-based policy covers a shortfall in what came out of the ground, not a shortfall in what got sold.

Fen Orchard's policy, written by Field Lantern Mutual, sets an insured reference yield of 40 tons and a covered-yield threshold of 75%, or 30 tons. If the farm's harvest fell below 30 tons because of specified weather conditions, the policy would pay. It didn't fall that low. At 36 tons, the farm harvested six tons above the trigger point — even after a restaurant supplier that had been due to take ten tons scaled back its purchasing schedule shortly before collection.

Field Lantern says that cancellation is not, by itself, a covered event. The policy insures yield loss from specified weather causes. It is not revenue insurance, and it does not insure against a buyer's business decisions. Neri Selt, a representative for the mutual, put it plainly: "A policy has to name the event it is covering, not just the fact that a season was expensive." Selt's comment describes this policy, not the terms of every agricultural insurance product on the market.

There's no finding in the record that the restaurant supplier was insolvent — only that it reduced its order. Of the ten tons the buyer no longer wanted, Fen Orchard resold three tons, donated four to Cedar Spoon Kitchen Network for its meal operations, and composted three after it could not find another outlet. The other 26 harvested tons went to the farm's remaining customers as planned.

Grower Bela Orr said the work of growing the carrots didn't change just because part of the expected revenue did. "We had the carrots," Orr said. "What vanished was the place they were supposed to go." Planting, lifting and washing all happened regardless of where the crop ended up — which is the same reasoning behind the policy's design: it measures what left the field, not what arrived at a customer's dock.

The farm has not released a full accounting of what the lost sale actually cost it — resale income, the value of the donation, disposal, or savings on delivery it didn't have to make. Cedar Spoon's acceptance of the four donated tons did not include payment at the farm's original contract price, and there's no tax valuation or reimbursement tied to that donation in the record.

The episode has prompted a regional growers' association to start collecting members' accounts of canceled orders, with an eye toward shared cold storage or backup buyers. No such project has been funded, and organizers acknowledge that more storage capacity would not guarantee a later sale.

No court or regulatory finding addresses how Field Lantern handled the claim. Fen Orchard, despite its name, grows vegetables; the farm's name dates to an earlier fruit-growing operation on the same land.
