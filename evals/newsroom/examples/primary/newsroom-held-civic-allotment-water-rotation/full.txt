**Dovemere allotments rotate watering times as shared tank struggles to keep up**

DOVEMERE — The Dovemere Allotment Association is testing a new watering schedule this month, splitting its 60 beds into five rotating zones after concluding that its shared tank cannot refill fast enough to supply every plot at once.

The three-week trial began this week and covers the association's irrigation network only. It does not affect household drinking-water connections in the surrounding neighborhood, according to the association.

The site is organized into five zones of 12 beds each, a layout set when a central path replaced several smaller access tracks. Under the trial, each zone gets its own watering period within the posted daily window, and the order rotates weekly so no zone is permanently stuck with the earliest or latest slot.

The association's maintenance note points to simultaneous demand — not a confirmed leak or broken pump — as the immediate cause of the shortfall. Staff will track tank levels and any missed watering periods during the trial, though the association has not published a measured savings figure and says none is available yet.

Coordinator Lera Moss said the schedule is meant to spread out more than the water itself. "We are sharing the order of access as well as the water itself," Moss said.

The committee considered and rejected a first-arrival queue, concluding it would favor members able to wait at the shared taps. The rotation instead is based on which zone a plot sits in, not how long a member has belonged to the association, and it does not change bed allocations or give longer-tenured members priority within a zone.

Not every member finds the arrangement equally workable. Some who work early shifts said the earliest watering periods are harder to use than later ones, even though each slot runs the same length. Plot holder Taren Wick summed up the concern: "Equal minutes are not always equally usable minutes when people work different hours."

Members may ask another plot holder to attend during their assigned period, but the association says that does not create an extra slot — the watering window itself stays fixed. If a zone doesn't receive water during its period, members are asked to notify the coordinator so staff can tell whether the problem was access-related or the slot simply went unused.

A few things are unchanged. Rainwater collected in a member's own permitted containers falls outside the shared tank schedule and can be used anytime. The association also has not issued crop-specific watering guidance and does not claim the rotation will deliver an ideal amount for every crop. And the schedule applies only to the allotment site — it is not a watering restriction for private gardens elsewhere in town.

A larger storage tank has been proposed as a longer-term fix, but that project would need funding and site approval and is not part of the current trial.

Printed copies of the schedule are posted at both entrance gates, since some members do not receive the association's electronic notices. The committee said it will review tank records and members' scheduling concerns before deciding whether to continue, adjust or end the rotation after the trial period ends.
