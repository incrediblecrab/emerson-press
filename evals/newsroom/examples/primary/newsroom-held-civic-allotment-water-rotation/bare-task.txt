**Dovemere Allotments Move to Rotating Watering Zones Amid Tank Refill Strain**

*Three-week trial staggers access times as shared tank can't keep pace with simultaneous demand*

The Dovemere Allotment Association has begun a three-week trial of rotating watering periods after determining that its shared water tank refills too slowly to supply all five of the site's watering zones at once, officials said.

The 60-plot site is divided into five zones of 12 beds each, based on the layout of the irrigation network rather than plot holders' length of membership. Under the new schedule, each zone gets a separate period within the posted watering window, and the order — including which zone gets the earliest and latest slots — rotates weekly.

The tank serves only the allotments' irrigation system and has no connection to household drinking-water lines in the surrounding neighborhood, the association said.

According to a maintenance note from the association, the immediate problem is simultaneous demand across zones, not a confirmed leak or a broken pump. The current five-zone layout dates to an earlier site change, when a central path replaced several smaller access tracks.

Coordinator Lera Moss said the rotation is meant to spread not just the water itself but also the order in which members reach it. "We are sharing the order of access as well as the water itself," Moss said.

The committee considered and rejected a first-arrival queue system, concluding it would favor members who were able to wait at the shared taps rather than distributing access more evenly.

Under the new arrangement, a plot holder may ask another member to attend during their zone's assigned period, but doing so does not create an additional watering slot. Members are also asked to notify the coordinator if a zone fails to receive water at all, so staff can tell the difference between an access problem and a slot that simply went unused.

Not every member has found the rotation equally convenient. Some who work early shifts said the earliest watering periods are harder to use than later ones. Plot holder Taren Wick summed up the concern: "Equal minutes are not always equally usable minutes when people work different hours."

The association said the rotation does not change how beds are allocated and does not give longer-standing members priority over newer ones within the same zone. Rainwater collected in a member's own permitted containers remains outside the shared tank schedule and is unaffected by the trial.

The association has not issued crop-specific watering guidance and has not claimed the rotation will give every crop an ideal amount of water. Officials also said they have not attributed the supply strain to excessive use by any named member.

During the trial, the association will track tank levels and any missed supply periods, though it has not yet published a measured savings figure. A previously discussed option — installing a larger storage tank — would require funding and site approval and is not part of the current trial.

The committee said it will weigh the operating data collected during the trial, along with members' concerns about shift-work availability, before deciding what happens after the three weeks end.

Because some members do not receive the association's electronic notices, printed copies of the rotation schedule have been posted at both site gates.

The arrangement applies only to the allotment site's shared irrigation system and does not signal any new watering restrictions for private gardens elsewhere in town, the association said.
