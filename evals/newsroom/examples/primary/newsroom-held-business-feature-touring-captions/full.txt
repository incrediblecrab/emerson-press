# Taking captions on tour means finding a place for them in every hall

A changed pause in the script needed a mark before Ternlight Theater's next tour performance. In a July rehearsal note, caption operator Ceri Varden recorded the adjustment: The words were prepared, but their timing still needed attention.

Ternlight took its original play "Four Rooms for Rain" to four town halls rather than a permanent theater. Its caption displays traveled with sets normally designed to fit in one small truck. At each stop, the company had to check where the screens could go and whether audience members could follow both the text and the actors.

The tour's captioning work joined script preparation and live operation with decisions about seating, transport and setup. A grant covered the approved $9,000 captioning budget for this tour. As of Sept. 8, funding for future tours had not been secured, leaving the company with reusable equipment but continuing work to pay for.

Caption text was prepared from the approved script and advanced by a trained operator following the performance. That arrangement did not amount to an automatic guarantee that every unscripted line would immediately appear correctly.

"The text is prepared, but the timing still belongs to the people performing it tonight," Varden said.

The company built caption preparation into paid production work rather than asking local volunteers to transcribe each show on arrival. Its approved budget allocated $3,000 to reusable display equipment, $4,000 to text preparation and $2,000 to operator time. Final expenditure was not supplied.

Buying the displays addresses only part of the work. Later productions will still require preparation, rehearsal and an operator to follow the action.

The halls also required separate checks of screen position, stage sightlines and cable routes. According to access rehearsal notes, a front-row position that gave a clear view of the stage at one hall made the captions difficult to follow at another. A seat's proximity to the actors did not settle whether it worked for watching a captioned performance.

Neri Calven, a Deaf audience member, attended one performance after asking where the caption display would be placed.

"Being told captions exist is the first answer. Being able to see them and the actors is the next one," Calven wrote in a supplied comment.

Director Omi Braven said portable equipment broadens venue options but adds transport and setup tasks. Ternlight is collecting venue-specific feedback rather than declaring one screen arrangement suitable everywhere. Captioning also does not itself provide step-free entry, audio description or every other access service a hall might need.

The four performances recorded attendances of 80, 100, 60 and 120, totaling 360 admissions. Those admissions include repeat theatergoers; they do not establish how many people came specifically because captions were available. The reporting record contains no controlled attendance or revenue comparison showing that captioning paid for itself.

For another tour, the displays can travel again. The preparation and operating work will remain, along with the need to check each hall. And even with an approved script ready, a changed pause can still require a mark.
