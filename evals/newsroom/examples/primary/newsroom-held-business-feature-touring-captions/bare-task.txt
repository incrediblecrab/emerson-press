# Beyond the stage, Ternlight’s touring captions require their own rehearsal

In a July rehearsal note for Ternlight Theater’s four-town tour, caption operator Ceri Varden marked a changed pause in the script. The words had been prepared. The moment to put them before an audience still depended on the performance.

That adjustment was part of taking the company’s original play, “Four Rooms for Rain,” to four town halls rather than a permanent theater. Captions traveled with the production, but making them usable required more than unloading a display and turning it on.

“The text is prepared, but the timing still belongs to the people performing it tonight,” Varden said.

The caption text came from the approved script and was advanced by a trained operator following the live performance. It was not an automatic guarantee that every unscripted line would immediately appear correctly. Preparation established the text; rehearsal and operation connected it to what the actors were doing.

The work also changed with the room. Each hall required a separate check of screen position, stage sightlines and cable routes. Access rehearsal notes recorded that a front-row position offering a clear stage view at one hall made captions difficult to follow at another.

For Deaf audience member Neri Calven, who attended one performance after asking where the caption display would be placed, that distinction mattered.

“Being told captions exist is the first answer. Being able to see them and the actors is the next one,” Calven wrote in a supplied comment.

The company is collecting venue-specific feedback rather than declaring one screen arrangement suitable everywhere. The checks address a practical question for a touring production: not simply whether the text is available, but whether an audience member can follow it alongside the action.

The tour’s approved $9,000 captioning budget reflected both equipment and labor. It allocated $3,000 to reusable displays, $4,000 to text preparation and $2,000 to operator time. Final spending figures were not supplied.

Ternlight built caption preparation into paid production work instead of asking local volunteers to transcribe each show on arrival. Although the displays can be used again, later productions will still need preparation, rehearsal and an operator.

A grant was awarded to cover this tour’s captioning budget. As of Sept. 8, funding for future tours had not been secured.

Director Omi Braven says portable equipment broadens the company’s venue options while adding transport and setup tasks. Ternlight normally travels with sets designed to fit in one small truck; the caption displays shared that transport.

The four performances recorded attendance of 80, 100, 60 and 120, for 360 admissions. Those admissions included repeat theatergoers and do not establish how many people came specifically because captions were offered. Nor does the reporting record contain a controlled attendance or revenue comparison showing that captioning paid for itself.

Captions also do not, by themselves, provide step-free entry, audio description or every other access service a hall may need.

For Ternlight, the tour’s captioning work linked a reusable piece of equipment to tasks that had to be done anew: preparing the play’s text, following its changing pace and checking what audiences could see in each hall. Varden’s notation of a changed pause captured one part of that work. The screen could travel from town to town; its timing and placement still needed attention at every stop.
