# At Clay Window, pottery tests and broken pieces have more to teach

A beginner compares a dull green test tile with a glossy sample at the Clay Window community pottery studio while technician Hara Venn looks up the notes for their different firing batches. The pieces offer something a description alone cannot: two results to examine side by side.

That comparison is the point of the studio’s test-shard archive. Beside communal worktables, labeled glaze tests and selected broken pieces remain available for study rather than making way for finished pots alone. Ordinary repeated tests share space with striking failures, giving users more to consider than the most dramatic things that can happen to clay.

The archive began after instructors found themselves describing the same past experiments without being able to show them. Keeping examples gave those discussions a physical reference. Venn links each retained test to notes about its clay, glaze and firing batch when those details are known.

“The piece that went wrong can be the one people spend longest looking at, provided we know enough to ask a useful question,” Venn said.

Knowing enough does not mean knowing everything. Some older pieces have incomplete notes and are marked accordingly; missing firing information is not filled in with guesses. Even a fully labeled sample is a starting point for comparison and discussion, not a promise that following its record will produce an identical object.

For member Dera Cole, a cracked bowl serves as a reference for a later version, not as a finished exhibition piece. Its value lies partly in making a change visible.

“I remember being annoyed with it. Now I can point to the part I changed instead of pretending the next bowl arrived from nowhere,” Cole said.

Members may contribute tests or failed pieces, but the archive is selective. Venn chooses what can be stored and documented within the available shelf space. The studio neither keeps every member’s work nor requires anyone to leave a personal piece they would rather take home.

Several metal biscuit tins hold small fragments, with written indexes directing users to particular tests. Photographs are being added so records remain available when fragile pieces can no longer be handled. An image can preserve an appearance, but it cannot preserve the weight and texture users compare when holding a sample.

The shelves also have practical limits as teaching resources. Their labels are learning records, not certifications that a glaze or finished pot is suitable for every food or household use. Studying a tile does not qualify a visitor to operate a kiln; that work remains with designated trained staff following the studio’s procedures. There is no laboratory safety testing or evidence of measured improvement in every member’s work in the supplied record.

Access to the reference shelves is included for current workspace users, who pay the studio’s ordinary membership and class fees. A small public display about experimentation is planned, with member permission required for named personal work. As of Sept. 18, its date and final selection have not been announced.

For now, the archive’s role remains close to the worktables: keeping an earlier result available when someone wants to ask what changed, what is known and what might be worth comparing next.
