**Mariva's First Homegrown Vaccine Doses Signal a Break From Pandemic-Era Delays**

MARIVA CITY — Fatou Diouf spent six months qualifying a production line before her team released a single dose. In March, the work paid off: Mariva's Health Ministry received the country's first batch of domestically manufactured mRNA vaccines, 600,000 doses of a seasonal influenza shot produced under a technology transfer agreement with BioGate International.

Diouf, 31, a biomedical engineer who manages the facility's lipid nanoparticle formulation line, was among the first cohort trained there. She said she returned from a graduate program in Europe to take the job.

"People asked me why I came back," she said. "I came back because this is where the work matters. We built something. It works. That's the answer."

The doses, released after a three-week national quality assessment, mark the first output of a plant that Health Minister Dr. Amara Diallo has cast as a corrective to Mariva's experience during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the country of 28 million people received vaccines roughly 18 months after wealthier nations.

"Never again," Diallo said. "That is not a slogan. It is a policy."

The three-year negotiated agreement gives Mariva rights to produce BioGate's mRNA platform for domestic use and for export to four neighboring countries. The first production run included 80,000 doses for two of those countries, neither of which has its own mRNA manufacturing capacity, Diallo said.

BioGate's tech transfer director, Chen Wei, described the deal as the company's most extensive technology transfer with a low-income partner. "We transferred the process, the quality systems, and the training," Chen said. "Not just the recipe."

That distinction matters, according to a pharmaceutical industry analyst, who said most vaccine technology transfer deals stop short of including full process documentation and quality system transfer — leaving recipient facilities able to produce but not troubleshoot. Chen acknowledged the quality system transfer took longer and cost more than BioGate initially planned. "It was harder than we planned," he said. "We're better at it now. That's the honest answer." He said the experience will lead BioGate to build longer quality-support periods into future agreements from the start.

The $220 million facility, built with $160 million in multilateral development bank loans, employs 280 people. Seventy percent trained through a two-year program at Mariva's national health sciences university. It currently produces 12 million doses annually, and the Ministry aims to reach 25 million by 2027 — enough to meet Mariva's annual influenza vaccination target.

Diallo said the Ministry ultimately wants to use the platform for pathogens beyond influenza; the facility was designed to allow a sequence change within two to three weeks. The current run adapted a seasonal formulation from the WHO Global Influenza Surveillance Network within three weeks of receiving the recommendation.

Challenges remain. The facility cannot yet produce the ultra-cold storage equipment needed to keep doses at minus 20 degrees Celsius, relying instead on imported refrigeration units — a vulnerability Diallo acknowledged. Mariva has applied for World Health Organization prequalification, which would allow United Nations agencies to procure the doses for global distribution.

Dr. Ingrid Lindqvist, a global health researcher at the Nordic Health Institute, said the combination of sustained political commitment, multilateral financing and genuine technology transfer behind Mariva's plant is rarely achieved and merits study as a policy model.

Diallo said the goal is regional, not global, reach. "We are not trying to become a pharmaceutical exporter," she said. "We are trying to not be left behind again."

For Diouf, the stakes are personal as much as national. "If we do this right, it proves that this can be done here," she said. "If we fail, it proves something else. That weight is there every day."
