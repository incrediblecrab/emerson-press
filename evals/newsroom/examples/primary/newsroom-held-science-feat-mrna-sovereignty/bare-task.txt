**Mariva Produces First Homegrown mRNA Vaccine, Aiming to Break Cycle of Delay**

By [Staff Writer]

Mariva's Health Ministry has released 600,000 doses of a locally manufactured mRNA influenza vaccine for use, marking the first time the nation of 28 million has produced the technology domestically, officials said.

The doses, from the facility's first production run, cleared a three-week national quality assessment before release. They were made under a technology transfer agreement with BioGate International, a multinational vaccine manufacturer, that gives Mariva rights to produce BioGate's mRNA platform for national use and authorized export to four neighboring countries.

"During COVID, we received vaccines 18 months after high-income countries. Never again," Health Minister Dr. Amara Diallo said. "That is not a slogan. It is a policy."

The agreement, negotiated over three years, is the most comprehensive vaccine technology transfer BioGate has completed with a low-income partner, according to Chen Wei, the company's tech transfer director. "We transferred the process, the quality systems, and the training. Not just the recipe," Chen said.

Building the facility cost $220 million, with $160 million financed through multilateral development bank loans. It employs 280 people, 70% of whom trained through a two-year program at Mariva's national health sciences university.

Among them is Fatou Diouf, 31, a biomedical engineer who manages the facility's lipid nanoparticle formulation line. She said her team spent six months qualifying the process before producing the first release batch.

"People asked me why I came back from my graduate program in Europe," Diouf said. "I came back because this is where the work matters. We built something. It works. That's the answer."

The facility's current capacity is 12 million doses annually; the Ministry aims to reach 25 million by 2027, which officials say would cover Mariva's annual influenza vaccination target. The first run also yielded 80,000 doses for two neighboring countries that lack domestic mRNA manufacturing capacity, Diallo said.

Industry analysts note the deal's scope is unusual. Most vaccine technology transfer agreements do not include full process documentation and quality system transfer, one pharmaceutical industry analyst said, often leaving facilities able to produce but not troubleshoot.

Chen acknowledged the quality system transfer took longer and cost more than initially planned. "It was harder than we planned," he said. "We're better at it now. That's the honest answer." He said the experience has changed how BioGate structures future agreements, which will now include longer quality system support periods from the outset.

Diallo said the Ministry envisions using the platform beyond influenza; the facility was designed to allow sequence changes within two to three weeks to respond to other pathogens. The current run used a seasonal formulation from the WHO Global Influenza Surveillance Network, which Mariva adapted to its manufacturing platform within three weeks of receiving the recommendation.

Dr. Ingrid Lindqvist of the Nordic Health Institute, a global health researcher, said Mariva's combination of sustained political commitment, multilateral financing and genuine technology transfer is rarely achieved and should be studied as a policy template.

Challenges remain. The facility cannot yet produce the ultra-cold storage equipment needed to maintain the vaccine at minus 20 degrees Celsius, relying instead on imported refrigeration units — a supply chain vulnerability Diallo acknowledged. Mariva has applied for World Health Organization prequalification, which would allow United Nations health agencies to procure the doses for global distribution.

Diallo said the facility is meant to serve regional needs rather than compete globally. "We are not trying to become a pharmaceutical exporter," she said. "We are trying to not be left behind again."

For Diouf, the stakes are personal as well as national. "If we do this right, it proves that this can be done here," she said. "If we fail, it proves something else. That weight is there every day."
