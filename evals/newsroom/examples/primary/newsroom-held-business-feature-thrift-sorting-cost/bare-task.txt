Sorting Through Donations: Why Every Bag at Morrow Cupboard Means Work Before It Means Money

Morrow Cupboard, a nonprofit thrift shop that raises money for adult music-study bursaries, took in 1,200 donated items in April, according to the shop's intake ledger. Of those, 740 were priced for sale, 260 were accepted by a recycling partner, and 200 were sent for disposal.

The numbers do not translate directly into revenue. The 740 items priced for sale had not all sold by the end of April, so that figure is not a cash total, and the shop's published annual account shows an $18,000 transfer to its bursary fund only after operating expenses were subtracted.

That transfer funded six $3,000 bursaries — not a payment available to everyone taking local music classes, according to the account. The record contains no measured change in recipients' eventual earnings or musical careers.

Manager Cera Veylan said the gap between a donation arriving and a dollar reaching the bursary fund reflects the work in between. "The donation arrives once," she said. "We still have to sort it, find room for it and sometimes pay to take it away."

An April stock note illustrates the process: volunteers separating saleable jackets from a donation that also contained incomplete bedding sets and damaged clothing. Veylan said condition, completeness and seasonal demand all affect whether an item can be priced and put on display.

The shop pays rent, Veylan's wages, utilities and waste-handling charges even though it receives its stock without paying a purchase price for it, according to shop records.

Fourteen volunteers contribute varying amounts of time to the sorting work; the shop does not operate with fourteen full-time unpaid workers, according to the shop. Volunteer Omi Nelden said evaluating an unfamiliar item can take longer than hanging a familiar garment on a rack. "A bag at the door is a question about what is inside," Nelden said, "not a price tag we can already count."

The shop's recycling partner accepts specified textiles and does not guarantee that every item rejected by the shop can be recycled, according to the shop. No verified weight-based landfill diversion or carbon-saving estimate has been supplied for the April item counts.

In response to the volume and variety of donations, the shop has begun limiting drop-offs to staffed hours so volunteers can explain what it currently accepts, Veylan said. The change has not been evaluated for its effect on donations, disposal costs or shop income.

The board is considering adding storage space but has not signed a lease or approved additional paid hours, according to the shop.

Customers browsing the shop's racks can buy ordinary clothing without applying for financial assistance or disclosing household income; the bursary program and the retail floor operate as separate functions under the same roof.

The shop occupies a former music-supply store and still displays goods in the building's long glass-fronted cabinet, a holdover from its previous use.

For now, the shop's math runs in one direction: a donated jacket becomes a priced item only after volunteers judge it wearable and in season; a priced item becomes revenue only if it sells; and revenue becomes a bursary only after rent, wages, utilities and disposal costs are paid. Each step, Veylan said, takes time the shop does not always have before the next bag arrives at the door.
