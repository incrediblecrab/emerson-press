**At One Village Store, $35 of Credit Buys Time Between Payday and the Cupboard**

ALDER RILL — The ledger behind the counter at Alder Rill Village Stores does not look like much: 18 open household accounts, a combined $420 owed as of Aug. 31. But it sits at the center of a small, constant balancing act between helping neighbors get through the week and keeping the shelves stocked.

Owners Ceri and Olan Drel bought the shop in June 2025 and kept a small-credit practice the previous owner had run before them. The rule is simple: no household can owe more than $35 at a time, and the shop charges no interest. Customers agree on a repayment date — usually their next payday — rather than carrying an open-ended balance.

"It is a few days between the cupboard and payday, not a different kind of shopping list," said Nessa Vale, a customer whose weekly wages sometimes arrive after the delivery day for household items she needs.

The record does not show that every household using credit has the same income or the same reason for using it. What it shows is a system built to be small and temporary rather than open-ended.

Credit is limited under the shop's policy to ordinary food and household essentials. Two of the 18 accounts are currently blocked from taking on further credit while repayments are arranged; their holders are not identified in the shop's records.

The $420 outstanding is money owed to the store — not a measured loss, and not cash the Drels can use to pay their wholesaler. That distinction matters because of a separate deadline running in the background: under the shop's supplied wholesaler agreement, invoices must be paid within 21 calendar days of the invoice date, regardless of when households settle what they owe on the household ledger.

Ceri Drel keeps repayments recorded separately from new sales, she said, so that goods are not counted as sold twice once a customer pays off a balance.

"Being paid on Friday does not make Tuesday's milk a second sale," Drel said.

The Drels have not published customer names or individual balances on the shop noticeboard. They also have not supplied a full annual bad-debt figure, and there is no comparison in the record showing whether the credit practice helps or hurts the shop's overall profit.

The nearest full-service grocery store is nine miles from Alder Rill, though some households use delivery services instead of driving. For customers with or without a credit account, the shop also keeps a reduced-price shelf for near-date goods, open to anyone in the store.

The counter still holds a drawer left over from a village postal agency that closed before the Drels took over the shop — one more small trace of services the store has absorbed over the years without formally expanding into them.

For now, the Drels say they have no plans to change the practice into something larger. They have not proposed adding interest, raising the $35 limit, or turning the household ledger into anything resembling a bank.
