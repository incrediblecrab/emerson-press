Moorlace Broadband Awards First Fiber Contract, but 170 Properties Wait Past the River

Moorlace Broadband Cooperative signed its first fiber construction contract Thursday, sending crews to build service to 620 premises while leaving 170 properties beyond a river bridge outside the deal for now.

The cooperative awarded the $8.4 million phase to Pella Trenchworks. The work is financed by a $5 million rural-infrastructure grant and a $3.4 million cooperative loan. Construction is scheduled to begin Sept. 14, with the first connections expected in spring 2027.

The signed route reaches 240 businesses and 380 homes. An earlier proposed route had covered 790 premises, including 170 farms and homes on the far side of the Greyfin road bridge. Those properties were dropped from the contract because the bridge's owner has not approved attaching a fiber conduit to the span. The cooperative has asked for an engineering study of an alternative river crossing but does not yet have a construction price for one.

General Manager Daro Senn said the contract does not fix a timeline for every address.

"Signing the trenching contract gives us a route to build, not a date for every front door," Senn said.

Senn said actual service dates along the signed route will still depend on road-opening permits and property access, and that a property passed by the new cable is not automatically connected — an owner must order service and approve installation before a line is run to the building.

The cooperative's residential package is priced at $59 a month, with a $149 standard installation charge. A business package that includes priority fault response is priced separately at $95 a month.

For the properties beyond the bridge, the cooperative said members there keep their voting rights and will not be billed for broadband service before a connection is installed. The cooperative has 412 members, a figure that does not match the premises count in either route, since some members hold no property on the routes and some premises belong to non-members.

Farmer Yara Tern, whose land lies beyond the bridge, said she had recruited neighbors to join the cooperative before learning her own property was cut from the signed route.

"I helped sign people up for this, and I am still on the far side of the map," Tern said.

The gap has practical costs on that side of the river. Farm-equipment dealer Neri Voss, whose workshop sits on the contracted side, said several customers who need remote equipment diagnostics live beyond the bridge; without reliable uploads there, Voss currently carries large diagnostic files to a supplier on a portable drive.

The cooperative formed in 2022 after a group of small employers surveyed local problems sending payroll and design files over existing connections.

Existing wireless providers in the area will continue operating, and the new fiber contract does not require any resident to switch services.

The cooperative has not released performance tests on the unbuilt network or estimates of what households might save compared with current service. Whether the 170 premises beyond the bridge are added to a future phase will depend on the outcome of the requested crossing study, for which no cost or schedule has yet been set.
