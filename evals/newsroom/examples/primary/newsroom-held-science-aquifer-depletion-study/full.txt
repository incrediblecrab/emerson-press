**Federal survey: Key farm-belt aquifer depleting 40% faster than previous estimate**

A federal geological survey released Thursday found that the Meridian Plains Aquifer is losing water far faster than previously estimated, raising fresh doubts about the long-term future of irrigation across a four-state farming region.

The survey found the aquifer is declining at 1.54 meters per year, about 40% faster than the 1.1-meter annual rate the same federal agency estimated in 2015. The findings, based on data from 1,200 monitoring wells collected from 2015 to 2024, cover an aquifer that underlies 2.8 million acres of irrigated farmland and supplies drinking water to more than 400,000 rural residents.

Groundwater levels in the study area are already 18 meters below their 1990 baseline, according to the survey.

"The revised rate reflects both increased extraction and reduced natural recharge," said Dr. Sarah Kimani, the survey's lead hydrologist. "Some portions of the aquifer won't recover on any human timescale."

The aquifer's natural recharge rate — the speed at which precipitation replenishes it — averages 0.03 meters per year, less than 2% of the current extraction rate, the survey found. Irrigation accounts for 91% of extraction, with the remainder coming from municipal and residential use.

At the current depletion rate, the survey estimates that economically viable irrigation in the shallowest portions of the aquifer will end within 20 to 35 years in those zones. The survey did not estimate when the aquifer as a whole might be exhausted, noting that depletion is uneven across the formation.

The survey also found a 22% increase in the number of wells reporting declining yield between 2019 and 2024, compared with the previous five-year period.

Bruce Holt, president of the Meridian Plains Growers Association, said the survey overstates the extraction rate and called for an independent review of the monitoring well methodology. Holt did not dispute the overall direction of the trend but said the rate calculation relied on wells concentrated in heavily pumped areas, which he said could skew the estimate.

The stakes for the region's agricultural economy are substantial. The U.S. Department of Agriculture estimated that crops grown in the aquifer's service area produced $14.2 billion in farm sales in 2023.

Several states in the region have imposed extraction limits, but enforcement mechanisms vary and the limits are set by state water authorities rather than a coordinated federal program. Some economists have proposed tradeable water rights markets as a way to reduce total extraction, though no state in the region has adopted such a system.

Federal water assistance programs provide subsidized irrigation infrastructure; critics say the subsidies reduce farmers' financial incentive to conserve.

Some producers are already adjusting. Dryland farming — growing crops that require no irrigation — has expanded to roughly 8% of the study area over the past decade as some anticipate water scarcity. At least three cities in the region are conducting feasibility studies for supplemental water sources, including pipeline imports and wastewater recycling.

The aquifer was first systematically surveyed in the 1950s, when large-scale irrigation expanded into the region.

Kimani said the agency plans to publish zone-specific depletion maps in early 2025 to give state water planners more granular data for decisions about future use.
