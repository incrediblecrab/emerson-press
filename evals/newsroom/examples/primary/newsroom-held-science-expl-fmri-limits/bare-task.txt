# What an fMRI brain scan can — and cannot — tell us about the mind

A brain region “lighting up” on a scan may sound like a glimpse of a thought taking shape. What functional magnetic resonance imaging actually detects is less direct: changes in blood flow and oxygenation associated with neural activity.

That distinction helps explain both the power of fMRI and its limits. It can map where activity changes during a task, but it cannot, by itself, establish what someone is thinking or what causes a behavior.

## A blood signal, not a thought

Active neurons need more oxygen and draw increased blood flow. Functional MRI uses that relationship to track changes across the brain. Its measurement, known as the blood oxygen level dependent, or BOLD, signal, is a proxy for neural activity — not a direct recording of neurons’ electrical signals.

The difference matters for timing. Neurons fire on a scale of milliseconds, while the BOLD response unfolds over seconds. Functional MRI offers excellent spatial resolution, but it is less precise about timing than electroencephalography, or EEG.

Even its detailed maps do not show individual neurons. A typical voxel, the smallest imaging unit, covers approximately 1 to 3 cubic millimeters of tissue and contains millions of neurons.

“When someone says ‘the brain region X lit up,’ what they mean is the BOLD signal in that region increased under those conditions,” said Yuna Breckinridge, a cognitive neuroscientist at Westfield University. “That is not the same as saying X causes or produces the behavior.”

## Activity does not establish cause

A scan can show an association between activity in a region and performance on a task. It cannot alone demonstrate that the activity causes that performance.

Nor can researchers reliably work backward from an active region to a single mental process. Many regions participate in multiple tasks. This problem, called reverse inference, limits what a scan can reveal about the mind.

Other methods can help. Transcranial magnetic stimulation can temporarily disrupt function in specific brain regions, supplying causal evidence to complement fMRI’s correlations.

## Results depend on research practices

Small samples have been a systematic weakness. Many classic fMRI studies involved fewer than 30 participants, leaving results vulnerable to false positives. A 2016 analysis found a coding error in standard processing software that inflated false-positive rates; the paper estimated that up to 40,000 published studies were affected.

Head movement also creates misleading features in the data. Correction software can reduce, but not eliminate, that problem. And lying still inside a noisy, confined scanner may alter the social or emotional behaviors researchers want to study.

Breckinridge said the field has substantially improved since 2016 through larger samples enabled by shared data, preregistration of hypotheses and replication across research sites. The Human Connectome Project’s structural and functional connectivity maps of 1,200 healthy adult brains have also improved understanding of individual variability.

Comparisons between sexes, ethnicities or clinical populations still require careful controls for factors such as education, socioeconomic status and scanner type.

## Useful maps, limited individual verdicts

Resting-state fMRI, performed without a specific task, has identified networks of regions that consistently activate together. Researchers are still establishing their functional significance.

Machine learning tools have found patterns that distinguish groups, but their reliability for individual clinical diagnoses remains limited, and such uses are not generally approved. Using fMRI as legal evidence of deception, mental state or intent is controversial and is not accepted as reliable by most forensic neuroscientists.

Breckinridge’s assessment is that fMRI remains a powerful tool for mapping functional brain organization at the population level. It is not a mind-reading technology — and its findings should not be interpreted as though it were.
