# Disputed mangrove credits put shipping company's carbon claims under scrutiny

A graphic in Lighthouse Shipping Group's 2022 annual report labeled the maritime company "carbon neutral." Now that designation has been removed from its website, pending a review of carbon credits it bought to support its emissions claims.

Lighthouse purchased 420,000 credits from the Coastal Mangrove Carbon Alliance for its 2022 and 2023 carbon neutrality claims. But an audit by the VCMIO found that the mangrove forests the program claimed to protect in Tambela Province had already been legally protected under national conservation law since 2011.

The finding has put the company's purchasing checks under scrutiny alongside the credits themselves. Lighthouse says it relied on its certification vendor. An investigative journalism team's review of the company's original due diligence records found no evidence it asked whether the forests were independently protected before entering the program.

Between 2018 and 2024, the alliance issued 1.8 million credits representing claimed carbon sequestration in the mangroves. The credits sold for $18 to $45 each, generating an estimated $54 million in revenue.

The dispute concerns what that money bought. Ruth Nkosi, a carbon market transparency advocate at the International Carbon Accountability Center, said the underlying science of mangrove carbon sequestration is sound. Mangroves store carbon, she said; what she described as fraud lay in the "additionality" claim, the assertion that the program newly provided protection.

Lighthouse sustainability director Marcus Ferreira said the company conducted due diligence through its certification vendor and was not told about the forests' existing legal protection.

"We paid for verified carbon reduction. We now know the verification was inadequate. We're responsible for the money we spent. We are not responsible for a fraud we had no way to detect," Ferreira said.

The records review raises a question about that defense: whether Lighthouse sought information that could have challenged the program's claim before buying its credits.

Nkosi said the case reflects a broader weakness in how buyers assess credits.

"The buyers' defense is 'we were defrauded.' But due diligence in this market has been aspirational, not operational. Most buyers never asked the hard questions," she said.

She also pointed to the financial relationship between the entities paid to certify credits and the sellers, rather than the buyers.

The alliance's founder, Paulo Avila, has not responded to media inquiries since the audit's release. His company's website went offline in July.

Lighthouse has committed to buying replacement credits from a VCMIO-verified program and not counting the disputed credits toward its emissions claims. Ferreira said an internal review of all its credit purchases began in July and the company will buy no new voluntary credits until that review is complete.

He declined to quantify the potential cost of replacing the 420,000 credits at current market prices. Lighthouse has not disclosed whether it will seek legal recourse.

Four other companies that bought alliance credits have disputed the audit finding. Two have hired independent auditors to review their purchases.

The VCMIO says buyers able to demonstrate good faith due diligence may receive a grace period to replace disputed credits, but it has not published the standard for good faith. It is examining at least six similar mangrove and forest protection programs in Tambela Province. Avila's was the largest.

The VCMIO says mandatory standards due in early 2026 will require developers to document what protection would have existed without their programs, rather than simply asserting that protection is new.

For now, Lighthouse's 2022 annual report retains a record of the claim the company has taken off its website: "carbon neutral."
