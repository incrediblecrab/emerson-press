# From farm surplus to pantry shelf, Fellwater volunteers coordinate the details

An offer of boxed zucchini is on dispatcher Vera Noll's list, but a pantry with storage nearly full can take only part of the load. Before a driver sets out, the amount available has to be matched with the amount a pantry can receive.

Noll begins a delivery day by comparing farm offers with the quantities and delivery times pantries say they can accept. A box count is a starting point, not a delivery plan.

"The offer arrives as a box count. We still have to find the time, the vehicle and the shelf where those boxes can land," Noll said.

That matching work is the purpose of Fellwater Produce Link, whose volunteers coordinate short-notice deliveries of already harvested farm surplus to small community pantries around Fellwater. The group arranges transport rather than sending volunteers into fields to harvest crops. Its work depends on finding a workable match among a farm's offer, an available vehicle and a pantry's capacity.

The produce available can change before collection is confirmed. Grower Elin Marr said ordinary sales and harvesting decisions affect what a farm can offer.

"I can offer what is left when the orders are settled. I cannot turn that into the same donation every Thursday," Marr said.

The group therefore does not promise pantries a fixed weekly quantity or the same products throughout the season. An offer gives the dispatcher something to work with, but it does not establish what will ultimately be collected or where every box will go.

Volunteer drivers use vehicles agreed through the group's transport arrangements. They do not collect any offered load without coordination. A local hauler makes a refrigerated vehicle available on agreed days, but the volunteers cannot call on it whenever they need it.

When a load requires conditions the group cannot provide, it is declined or referred through another suitable arrangement. Avoiding a refusal is not, by itself, a reason to accept the food. The receiving pantries retain responsibility for deciding what to accept and how to distribute it under their own operating procedures.

The group records both what was accepted at collection and what each pantry actually took. Those are separate points in the delivery process: Produce available at a farm is not automatically a usable donation at every pantry.

A missed collection can affect several recipients. Farms and pantries are asked to confirm changes before a vehicle is dispatched, and Noll keeps paper contact and route sheets available for drivers who cannot receive an electronic update on the road.

A small community fund reimburses travel costs. The drivers are volunteers, not unpaid farm employees, and the fund neither pays farmers a market price for donations nor guarantees to buy unsold crops.

As of Sept. 18, the group is seeking more regular transport availability, but no additional vehicle commitment has been announced.

At the end of a delivery day, the volunteers hold a short review of practical mismatches, including excess packaging and delivery times that did not suit the recipients. The questions return to the details Noll starts with: how much a pantry can take, when it can receive it and whether there is room for the boxes.
