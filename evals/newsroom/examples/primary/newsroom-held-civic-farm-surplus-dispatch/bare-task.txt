# For Fellwater volunteers, surplus produce needs more than a willing driver

Vera Noll begins a delivery day by comparing what farms have to offer with what small community pantries around Fellwater have room to receive — and when they can receive it.

Among the offers are boxed zucchini. But a pantry with storage nearly full can take only part of the load. Before a driver sets out, Noll has to match available food with available space.

That is the work of Fellwater Produce Link, a volunteer transport and matching operation coordinating short-notice deliveries of already harvested farm surplus. Its volunteers do not go into fields to harvest crops. They work out how produce offered by participating growers can reach pantries able to accept it.

“The offer arrives as a box count. We still have to find the time, the vehicle and the shelf where those boxes can land,” Noll said.

The distinction matters: Produce available at a farm is not automatically a usable donation for every pantry. Storage, delivery schedules and transport conditions all shape what the group can move. An offer is the start of that coordination, not a guarantee that the whole load will be collected or accepted by a particular recipient.

Grower Elin Marr said the amount available can change with ordinary sales and harvesting decisions before a collection is confirmed.

“I can offer what is left when the orders are settled. I cannot turn that into the same donation every Thursday,” Marr said.

The group accordingly does not promise pantries a fixed weekly quantity or the same products throughout the season. Each delivery requires matching the current offer with what recipients say they can take.

Volunteer drivers use vehicles agreed through the group’s transport arrangements. A local trucking operator makes a refrigerated vehicle available on agreed days, but volunteers do not have permanent access to it. When a load requires conditions the group cannot provide, it is declined or referred through another suitable arrangement rather than accepted simply to avoid turning food away.

The pantries retain responsibility for deciding what to accept and how to distribute it under their own operating procedures. Produce Link records what was accepted at collection and what each pantry actually took.

Coordination continues beyond the initial match. Because a missed collection can affect several recipients, the group asks farms and pantries to confirm changes before a vehicle is dispatched. Noll keeps paper contact and route sheets available when a driver cannot receive an electronic update on the road.

A small community fund reimburses travel costs. The drivers are volunteers, not unpaid farm employees, and the fund neither pays farmers a market price for donated produce nor guarantees to buy unsold crops.

At the end of the day, volunteers hold short reviews of practical mismatches, including excess packaging and delivery times that did not suit recipients. Those discussions keep attention on the details that determine whether an offered donation can fit a pantry’s operations.

As of Sept. 18, the group is seeking more regular transport availability, but no additional vehicle commitment has been announced. For now, each collection still depends on bringing together a changing farm offer, an agreed vehicle and a pantry ready to take the boxes.
