Wind Farm's Undersea Cables Set to Cut Through Maine Lobsterman's Best Grounds

EASTPORT SOUND — When construction crews begin work on Atlantic Ridge Wind's 180-turbine array in September, the cables they bury on the seafloor will run straight through the most productive lobster grounds in the region — and through the heart of Sam Callahan's livelihood.

Callahan, 52, has fished these waters for 28 years. He says the 14-month cable installation phase will close 22,000 acres of his traditional fishing territory.

"They're building something that'll be there for decades," Callahan said. "We're fishing something that's been here since before any of us were born. Those things don't automatically coexist."

Atlantic Ridge Wind hired marine liaison Divya Singh to coordinate with the fishing fleet, and Singh said the company has redesigned the cable route three times based on fishermen's input.

"The route isn't perfect," Singh said. "But it avoids the peak season grounds between April and July. What's left is a real compromise. It doesn't fully satisfy anyone."

Callahan, who participated in the consultations, disputes that characterization. His GPS vessel tracking data, he said, shows the avoided grounds contain his most productive traps — meaning the areas still affected are the ones that matter most to his income.

The project's environmental review found no evidence that operational turbines harm lobster populations, and studies from European offshore wind sites show lobster populations stable or increasing around turbine foundations, though those studies were conducted in different water conditions.

Callahan said that's not his concern. He worries about construction disturbance — specifically, high-pressure jetting used to bury cables, which he said disrupts the seabed and interferes with lobster molting cycles.

Atlantic Ridge has offered a $4.2 million compensation fund for fishermen affected during construction. Three Eastport Sound fishing families have accepted the terms. Callahan and two others have not, saying the fund's methodology underestimates their losses.

"My trap license is worth $280,000," Callahan said. "The process didn't put a dollar figure on that." He argues the deeper problem is a regulatory system that treats fishing grounds as an environmental consideration rather than an economic asset.

The regional marine fisheries council has no formal role in offshore wind siting decisions. It can submit comments but cannot veto or condition approvals.

A federal coastal fisheries attorney said the legal standard for compensating displaced commercial fishermen remains unclear and varies by circuit court. Two pending cases in other regions could establish precedent.

Singh said the company remains open to further negotiation but cannot delay construction past August without losing its installation vessel window — a delay that would cost $18 million in penalties.

For the operational phase, Singh said Atlantic Ridge intends to pursue a long-term agreement allowing trap fishing between turbine foundations. Callahan said he won't commit to that until he sees how construction plays out.

European operators who allow fishing between turbines say rebuilding trust takes years. One Norwegian case study found 80% of displaced fishermen returned to their grounds within four years.

Atlantic Ridge is also funding a two-year seabed monitoring study tracking lobster density before, during and after construction, with results to be published. Callahan called the study welcome but too late to affect the cable route.

Singh and Callahan first met face to face in April, after 18 months of correspondence conducted through lawyers and industry liaisons. Callahan said the meeting changed nothing substantively but felt like the conversation they should have had two years earlier.

Despite the dispute, Callahan said he isn't opposed to offshore wind itself.

"I'm not against clean energy," he said. "I'm against doing it in a way that destroys something else without accounting for it properly."
