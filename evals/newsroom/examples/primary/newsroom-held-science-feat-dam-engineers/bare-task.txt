**Aging Dam Serving 220,000 Faces Funding Gap as Deterioration Accelerates, Engineers Say**

RIDGELINE — The concrete face of the Colton River Dam is losing 2.8 millimeters a year, according to internal estimates from the engineer tasked with monitoring it, a rate that has accelerated since 2018 and left the structure with less time than originally projected before it needs major repairs.

The dam, built in 1961, supplies water to 220,000 people in the Ridgeline region. Federal dam safety engineers gave it a Level 2 structural concern designation in 2023, but the Ridgeline Water District has not secured a fully funded remediation plan.

Leah Petrov, 41, a dam safety engineer with the district, has spent three years documenting deterioration in the dam's upstream face. She said an alkali-silica reaction in the concrete — a chemical process that causes expansion and cracking — has sped up in recent years, coinciding with warmer average summer temperatures.

"We're seeing expansion rates we didn't see in the first two decades of monitoring," Petrov said. "We have less time than the original projections said."

Her team's internal estimate, not yet formally submitted to federal regulators, is that the dam could be maintained safely for another 12 to 15 years under an accelerated repair program — or six to eight years without one.

The district has applied for $180 million in federal dam rehabilitation funding. The federal program received $300 million nationally this year for a priority list of more than 60 structures, and Colton is not yet in the top tier. In August, the district's board voted to prioritize the funding application and explore bonding options if federal money doesn't arrive in time.

"We are buying time," Petrov said. "We know that. It's not a comfortable thing to know."

Bernard Cho, 77, one of the dam's original design engineers, has followed its condition since retiring, describing his feelings as professional regret. At Petrov's request, he reviewed her monitoring reports and said the data was consistent with what he would have predicted — and worse than he had hoped.

"We designed that dam to last 50 years with proper maintenance," Cho said. "It's lasted 63, and the maintenance has been adequate but not comprehensive. I don't know whether to take credit for the engineering or responsibility for what comes next."

Cho said he wrote to the district twice in the past decade offering his knowledge of the original design drawings but received no response until Petrov contacted him directly. Petrov said his records included detail on original reinforcement spacing that updated drawings did not capture. "He knew things about this dam that weren't in the files," she said. "That matters."

Federal records show the dam's downstream inundation zone includes 38,000 residents in three communities. Those communities have no designated emergency shelters within their boundaries; residents would need to travel 14 to 22 miles to reach designated safe zones. The district's emergency action plan, last updated in 2019, covers evacuation routes for the area, but Petrov said the community notification system it relies on has not been tested recently.

Petrov said she has not shared the full scope of her monitoring data publicly, citing concerns about property values and community alarm — a decision she called difficult. Dr. Grace Mbeki, a water policy researcher at Ridgeline University, said that tension is common for infrastructure carrying slow-developing risks. Transparency is required under the district's own public information charter, she said, but is constrained in practice by who controls release of raw monitoring data.

For Petrov, the hardest part is not measuring the risk but deciding what to do about it. "I can tell you the concrete is losing 2.8 millimeters a year," she said. "I cannot tell you who decides when that number becomes an emergency."

Cho still visits the dam once a year. "I like to see it holding," he said. "This year it was holding. Next year, I expect it will still be holding. The year after, I don't know."

"In 1961, we thought we were building something permanent," he added. "We weren't, of course. Nothing is. But we built it to last long enough that someone else would have to figure out what to do with it."
