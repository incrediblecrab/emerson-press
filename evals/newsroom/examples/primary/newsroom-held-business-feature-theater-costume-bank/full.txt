# At Aster Fold, shared costumes bring lower fees and fitting-room trade-offs

Omi Savren pinned a temporary hem while a drama teacher compared the garment with a production's color plan. The moment, recorded in a June costume-room note, captured two parts of borrowing a stage wardrobe: making the clothing fit and deciding whether it fits the show.

At Aster Fold, a costume bank serving 14 member schools from a rented room in an arts center, those decisions are part of the practical work behind sharing garments. Schools gain access to clothing without having to supply every piece themselves, but their productions must work around availability, alteration rules and the next borrower's schedule.

The financial terms are straightforward: Each school pays $180 a year, plus a separate $2 cleaning contribution for each garment returned. What that arrangement saves is harder to establish. The bank has not measured a uniform per-school saving because schools would otherwise buy, hire, make or reuse different items.

Drama teacher Nera Vossel encountered the limits of a shared wardrobe when coats requested for a production were already reserved by another school. Vossel shifted the production's color choices.

"Borrowing changes the budget, but it also asks the production to work with what is actually on the rail," Vossel said.

Membership permits schools to request garments subject to availability. It does not guarantee a full matching wardrobe for every cast. The reservation ledger shows that popular sizes and neutral coats are booked earlier than unusual costume pieces, making the availability of ordinary clothing a consideration in production planning.

The service recorded 1,200 garment checkouts last year. That figure includes repeated loans of the same clothing; it is neither an inventory of 1,200 distinct costumes nor a count of student performers.

Repeated lending also makes the work between productions important. Schools may make reversible alterations, but they must seek approval before cutting or permanently changing a garment. A late return can shorten the next school's fitting period even when the clothing comes back undamaged.

Savren is paid for eight hours a week. Volunteers help sort returns and perform simple repairs, work that continues after a school's use of the garments ends.

The membership fees would generate $2,520 annually if all 14 current memberships were paid. Cleaning contributions bring in separate receipts, but the supplied figures include no annual return count or total cleaning receipts. Membership and cleaning income alone do not cover all staffing and room costs. Arts-center support and donations help sustain the service.

Donated clothing also requires decisions about labor. Some garments need more alteration or cleaning than their likely future use justifies, and Aster Fold does not accept every donation.

"A free coat can still arrive with an expensive amount of work inside it," Savren said.

The collection began in 2019 with costumes from a dissolved amateur theater company. As of Sept. 8, a proposal to increase Savren's paid hours remains unfunded, and the service cannot promise to add more member schools.

For the schools already borrowing, the work includes checking reservations, adjusting production choices and fitting garments without permanently changing them. In the June costume-room note, the hem was temporary, and the teacher was still comparing the garment with the show's colors.
