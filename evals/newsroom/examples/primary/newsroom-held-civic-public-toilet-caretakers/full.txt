# Behind Norbridge's public toilet doors, caretakers keep the day moving

Sena Vell begins her shift at Norbridge's market toilets by checking supplies, testing the doors and checking the notice showing that day's opening hours. Each check concerns something a visitor may need to rely on as soon as they arrive.

"A missing roll or a door that will not close is a small job on a list and a very large problem for the person who has just arrived," Vell said.

During a routine cleaning closure, visitors wait while one section is briefly shut. Another section remains available. Explaining that distinction is part of the caretakers' work, alongside cleaning and keeping supplies stocked.

Through the busy season, Norbridge's public-facilities team maintains three staffed toilet sites around the market, riverside park and bus interchange. Their work keeps facilities available to people who do not have to make a purchase at a nearby business to use them. Whether a site is open, and whether visitors can find that information, can shape an ordinary trip into town.

Resident Elra Moss uses the team's location and opening-hours sheet to plan her shopping trips. An unexpected closure, she said, can change how long she stays.

"I plan the ordinary things around knowing there will be somewhere open. I am not the only person carrying that plan in my head," Moss said.

The facilities team distributes the sheet through the visitor office and community centers. Each site's hours are tied to its staffing arrangements; visitors cannot assume the three sites share a schedule.

The caretakers are paid employees of the city's contracted facilities provider. Their contract includes regular public-facing service as well as cleaning, including telling visitors which section remains open when another is unavailable.

At the market site, visitor flows differ between market and nonmarket days, affecting when staff can fit routine work around users. A brief closure for cleaning is recorded separately from a fault that puts an entire site out of service. Both may interrupt someone's plans, but they describe different conditions.

A small storeroom holds spare signs so staff can identify a particular closure instead of using the same vague notice for every problem. The notices give visitors information about what is unavailable, while caretakers can explain the remaining options.

Damaged fittings go into a maintenance reporting system. A separate contractor handles larger repairs, so logging a fault is one step in the process, not confirmation that the work is finished. The team also records supply use and closures to help plan its work.

Requests for longer access remain unresolved. As of Sept. 18, the city had received requests for later opening near the bus interchange, but no extra staffing hours had been approved. Extending service at one site would require a budget and roster change rather than simply leaving a staffed building unlocked.

For now, the published schedules tell visitors when they can expect access. At the market site, that information is among the things Vell checks at the start of her shift, along with the supplies and the doors.
