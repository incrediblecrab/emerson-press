# Behind Norbridge's public toilets, caretakers keep a busy season moving

NORBRIDGE — Sena Vell begins her shift at the market's public toilets with a check of supplies, doors and the notice listing the day's opening hours. Each is a small part of keeping the site usable. For someone arriving at the door, any one of them can matter immediately.

“A missing roll or a door that will not close is a small job on a list and a very large problem for the person who has just arrived,” Vell said.

Vell is among the caretakers keeping Norbridge's three staffed public toilet sites operating through the busy season. Around the market, riverside park and bus interchange, their work combines cleaning with checks, maintenance reports and explanations to visitors.

They are paid employees of the city's contracted facilities provider, not volunteers dependent on donations. Their contract includes regular public-facing service as well as cleaning — including explaining which section remains available when another is closed.

At the market site, visitors wait while one section is briefly shut for routine cleaning. Another section remains available. That temporary interruption is recorded separately from a fault that puts an entire site out of service, a distinction that matters both to the people arriving and to the team planning its work.

A small storeroom holds spare signs so staff can identify a specific closure rather than use the same vague notice for every problem. The task is not only to clean the facilities, but also to make clear what people can still use.

The timing of that work varies. Market days bring different visitor flows than nonmarket days, affecting when caretakers can fit routine tasks around users. The team records supply use and closures to help plan the work, though those records do not provide a count of unique people served.

For resident Elra Moss, reliable information about the toilets helps shape an ordinary shopping trip. She uses the team's location and opening-hours sheet, distributed through the visitor office and community centers. An unexpected closure, she says, can change how long she stays in town.

“I plan the ordinary things around knowing there will be somewhere open. I am not the only person carrying that plan in my head,” Moss said.

The public can use the facilities without buying anything at a nearby business. But access depends on the hour: Each site has opening times tied to its staffing arrangements, rather than round-the-clock service.

As of Sept. 18, the city has received requests for later opening near the bus interchange, but no additional staffing hours have been approved. Extending service would require changes to the budget and roster, not simply leaving a staffed building unlocked.

Repairs involve another division of responsibility. Caretakers report damaged fittings through a maintenance system, while a separate contractor handles larger repairs. A logged report marks a problem for attention; it does not mean the repair is finished.

The sites also reflect work undertaken over time. At the riverside toilets, an older tiled entrance remains after an interior refurbishment funded in an earlier budget year.

For the caretakers, keeping those facilities operating means attending to both the building and the information at its door: supplies checked, faults reported, hours displayed and visitors told where they can go while cleaning continues.
