**Prospect Valley's wells are running dry, and no one's water is safe**

The pump ran dry on a Tuesday morning while Nkechi Okonkwo was washing dishes. There was no warning, no gradual decline she could point to afterward. One day the well that had served her family's Prospect Valley home for 31 years simply stopped.

"I had to ask a neighbor to bring water in a tank," Okonkwo, 58, said. "We have a house and a yard and we're living like it's 1890. You don't expect that in this county."

Her well drew from a stretch of the Prairie Basin Aquifer where the water table has dropped 9 meters in eight years. It failed in June 2024, and it was not alone.

Of the 340 domestic wells registered in the county that draw from the Prairie Basin, water district records show 28 reported yield problems or outright failure this year. In 2019, four did.

The failures are not scattered evenly across the aquifer. Water district engineer Mark Dahl said they are concentrated in wells drawing from the upper 40 meters, where the water table has fallen fastest.

"These aren't agricultural wells," Dahl said. "These are people's drinking water. When they fail, there is no backup. They were never designed to have a backup."

A new, deeper well costs $18,000 to $35,000. Okonkwo received a $12,000 county emergency assistance grant, but said the remaining gap is beyond what she and her husband can cover on short notice. The county's annual well-assistance fund totals $1.4 million; it has received 47 applications this year and can fund roughly 20 at average grant levels. Okonkwo applied in August and, as of December, has received no decision. The county says applications are processed in order of submission, though some require additional documentation before they can move forward.

Three of her neighbors, all with shallow wells like hers, have already drilled deeper and recovered water. One told Okonkwo her total cost was $29,000 and the work took seven weeks.

Dahl said the deeper zones of the aquifer are declining too, just more slowly, and that without reductions in agricultural extraction, they will eventually follow the shallow zones downward. The dominant users of the aquifer are irrigated corn farms. A 2023 state-commissioned study found that cutting irrigated corn acreage by 20% would meaningfully slow the aquifer's decline. No such reduction program exists. The state legislature has twice declined to expand the water district's authority to regulate domestic well zones, and Dahl said the district's power is currently limited to distributing surface water and helping fund repairs — it cannot regulate groundwater extraction without further legislative authorization.

A state water policy attorney said domestic well users in most western states are the least protected class of water right holders, with no priority over agricultural or municipal users when supplies run short.

Okonkwo has taken her case to county water board meetings. "They say 'complex,'" she said. "I say I have no water. Those are not the same conversation."

Dahl called this year's 28 failures a turning point. Before 2024, he said, well failures were isolated enough to handle case by case. At the current rate, he said, the problem will require a systemic response. A hydrologist under contract with the county projects that, absent a change in policy, domestic well failures in the basin could reach 60 to 80 a year by 2027.

Okonkwo said she still believes the county is trying. "They're trying," she said. "They just don't have what it takes to actually fix it."

She and her husband have owned their house for 30 years. Lately, she said, she has been thinking about leaving.

"We own this house," she said. "We've been here thirty years. But a house without water is not a home."
