# More automation, new roles, fewer workers at North Loom warehouse

North Loom Fulfillment has added maintenance and returns-processing jobs while shrinking its permanent workforce at its Fenstone warehouse, illustrating why new job titles and recruitment advertisements do not necessarily mean an employer is growing.

The warehouse installed 17 automated carrying carts during the first half of 2026. Permanent staffing fell from 96 employees before the transition to 94 at the end of July. As of Aug. 10, its careers page also advertises two seasonal returns roles, but those openings are not two confirmed additions to permanent staffing.

The explanation lies in three different counts: positions created or eliminated, people entering or leaving the company, and workers moving between jobs.

### How can new jobs coexist with a smaller workforce?

North Loom did not refill 12 picking positions left vacant by departures. It added four maintenance positions and six returns-processing positions — 10 new posts against 12 eliminated ones, a net reduction of two.

The movement of employees is a separate calculation. The company's payroll reconciliation records 12 departures and 10 external hires, bringing the workforce from 96 to 94. Five of those hires filled posts vacated by internal transfers, rather than any of the 12 eliminated picking positions.

An employee moving into a new role does not, by itself, increase headcount. Hiring someone to fill the job that employee leaves can add a person without creating another position.

“Counting job titles alone misses the work that moved between them,” operations director Kalen Orr said.

The company says no employee was dismissed as part of the transition. That describes how this change occurred; it is not a guarantee about future employment.

### What work do people still do?

The carts carry bins between workstations. Employees still select items, inspect orders and pack parcels at the warehouse, which handles clothing and household accessories.

Worker Iri Dain moved from picking to returns and now spends more time examining damaged packaging than walking between shelves.

“The walking went down, but deciding what can go back into stock is still somebody's job,” Dain said.

The new maintenance roles require an equipment certificate and pay a higher base rate than entry-level picking jobs. North Loom paid for eight existing employees to take a certification course. Seven completed it, and four obtained the new maintenance jobs.

The other participants remained eligible for their existing roles; taking the course did not automatically secure a technician position. The training figures therefore show a route into different work for some employees, not an automatic move into higher-paid jobs for everyone enrolled.

### Does higher output prove automation paid off?

Average dispatched order lines increased from 10,000 to 12,000 per operating day during the transition, a 20% rise. An order line is one product entry within an order, not necessarily a whole parcel or a unique customer.

That increase coincided with the carts' introduction, but product mix and customer order sizes also changed. The company has not isolated the carts' effect on output.

The equipment cost $1.8 million. Without a published full unit-cost or payback calculation, the figures do not establish how quickly the investment might recover its cost.

### What do the figures leave out?

North Loom uses temporary staff during its winter peak. Those placements are excluded from the permanent headcount comparison, and the two advertised seasonal returns jobs should not be added to the July total as confirmed permanent hires.

The reported comparison also supplies no matched measurements of employee injuries, turnover or satisfaction.

The snapshot shows a smaller permanent workforce, a changed mix of jobs and higher reported output. It does not establish that every worker benefited, that the carts alone produced the output increase or that advertised openings reverse the overall staffing decline.
