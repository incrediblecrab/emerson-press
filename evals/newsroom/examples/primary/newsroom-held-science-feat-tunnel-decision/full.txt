It's a hole in the ground and a billboard. That's how Marcus Oduya, the civil engineer who led the original planning team for the Redcliff Tunnel, describes what he sees when he visits the project's staging area. "Eleven years," he said. "The city is holding its breath."

The proposed 4.2-kilometer road tunnel beneath a city neighborhood has been in environmental review for 11 years, and a final approval decision is now expected in spring 2025. The state transportation department has publicly committed to deciding by June 2025, and a department spokesperson said the decision will not be delayed again. The stakes for missing that window are financial as well as political: federal highway funds committed to the project, worth $620 million, expire in September 2025, and losing them would force the state to restart the funding process.

The delay has already reshaped the project's economics. The tunnel was estimated to cost $840 million when it was first planned in 2013; it is now projected at $1.4 billion. State officials attribute an estimated $280 million of that increase to inflation-adjusted construction cost increases, though the full gap between the original and current estimates is larger than that figure alone. The project's prime contractor has maintained a standby contract, at an annual cost of $12 million, since 2021, awaiting word on whether to proceed.

Three points of contention have driven the delay: an underground aquifer that an early environmental study underestimated, opposition from the Ridgemont neighborhood over surface disruption during construction, and two competing surface alternatives proposed by environmental advocates. An independent review commissioned by the state in 2023 found the aquifer management plan inadequate for the project's depth profile in its northeastern section. The plan has since been revised, but critics say the revision is also inadequate.

Carmen Osei, 43, leads the Ridgemont Traffic Justice Coalition, which opposes the tunnel in its current form but says it is not opposed to a tunnel in principle. The coalition wants a rerouted alignment that avoids the neighborhood's historic district. "We were told in 2016 that they had heard our concerns," Osei said. "We were told in 2019 that they had heard our concerns. We are still here in 2025." Osei said her coalition will go to court if the original alignment is approved without addressing the historic district concerns. A previous lawsuit by the coalition was dismissed on procedural grounds.

Community surveys conducted in 2024 found 58% of Ridgemont residents prefer the rerouted alignment over the original, while 61% of respondents citywide prefer the faster original alignment.

Meanwhile, the traffic problem the tunnel was designed to relieve has continued to grow. The surface arterials it is meant to relieve now handle 92,000 vehicle trips a day, up from 71,000 when the project was first studied. Veronica Cheng, a transportation planner who advises the city on the alternative surface options, said transit ridership in the corridor has grown 40% since 2013, which she said strengthens the case for a transit-oriented alternative. "A transit-oriented surface redesign could absorb a third of those trips and cost a fifth as much," Cheng said. "But it requires taking roadway space away from cars, and that is politically very hard."

On the city council, three members have publicly called for a full project restart; two others have said cancellation would waste the $340 million already spent on design and permitting. The state has not disclosed which alignment it prefers. The transportation director said the decision will incorporate community feedback, traffic modeling and cost.

Oduya retired in 2022, before any decision was made, and now works as a transportation consultant. He said the project's delay has been professionally formative. "I designed that tunnel to solve a traffic problem that existed in 2013," he said. "I can't tell you whether it solves the 2025 problem because we haven't made decisions fast enough to find out."

Whatever the state decides this spring, Oduya said he hopes it decides something. "A bad decision we can learn from," he said. "No decision is just time and money and opportunity disappearing."

The hole in the ground remains. So does the billboard.
