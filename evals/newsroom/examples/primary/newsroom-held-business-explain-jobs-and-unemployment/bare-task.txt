Bellwether County Adds Jobs and Sees Unemployment Rate Rise — Here's Why Both Are True

By [Staff Writer]

Bellwether County's economic bulletin this month contains a pairing that strikes many readers as contradictory: payroll jobs are up, and so is the unemployment rate. Local officials say both numbers are accurate — because they measure different things.

The bulletin, covering data as of August 31, 2026, reports 25,600 payroll jobs located in the county in July, up from 25,000 a year earlier. That count tracks positions at workplaces inside Bellwether's borders, regardless of where the workers who fill them actually live. It includes people who commute in from neighboring areas.

A separate measure, drawn from a survey of county residents, tells a different story. It estimates that 19,740 residents held jobs in July, up from 19,000 the year before — a gain of 740 people. But the same survey found 1,260 residents unemployed, up from 1,000, an increase of 260. Together, the county's resident labor force — those either working or actively looking for work — grew to 21,000 from 20,000. The unemployment rate rose to 6% from 5%, a one-percentage-point increase, not a one-percent change from the prior rate.

County analyst Omi Drel said the two counts simply start from different places. "One count starts at the workplace. The other starts with the person who lives here," Drel said.

Because of that difference in scope, the bulletin does not attempt to calculate how many payroll jobs are held by commuters by subtracting one total from the other. The two figures also differ in what kinds of work they capture — the payroll count and the resident count treat self-employment and people who hold more than one job differently, making a direct subtraction unreliable. Drel said later employer filings could still revise the payroll figure.

The unemployment rate also depends on a specific definition: a resident counts as unemployed only if they have no job, are available to work and are actively seeking it, under the survey's stated criteria. Students, retirees and caregivers who are not looking for work fall outside that definition — they are not automatically counted as unemployed.

That distinction matters for people like Nessa Holt, a parcel sorter who resumed job hunting in June after a year spent caring for a relative. Her return to the labor force is the kind of movement that can push up both the labor force and, if a job hasn't been secured yet, the unemployment count. Holt said she has applied for daytime positions but has run into a mismatch with what employers are offering. "A vacancy can be close to home and still be a shift I cannot take," she said, citing overnight-availability requirements attached to several local openings.

A county employer survey lists transport scheduling and machine maintenance among roles that have been hard to fill, though the survey does not match specific vacancies to specific job seekers, so it cannot confirm whether openings like those factored into Holt's search.

The bulletin does not identify how much of the labor force's growth came from migration into the county, new graduates entering the workforce, or residents like Holt returning after time away. Nor does it show that every industry expanded or that people already employed saw wage increases. Bellwether has published its workplace and resident job tables separately since 2012, and the July comparisons are not seasonally adjusted, meaning they compare like months in consecutive years rather than adjusting for typical seasonal patterns.
