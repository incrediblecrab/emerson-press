US Regulators Approve First Commercial Fusion Power Plant in Wyoming

RESTON FLATS, Wyo. — The Federal Energy Licensing Commission voted 4-1 on Thursday to grant a construction permit to Meridian Fusion Sciences for a commercial fusion power plant here, the first such permit ever issued in the United States.

The proposed facility, called the Reston Fusion Station, is designed to produce 400 megawatts of net electrical output using a tokamak reactor design. Construction is expected to take seven years, with Meridian projecting first power generation by 2032 at the earliest.

The permit followed a 38-month safety review that examined neutron activation of structural materials, tritium handling procedures and scenarios involving failure of the plant's electromagnetic confinement system. The Commission concluded that fusion reactions carry no chain-reaction risk and that the facility's primary radiological hazard is tritium, a mildly radioactive hydrogen isotope that will be bred on-site from lithium blankets to help supply the plant's continuous fuel needs, which also include deuterium.

Unlike fission reactors, which split heavy atoms, fusion plants combine light hydrogen isotopes and do not generate long-lived radioactive waste, according to the Commission.

"This permit represents the most rigorous safety evaluation ever conducted for a fusion facility, and we intend to meet every condition attached to it," Meridian CEO Dr. Sanjay Parikh said.

Commission Chair Elena Voronova said the vote reflected the agency's finding that fusion's hazard profile differs fundamentally from fission, requiring substantial adaptation of existing nuclear regulations to apply.

The dissenting commissioner cited insufficient public comment time on the plant's tritium storage plan, which was revised 11 months into the review.

Two environmental groups filed for administrative review within hours of the vote, arguing that the environmental impact statement covering tritium storage was inadequate.

The permit requires Meridian to submit quarterly construction reports and gives the Commission authority to halt work if safety benchmarks are not met. The agency has not yet issued guidance on how fusion plant operators should handle the eventual decommissioning of activated structural components.

Meridian has raised $4.1 billion in private investment and holds a conditional $1.3 billion loan guarantee from the Department of Energy, contingent on construction milestones. Wyoming's governor welcomed the permit, citing an estimated 2,400 construction jobs and 180 permanent operations positions. The Reston Flats site was chosen partly for its remote location and access to Wyoming's existing high-voltage transmission infrastructure.

The approval comes amid a broader surge in fusion investment. Global private capital committed to fusion companies has exceeded $6 billion since 2020, with more than 30 companies pursuing commercial timelines, according to the Fusion Industry Association.

But some researchers cautioned that regulatory approval does not guarantee the plant will perform as projected. "Approval-stage optimism has historically outpaced operational performance in first-of-kind energy facilities. The timeline risk here is real," said Dr. Marcus Webb, a nuclear policy researcher at the Clearwater Energy Institute.

No fusion plant anywhere in the world has yet operated at net positive energy output on a sustained commercial basis, making the Reston Fusion Station, if completed, a significant test of whether the technology can move from laboratory demonstrations to the power grid.
