# Where Copper Wren's 18% service charge goes — and how it differs from a tip

Diners at Copper Wren have paid an 18% service charge on dine-in food and drink checks since July, but the money does not go directly from each table to its server. Under the restaurant's stated policy, it is business revenue used for payroll, paid leave and other labor costs.

The distinction matters for customers deciding what their bill covers and whether to leave anything extra. The service charge is mandatory on eligible dine-in orders. An additional tip is optional, and the restaurant's payment screen starts with no additional tip selected.

Here is how the policy works as of Aug. 17, 2026.

## What does the charge add to a bill?

On a $50 eligible food and drink check, the service charge is $9, bringing the total to $59 before separately calculated taxes. The charge does not apply to takeaway orders.

Copper Wren left its menu base prices unchanged when it introduced the charge in July. That means the mandatory pretax total increased for eligible dine-in orders, even though the listed base prices stayed the same.

The charge is disclosed on menus and the restaurant's booking page, rather than appearing for the first time on the final receipt. Copper Wren has 46 seats and serves lunch and dinner five days a week.

## Where does the money go?

The restaurant retains service-charge receipts and uses them for its labor budget, including payroll and paid leave. It does not promise to give the full 18% collected from a table directly to that table's server.

When the charge began, Copper Wren raised servers' base pay from $16 to $22 an hour. Kitchen workers also receive pay under the revised labor budget, although their individual rates were not provided.

Owner Leda Selt says the goal is to make the base wage less dependent on the size of individual table tips.

“We can promise an hourly rate more clearly than we can promise what a particular table will leave,” Selt said.

The policy therefore connects the charge to labor spending, but not through a promise that each table's payment becomes an equivalent payout to its server.

## Does that mean every worker earns more?

The higher hourly base rate does not guarantee a fixed number of shifts or a particular weekly take-home amount. The reporting record also lacks matched shift-by-shift earnings data establishing how every employee's total earnings changed.

Server Toma Dain says the new base rate makes quiet shifts easier to plan around, while busy nights previously could produce larger tips.

“A steadier floor matters, but people should not assume every worker's total went up by the same amount,” Dain said.

Nor has the restaurant published a verified before-and-after profit comparison. The available information does not establish how the change affected profits.

## Should diners add another tip?

Customers may leave an additional tip, but Copper Wren's notice asks them not to treat the service charge as an automatic obligation to add another 18%.

Under this business's policy, the key difference is choice and allocation: The service charge is a required payment retained as restaurant revenue for labor costs, not a promised direct payment to the table's server. Any additional tip is a separate, optional payment.

That explains Copper Wren's stated approach. It is not a universal legal rule about how all restaurants must handle service charges or tips.
