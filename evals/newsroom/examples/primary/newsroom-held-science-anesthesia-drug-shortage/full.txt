**Fire at Indiana Plant Triggers National Shortage of Key Surgical Anesthetic**

CLEARFIELD, Ind. — A fire that destroyed the largest fill-and-finish production line at Meridia Pharma's propofol plant here on Oct. 22 has set off a national shortage of the anesthetic used in roughly 50 million surgical procedures a year, hospital officials and federal regulators said.

The Clearfield facility is Meridia's primary U.S. manufacturing site for propofol, the most commonly used general anesthetic in American operating rooms. The destroyed line accounted for about 40% of the company's U.S. propofol supply. Meridia holds a 58% share of the national propofol market, according to the company.

The Indiana State Fire Marshal's office is investigating the cause of the fire. No injuries were reported.

The Food and Drug Administration placed propofol on its critical shortage list Oct. 25 and issued orders facilitating imports from two overseas manufacturers, Pharmax Belgium and Adena Laboratorios of Spain, to help offset the loss. The agency said it expects the first imported shipments from Pharmax Belgium to arrive within three weeks.

Import facilitation does not waive federal manufacturing standards, the FDA said; imported product still requires FDA inspection or documentation showing it meets equivalent standards.

Hospital procurement officers said distributors have already cut propofol allocations to their institutions by 25% to 35%. Hospital networks in the Southeast and Midwest have reported the most severe constraints because of their heavier reliance on Meridia as a regional distributor.

Dr. Patricia Lam, chief of anesthesiology at a regional hospital, said providers have begun shifting some elective cases to alternative sedatives such as etomidate and ketamine, but that no single substitute fully replicates propofol's effects.

"We have shifted some elective cases to alternative agents, but propofol's pharmacokinetics — particularly how quickly patients wake up — cannot be fully replicated by any single substitute," Lam said.

Lam said anesthesia training programs focus heavily on propofol protocols, meaning providers switching to other agents during the shortage need rapid in-service retraining.

The FDA said it is monitoring substitution rates to track whether alternative agents are being used safely in patients for whom they may be less appropriate.

No elective surgery cancellations tied to the shortage had been publicly confirmed as of Tuesday, but hospital administrators said they were making daily decisions about which cases to prioritize.

Meridia CEO Dr. Carlos Estrada said the company is redirecting output from its secondary manufacturing plant in Arizona to help meet demand and expects the damaged Clearfield line to be rebuilt within 12 to 18 months.

Meridia's stock fell 18% in the week after the fire before partially recovering following the FDA's import announcement.

Propofol's relatively short shelf life — 24 months from manufacture — makes building strategic reserves more difficult than for solid oral medications, complicating efforts to buffer against the disruption.

The shortage is the highest-volume item among 47 active shortages of sterile injectable medications currently listed in the federal drug shortage database. Drug policy researchers have pointed to the episode as a case study in the risks of single-source dependence, noting that one company held a majority U.S. market share while relying on a single primary domestic manufacturing site.

Members of Congress from Indiana have asked the FDA to provide monthly status reports on the shortage as recovery efforts continue.
