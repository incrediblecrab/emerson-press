At Dellford Station, Volunteers Keep an Old Piano — and Its Rules — in Tune

DELLFORD — Every day the public piano sits open in an agreed corner of Dellford station's concourse, a small crowd of regulars, commuters and curious travelers takes turns at the keys, watched over by a rotating team of volunteers who treat the instrument as much a piece of station infrastructure as a source of music.

The upright arrived at the station as a donation, transferred for community use — but not, as volunteers are careful to note, with any guaranteed future funding for its upkeep. That distinction shapes almost everything about how the piano is managed.

Before each playing session, volunteer steward Mara Cale runs through a short routine: checking the bench, looking over the piano's visible condition and confirming it falls within the posted hours before she lifts the cover. It's not a technical inspection, she said — just a way of catching obvious problems early.

"You can hear someone settle into a tune and then watch them remember they have a train to catch," Cale said, describing the rhythm of concourse life around the piano. On a recent afternoon, one traveler played a short passage while a companion kept an eye on the departure board, the music pausing as quickly as it started.

That give-and-take with the demands of a working station is by design. Playing stops during specified station activities and outside the piano's published hours, under the terms of a local-use agreement that treats the instrument as a guest in transportation space, not the other way around. Announcements and passenger movement take priority.

Station employee Rena Voss sometimes points waiting passengers toward the piano area, though she's careful to keep the main information queue clear while doing so. "It changes the wait for some people," Voss said. "For others the useful thing is still a clear announcement and somewhere to put a bag." Nearby staff, who work long shifts within earshot of the piano, have asked for hours that build in breaks from the sound.

The instrument itself shows its age. A repaired corner on the case remains visible beneath the current finish, a reminder that the piano's condition is monitored but not guaranteed. A simple fault log tracks problems like sticking keys or bench issues, giving a local piano technician — paid through a small donation-supported fund — a way to distinguish one-off complaints from recurring trouble. Volunteers are quick to point out that their own checks don't substitute for that professional maintenance, nor certify that every internal part is sound.

Access to the piano stays deliberately informal. Volunteers don't audition players or reserve the instrument for anyone with formal training; anyone during open hours can sit down and play. Organized performances are the exception — those require a separate arrangement with the station rather than simply claiming the piano for an unannounced event.

Visitors hoping to record a player up close are asked, under local guidance, to get that player's agreement first — a station practice rather than a broader legal rule about filming in public spaces.

No one involved claims to know exactly how many people play the piano in a given week, or how much it affects passengers' moods during a wait — those numbers simply haven't been tracked. What organizers do know is that upkeep costs money. Volunteers are planning a fundraising session once the next technician's assessment lays out what work the piano actually needs.

Dellford station has made no announcement about platform expansion or any permanent guarantee that the piano can keep its current spot on the concourse — leaving its future, like its music, dependent on what happens next.
