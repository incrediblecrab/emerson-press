# Nuclear waste repository approved in Nevada after four-decade review

The Nuclear Regulatory Authority on Thursday granted a construction and operating license for a permanent nuclear waste repository in southern Nevada, ending a regulatory process that began in 1983 and drawing a pledge of legal challenges from the state’s governor.

The April 3 approval for the Ashford Basin facility concludes the longest-running major infrastructure licensing proceeding in U.S. history. Construction is expected to begin in 2026, with the first waste shipments arriving by 2032.

The repository is designed to hold 77,000 metric tons of heavy metal, roughly equivalent to the spent nuclear fuel accumulated at U.S. reactors since commercial nuclear power began in the 1950s. It would provide a permanent destination for spent fuel and high-level radioactive waste now held at scattered locations.

“The scientific case for this site has been sustained over four decades of study. The question was never geology — it was politics,” authority Chair William Sokol said.

Nevada Gov. Diana Farr called the decision an act of federal overreach, saying the state had never consented to serve as the nation’s nuclear waste dump. She said Nevada would pursue legal challenges.

The approval follows a 2023 federal court ruling that dismissed the most recent state challenge to the licensing process. Sokol said the authority would work with Nevada and affected tribal nations on transportation safety planning, but the license itself was final, subject to court review.

Waste would be placed about 980 feet below the desert surface in volcanic tuff that scientists assessed as geologically stable for at least 100,000 years. The design also includes engineered barriers, including steel waste canisters and a titanium drip shield.

Several national environmental organizations opposing the site argue that the geology has not been adequately modeled for water infiltration over long periods. Spent fuel contains uranium, plutonium and fission products that remain hazardous for periods ranging from decades to hundreds of thousands of years.

Transportation is another point of dispute. Farr and Nevada’s congressional delegation argue that thousands of rail and truck shipments would pose unacceptable risks along routes through populated areas. The license requires waste to be transported only in certified containers meeting a standard set by the International Atomic Energy Agency.

Two Indigenous nations with treaty claims to land near the proposed site also formally objected during licensing. Their petitions for consultation were acknowledged, but they were not granted standing to block approval.

The federal government currently stores spent fuel in temporary dry casks at 77 reactor sites across 33 states. That arrangement was intended as a short-term measure when introduced in the 1980s. Supporters say a permanent repository would allow that storage to be consolidated, reducing the number of places where waste is kept.

Construction and operation over the repository’s licensed lifetime are projected to cost $38 billion. Funding comes from a federal nuclear waste trust fund financed by utility ratepayers since 1983. The fund held about $44 billion as of 2024, exceeding the project’s current cost estimate.

The decision comes as other countries move forward with deep underground disposal. Finland opened the world’s first operating deep geological repository for high-level nuclear waste in 2025, and Sweden’s is under construction.
