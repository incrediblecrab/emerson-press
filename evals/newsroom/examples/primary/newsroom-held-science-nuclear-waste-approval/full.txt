# Nuclear waste repository approved in Nevada after decades of review

The Nuclear Regulatory Authority granted a license April 3 for a permanent nuclear waste repository in southern Nevada, ending decades of regulatory review and drawing a pledge of legal challenges from the state's governor.

The construction and operating license authorizes a deep geological repository in the Ashford Basin for spent nuclear fuel and high-level radioactive waste. Construction is expected to begin in 2026, with the first waste shipments expected by 2032.

The approval concludes a regulatory process that began in 1983, the longest-running major infrastructure licensing proceeding in U.S. history. But Nevada Gov. Diana Farr called the decision federal overreach, saying the state had never consented to serve as the nation's nuclear waste dump.

The repository is designed to hold 77,000 metric tons of heavy metal, roughly the amount accumulated in spent fuel at U.S. reactors since commercial nuclear power began in the 1950s. Waste would be placed 300 meters below the desert surface in volcanic tuff, a rock formation scientists assessed as geologically stable for at least 100,000 years.

"The scientific case for this site has been sustained over four decades of study. The question was never geology — it was politics," said William Sokol, chair of the Nuclear Regulatory Authority.

Several national environmental organizations dispute the adequacy of the site's geological modeling, arguing that it does not sufficiently account for water infiltration over long periods. The repository's design includes steel waste canisters and a titanium drip shield as engineered barriers in addition to the surrounding rock.

Spent nuclear fuel contains uranium, plutonium and fission products that remain hazardous for periods ranging from decades to hundreds of thousands of years.

The federal government currently stores spent fuel in temporary dry casks at 77 reactor sites across 33 states. That arrangement was intended as a short-term measure when introduced in the 1980s. Supporters of the repository say it would consolidate that storage, reducing the number of locations where waste is kept.

Moving the waste remains a central objection for Nevada officials. Farr and the state's congressional delegation argue that thousands of rail and truck shipments would pose unacceptable risks along transport corridors through populated areas. The license requires that waste be transported only in certified containers meeting a standard set by the International Atomic Energy Agency.

Two Indigenous nations with treaty claims to land near the proposed site also formally objected during licensing. Their petitions for consultation were acknowledged, but they were not granted standing to block approval.

Sokol said the authority would work with Nevada and affected tribal nations on transport safety planning. He said the license itself was final, subject to court review. The approval follows a 2023 federal court ruling dismissing the most recent state legal challenge to the licensing process.

Construction and operation over the repository's licensed lifetime are projected to cost $38 billion. Funding comes from a federal nuclear waste trust fund financed by utility ratepayers since 1983. The fund held approximately $44 billion as of 2024, more than the project's current cost estimate.

Finland opened the world's first operating deep geological repository for high-level nuclear waste in 2025. Sweden's is under construction.
