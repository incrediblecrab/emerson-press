At the Chess Tables in Greymere Square, No Membership Required

GREYMERE SQUARE — On a recent afternoon, six fixed chess tables here held a small cross-section of the square's routine: one game already underway, a newcomer watching from a few feet off, and a second pair working through a match with a mismatched set at the table next door.

The tables, installed during an earlier refurbishment of the square, are public furniture, not property reserved for the town's registered chess club. Anyone can sit down. Anyone can walk away. The boards are built into the tabletops, but the pieces are not — they belong to whoever brings or lends them.

That gap is where Arven Pell comes in. A regular at the tables, Pell shows up carrying two cloth bags of spare pieces, because passersby often arrive without a set of their own.

"Some people want a game, some want to point at a move, and some are still deciding whether to sit down," Pell said.

Pell's only request, at the end of an afternoon, is to get the borrowed pieces back. There is no fee, no membership card, no sign-in sheet. Some of the pieces in Pell's bags are mismatched replacements, collected over time, but a chipped bishop or an odd-colored pawn does not stop a game from being played.

Among the regulars there is an informal custom: when people are waiting for a table, someone already playing will look to wrap up and clear space for a new game. It is not a rule enforced by the city, and it is not a guarantee — a table is not always free the moment someone wants one. But it is enough, players say, to keep the square from feeling closed off to anyone who wants in.

Edda Vale was one of the newcomers who took advantage of that openness, though not right away. Vale watched from the sidelines for several afternoons before asking to play.

"It helped that I could listen before I had to explain what I knew," Vale said.

The square's informal games exist alongside a more structured option a short distance away. The town's registered chess club sometimes invites players from the square to its indoor sessions, though the two settings run on different terms — the club requires booking and membership, while the square does not. Some regulars say they prefer the open, unstructured conversation of outdoor play to timed club matches; others move between both settings depending on the day.

None of this is overseen by any official body. The parks team that maintains the square repairs the furniture — including a chipped corner on one table, logged for repair but not yet fixed — but does not referee games or keep a ranking of players. There is no attendance register, so no one can say how many distinct people have sat down to play over the course of a season.

The square serves other purposes, too. Shoppers pass through, and people wait for buses near the tables without any interest in the games beside them. A nearby cafe lets players refill a water jug during its open hours, an informal courtesy rather than a service tied to the city installation.

The setup has limits. Wind and rain can end an afternoon outright; the city has not enclosed or roofed the tables. And for all its regulars and newcomers, the square has produced no titled champion, no claim of measurable improvement in local play, and no data suggesting an effect on crime or health in the area. What it has, on most afternoons, is a place to sit down and play — or just to watch.
