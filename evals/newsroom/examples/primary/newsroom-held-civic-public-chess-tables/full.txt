Squares off, no scoreboard: Greymere's public chess tables draw regulars and the curious

GREYMERE SQUARE — On a recent afternoon, one game held steady at a corner table while a newcomer stood back and watched. At the next table, a pair worked through a match using pieces borrowed from a cloth bag. Nobody asked them for a fee, and nobody asked for a club card.

The six chess tables here were installed during an earlier refurbishment of the square, and they belong to the public, not to the town's registered chess club. Anyone can sit down.

That openness depends partly on Arven Pell, a regular who carries two cloth bags of spare pieces to the square because passersby often show up without a set of their own. Some of the pieces are mismatched replacements — a bishop from one set standing in for a missing one from another — but Pell said that does not stop a game.

"Some people want a game, some want to point at a move, and some are still deciding whether to sit down," Pell said.

Pell asks only that borrowed pieces come back before people leave. There is no playing fee and no requirement to join the club that meets indoors elsewhere in town.

The tables themselves have built-in boards, but the pieces are the responsibility of whoever brings or lends them. The parks team that maintains the square repairs the furniture — a chipped corner on one table has been logged for repair and has not yet been fixed — but the team does not referee games or keep any kind of player ranking. There is no attendance register, so no one can say how many different players have used the tables over a season.

Among the regulars is an informal custom: when people are waiting for a turn, a table opens up for a new game. It is not a rule enforced by the city, and it does not guarantee a table is free the moment someone wants one.

Edda Vale learned the square's rhythms before playing in it. Vale watched several afternoons before asking to sit down for a game.

"It helped that I could listen before I had to explain what I knew," Vale said.

The town's formal chess club sometimes invites square players to its indoor sessions, though the two settings run on different terms — the club requires booking and membership, while the square does not. Some regulars said they prefer the open conversation of the square to timed club matches; others use both.

Not everyone near the tables is there for chess. The square also serves shoppers and people waiting for buses, and a nearby cafe lets players refill a water jug during its opening hours, an arrangement between the cafe and its customers rather than part of the city's installation.

Play is also at the mercy of the weather. The tables sit uncovered, and wind or rain can end an afternoon outright; the city has not enclosed or roofed them.

No player at the tables holds a competitive title, and the reporting for this story found no claim that the square's games have measurably improved local chess skill, reduced crime or produced any documented health benefit. What continues, on the afternoons the weather allows, is simpler: a spare set, a place to sit, and a custom of making room for the next game.
