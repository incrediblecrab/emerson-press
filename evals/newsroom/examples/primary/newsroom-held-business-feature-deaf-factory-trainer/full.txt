**At Bracken Spur Fabrication, a Deaf Mentor Rebuilds How Newcomers Learn the Job**

The training corner at Bracken Spur Fabrication used to hold empty shipping cartons. Now it holds a screen, a camera mount and a set of demonstration videos that trainees can pause and replay until a step makes sense.

The change traces to a June training note. Vara Chenro, a Deaf metalworker who has spent 12 years at the plant, stopped a demonstration video midplay because the camera angle hid the instructor's hand position — the exact detail a trainee would need to copy. Chenro became a paid training mentor at Bracken Spur in January and has since worked with production trainers to rebuild the material trainees rely on.

Bracken Spur makes brackets and enclosures for commercial equipment and employs 64 people. Its earlier induction combined spoken demonstrations with a printed checklist, which did not reproduce every explanation a trainee heard live. The revised materials pair captioned demonstrations with close camera views and numbered written steps, checked by production trainers before they go into use.

A caption is not sufficient on its own, according to the training record: if a movement is obscured on camera, the caption underneath it does not fix that, and the team sometimes reshoots a scene to get the angle right. "Access is not putting words under a picture that still hides the thing you need to see," Chenro wrote in comments supplied for this account.

Chenro helped select camera angles and check instructions as part of paid mentor duties, not as a volunteer accessibility effort. The record does not describe Chenro's motivations for the work, any change in hearing or a skill attributed to deafness; it documents a role and a set of decisions made in that role.

Trainees can pause and replay a demonstration, but replay does not replace supervised practice or an instructor's signoff before a worker is cleared on a task. The company also continues to provide agreed communication support for individual workers, treating the videos as one part of training rather than a full substitute for that support.

The first 22 trainees to use the revised materials included hearing workers and people new to the factory's technical vocabulary. Seventeen of them reported using the replay function, but Bracken Spur did not measure how each person's performance changed afterward, and the record contains no injury-rate or defect-rate study tying the new materials to a measurable improvement.

Omi Raylen, a trainer at the plant, said the videos let a worker revisit a step without waiting for someone to repeat a live explanation. "A question asked twice is still cheaper than work done once on the wrong understanding," Raylen said.

The materials were not free to build. Bracken Spur spent $2,400 on screens and mounting equipment and recorded 60 staff hours preparing the first set of videos. That figure does not include a cost for the staff time, and the factory has not produced an estimate for future revisions as more procedures are recorded.

For now, the training corner that once held cartons holds instead a small, checked library of video and text — material trainees said they used, without a company record yet of what using it changed.
