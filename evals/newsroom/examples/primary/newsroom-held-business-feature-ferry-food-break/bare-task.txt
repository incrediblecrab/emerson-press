# At Selwick ferry counter, breakfast runs on the waterfront's clock

Before the first ferry crew’s boarding call at Selwick Ferry Terminal on Aug. 18, Ceri Lann was packing preordered breakfast rolls. At the food counter, which occupies a former ticket-information booth, an insulated collection shelf holds completed orders for workers whose chance to collect them can depend on the next sailing.

Lann moved opening time from 7 a.m. to 5:45 a.m. in May to serve terminal and vessel workers. The terminal’s first staff shifts begin at 5:30 a.m., while most nearby shops do not open until after 8 a.m. The earlier start puts the small counter’s timetable closer to the waterfront’s working day, though even a prepared breakfast requires a gap in the work.

Deck worker Omi Darsen sometimes orders ahead because boarding checks can leave only a short collection window.

“The useful part is knowing the food is ready when the gap in the work appears,” Darsen said.

Workers can bring their own food. But opportunities to leave the secure work area depend on their duties and sailing schedules. The terminal has a staff break room, too, though its opening does not mean every job can pause at once. For the counter, serving breakfast early is only part of the task; having it ready when a worker can collect it is another.

A six-week pilot run by the ferry operator adds a subsidy to that arrangement. It offers 40 eligible early-shift employees a $5 food voucher for each shift worked. The voucher can be used at Lann’s counter before 9 a.m.; it is not a cash payment added to wages.

The posted breakfast package costs $6.50, including any applicable tax. A worker using one $5 voucher pays the remaining $1.50. Unused vouchers expire on the day they are issued, and the pilot is not available to everyone working around the harbor.

For Lann, the voucher also puts part of each sale on a different clock. The operator reimburses redeemed vouchers every 14 days, while suppliers are paid weekly. That leaves a gap between handing over food and receiving the subsidized portion of the price.

“A voucher is a sale I have made, but it is not cash I can hand to the bread supplier that morning,” Lann said.

The kiosk’s rent is $850 a month, with electricity billed separately. Lann said early-opening labor costs continue even when a sailing is canceled. Those commitments sit alongside a service designed around workers’ limited collection windows, but Lann has not provided a complete profit comparison for the earlier hours.

The pilot’s broader effects also remain unestablished. As of Aug. 18, the operator had not published evidence that the vouchers improved attendance, productivity or health. The reporting record shows no change to sailing times, security access rules or workers’ contractual break entitlements.

The operator plans to review the pilot after six weeks and has made no commitment to extend it to later shifts or other vendors. For now, the counter’s earlier opening and preordered breakfasts offer a service within the existing work schedule, not a change to it: food can wait on the insulated shelf, but collection still has to fit around the job.
