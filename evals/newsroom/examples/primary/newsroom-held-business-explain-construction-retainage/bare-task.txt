**Certified Doesn't Mean Paid: A Loden Roof Job Shows Why**

LODEN — When the city certified Wickmere Roofing's invoice for the roof it installed at the municipal workshop, the paperwork said the job was worth $200,000. Wickmere's check said $190,000.

The $10,000 gap is not a dispute over quality or an accounting error. It is retention — a standard clause in the company's contract with the city that holds back a share of certified payments until specific, later conditions are met.

"Certification records the work's value," said Iri Keld, the city's contracts manager. "Release of the last money has another set of checks."

Under the roofing contract, the city withheld 5% of the certified amount, or $10,000, as retention. Half of that, $5,000, is due after a completion inspection. The rest follows a six-month defect-correction period, provided the conditions spelled out in the contract are met. City officials stressed the terms apply to this contract and are not a citywide formula; other municipal agreements can set different percentages or release schedules.

Wickmere installed the main roof in June under a $200,000 contract at the workshop, a city building that also houses road-maintenance equipment. Other contractors handled the building's electrical and door work under separate agreements.

The completion inspection has already turned up a snag. The city's inspector found an unfinished gutter connection that must be completed before the first $5,000 is released. Wickmere does not dispute the finding. Owner Edda Marsh said the specified fitting is on order, and the company is waiting on delivery before it can finish the connection. The city said it will inspect promptly once the fitting is installed but has not set a date, because the delivery timeline is still uncertain.

Keld said the withheld money is not sitting in limbo because of budget trouble; the city has already budgeted the retained funds. The delay is procedural, not financial, he said.

That distinction matters to Marsh, whose five-person crew has weekly payroll obligations that don't wait for inspections. "The work can be on the building while part of the payment is still on somebody else's calendar," she said.

Wickmere's combined weekly payroll runs about $4,800, meaning the $10,000 held back is worth more than two weeks of wages for the crew. That does not mean the money represents the company's profit on the job — Marsh has not disclosed the roofing package's full costs for materials, labor and overhead, so how much of the retained amount is profit versus reimbursed expense is not public.

Keld said the arrangement is intentional, not punitive: holding back part of the payment gives contractors a financial reason to finish smaller, lingering details — like a gutter connection — after the bulk of the work is done and inspected.

Notably, the six-month clock for releasing the final $5,000 does not start simply because Wickmere submitted its original invoice. Under the contract, it begins only when the project reaches a defined completion stage, which has not yet occurred because of the outstanding gutter work.

Neither the city nor Wickmere describes the situation as a breach of contract, and the city has announced no changes to its procurement rules as a result. Both sides describe the holdback as retention working as designed — a mechanism built to outlast the invoice itself.

Wickmere has since asked other public clients whether smaller contracts might carry lower retention percentages, though no such change is expected in the current agreement.

For now, the fitting remains on order, the gutter connection remains unfinished, and $10,000 remains where the contract put it: certified, but not yet released.
