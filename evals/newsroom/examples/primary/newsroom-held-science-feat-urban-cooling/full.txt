# Riverdale's cooling effort brings shade, but some blocks still wait

When James Abubakar moved to Elmore Street in Riverdale's South District 44 years ago, the street had no shade trees, he said. Now it has 14. During hot weeks, he has begun watering the young trees himself.

For Abubakar, 71, the change has become a matter of looking after his block. He believes the trees are helping his neighbors through the summer, though he is careful about what he can claim.

“In the summer of 2021, I counted four people from this block who went to the hospital for heat,” he said. “This summer, nobody I know of. I can't prove the trees did that. But I believe it.”

Riverdale's Cool Streets Initiative has produced measurable cooling, but its benefits have not reached every neighborhood. As of July 8, the city is planning an expansion whose funding remains uncertain. Its experience shows both what a cooling program can accomplish and how difficult it is to connect lower temperatures to specific health outcomes.

Between 2019 and 2024, Riverdale planted 18,000 trees and installed cool roofing on 340 municipal buildings. Monitoring using 160 distributed temperature sensors and satellite thermal imagery found an average temperature reduction of 1.8 degrees Celsius within 200 meters of intervention sites.

That boundary matters. Naomi Wheeler, the urban climatologist who designed the monitoring program, said areas without interventions showed no significant reduction. Those areas were predominantly lower-income, higher-density neighborhoods.

Wheeler said the health effects are harder to establish than the temperature change. Access to cooling centers and individual health conditions complicate efforts to determine which outcomes can be attributed to the initiative.

“We measured the temperature. The temperature went down,” Wheeler said. “The physiological effect of that reduction is real physics. The specific health outcomes in specific individuals are much harder to attribute to the trees.”

The city's heat emergency plan credits the initiative with reducing emergency cooling center activations from 11 in 2019 to four in 2024. Emergency management officials said the correlation is plausible but not causally established.

Keeping trees alive has presented another challenge. Urban climate coordinator Maria Fontaine said 12% of the planted trees died in their first two years because of compacted soil and inadequate watering during drought. The city invested an additional $800,000 in replacements.

The initiative cost $28 million, with support from federal grants, state health department funds and private foundations. The city's contribution was $6 million.

In Northgate, Clara Adjei, 38, said her block is waiting for the second phase.

“I read about the temperature results in South District,” she said. “We have the same pavement, the same sun, no trees. We've been waiting.”

Fontaine said the next phase will prioritize neighborhoods without coverage. The city plans to apply for another federal grant cycle targeting 6,000 more trees and 80 additional cool-roof installations. Approval is not guaranteed, and the current federal grant program is up for reauthorization.

“We showed it works,” Fontaine said. “The equity question is where we do it next.”

Wheeler said the monitoring data has been shared with eight other cities developing urban cooling programs. Its five-year span captures trees maturing into meaningful shade, she said.

On Elmore Street, Abubakar has taken on some of the work of keeping that shade alive.

“Nobody asked me to,” he said of watering the trees. “But I watched them plant those trees. I'm not going to watch them die.”
