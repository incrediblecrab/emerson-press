# How Meadow Key’s resale formula limits home prices — and shares gains

Buying a home through the Meadow Key Community Land Trust means agreeing in advance to a limit on its resale price. Owners can receive part of an increase in the home’s appraised value, but they cannot assume they will be allowed to sell at its full unrestricted market value.

The arrangement is intended to preserve access for later eligible buyers. It also creates a trade-off for the current owner: The agreement limits how much appreciation can be included in the sale price.

As of Sept. 18, 2026, the trust has 32 homes using the agreement. Here is how the formula works and what its limits mean.

## What does the buyer own?

The trust retains ownership of the land, while participating buyers purchase the homes under its resale agreement. The trust is a separate nonprofit organization; participation does not make the city the owner of each house.

Buyers receive the resale formula and land-use agreement before purchasing. The cap is not introduced only when an owner decides to move.

## How is the resale ceiling calculated?

The basic formula starts with the original purchase price and adds 25% of the appraised increase in the home value defined by the agreement.

The trust’s hypothetical example begins with both a purchase price and an initial appraised home value of $120,000. A later appraisal puts that defined home value at $160,000.

The arithmetic proceeds in three steps:

- The appraised increase is $40,000: $160,000 minus $120,000.
- The seller’s 25% share of that increase is $10,000.
- Adding $10,000 to the original $120,000 purchase price produces a basic resale ceiling of $130,000.

The example then adds an $8,000 approved improvement credit, bringing the adjusted ceiling to $138,000.

“The formula leaves part of the gain with the seller and part in the price available to the next buyer,” trust director Pera Noll said.

Those figures illustrate the agreement. They do not describe an actual household’s transaction or forecast future appreciation.

## Do all repairs and improvements raise the ceiling?

No. An improvement credit must follow the agreement’s approval process. It does not automatically equal every dollar an owner says they spent.

The supplied credit policy also treats routine repairs differently from approved value-adding improvements. Owners should not read the example’s $8,000 credit as a blanket allowance for any work on a home.

The appraisal process is specified in the agreement. An informal estimate on a property-advertising website is not the same thing.

## Is the ceiling what the seller takes home?

No. The ceiling is the maximum permitted sale price, not a guaranteed offer or a promise of an immediate sale.

An owner’s mortgage balance, selling costs and other transaction items affect the cash retained after a sale. None is included in the simple price example.

The trust helps identify eligible buyers, but the agreement does not give every interested applicant an automatic right to a home. Its separate waiting list is not a count of homes available for sale.

## What should buyers understand before signing?

“People should see the arithmetic before they buy, while there is still time to decide whether this arrangement fits them,” resident board member Elra Venn said.

Staff work through the example and take questions at buyer-information meetings in the trust’s community room.

Some questions require more than the illustration. The packet does not establish what happens if appraised value falls; that requires the agreement’s separate provisions. It also supplies no long-term outcome study showing whether this trust has achieved its intended access goals.

Finally, this is Meadow Key’s formula, not a rule shared by every community land trust.
