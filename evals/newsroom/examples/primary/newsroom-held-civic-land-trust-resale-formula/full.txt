# How Meadow Key's resale formula limits the price of a home

Homeowners in the Meadow Key Community Land Trust can receive a share of their home's appraised increase in value when they sell, but their resale agreement caps the price. The basic formula adds 25% of that increase to the original purchase price, with a separate credit for approved improvements.

The arrangement divides ownership: The nonprofit trust keeps the land, while participating buyers purchase the homes under its resale agreement. The city does not own each house. The price limit is intended to preserve access for later eligible buyers, though the supplied reporting materials include no long-term study showing this trust's results.

For someone considering a purchase, the central question is how that limit would affect a later sale. Meadow Key provides the resale formula and land-use agreement before purchase, so buyers can consider the restriction before committing.

## How the arithmetic works

The trust's hypothetical example begins with a home purchased for $120,000 and an initial appraised home value of $120,000. A later appraisal puts the defined home value at $160,000 — an increase of $40,000.

The seller's share under the basic formula is 25% of that $40,000 increase, or $10,000. Adding that amount to the original $120,000 purchase price produces a basic resale ceiling of $130,000.

The example then adds an $8,000 approved improvement credit, bringing the adjusted ceiling to $138,000. That is the maximum permitted price in the illustration, not the later appraised value of $160,000.

"The formula leaves part of the gain with the seller and part in the price available to the next buyer," trust director Pera Noll said.

These figures illustrate the agreement. They are neither an actual household's transaction nor a forecast of future appreciation. The 25% share applies to the appraised increase in the defined home value, not to the home's entire later value.

## What can change the calculation

An improvement credit is not an automatic reimbursement for every dollar an owner says they spent. It follows the agreement's approval process, and the supplied credit policy treats routine repairs differently from approved value-adding improvements.

The appraisal also follows a process specified in the agreement. An informal estimate on a property-advertising website is separate from that process.

The example covers an increase in value. The supplied materials do not establish what happens if the appraised value falls; that situation requires the agreement's separate provisions. Meadow Key's formula should not be assumed to apply to every community land trust.

## What the ceiling does not promise

A permitted price is not a guaranteed offer or a promise of an immediate sale. Nor does the $138,000 adjusted ceiling show how much cash the seller would retain. The owner's mortgage balance, selling costs and other transaction items affect that amount and are left out of the simple example.

The trust helps identify eligible buyers, but the agreement does not give every interested applicant an automatic right to a home. As of Sept. 18, 2026, 32 homes use the agreement. The trust also has a waiting list, which is not a count of homes available for sale.

Staff work through the example and take questions at buyer-information meetings in the trust's community room.

"People should see the arithmetic before they buy, while there is still time to decide whether this arrangement fits them," resident board member Elra Venn said.
