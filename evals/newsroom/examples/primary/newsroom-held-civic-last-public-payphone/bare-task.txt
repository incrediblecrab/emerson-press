# At Ternhaven station, keeping the last payphone ready for the next caller

TERNHAVEN — During his opening routine at the Ternhaven bus station, attendant Jorin Fell wipes the receiver of the station’s last public payphone and checks that its fault-reporting number is legible. At the nearby information window, he keeps spare paper and a pencil for travelers who need to write down a number before calling.

The small tasks help keep a backup available for someone whose travel plans — or mobile phone — have stopped working.

A station record describes one such traveler: After missing the last bus to a nearby village, the traveler used the payphone to arrange a ride. The traveler had a mobile phone, but its battery was exhausted.

That account shows one reason the phone remains useful. It does not explain every caller’s circumstances, and the available records do not establish how many different people rely on it.

“Most days I walk past it carrying something else. Then somebody needs it and the whole point of keeping it becomes obvious,” Fell said.

The phone is mounted beside the staffed information window, not in a separate street booth. A bench nearby gives callers somewhere to set down luggage. The surrounding concourse, however, offers no private, soundproof space for a conversation.

Keeping the phone usable involves more than Fell’s daily attention. Northline Telephone cooperative owns and maintains the unit; neither Fell nor the bus operator owns it. The station pays a small site-service charge under its agreement with the cooperative. The supplied records do not disclose whether this particular phone makes or loses money.

For community worker Eda Rill, its value includes being a service that does not require an existing online account. Rill helps new residents identify practical services they can use without one.

“It is one of the few things here that does not begin by asking you to log in,” Rill said.

Access still has limits. The phone accepts coins and a prepaid calling card sold at the station kiosk, but the kiosk and information window keep different hours. Callers can reach the phone while the concourse is open, not at every hour of the night.

Its place in the station has also changed over time. Two other public phones were removed during earlier concourse renovations because their equipment was no longer supported. A faded rectangle on the wall marks where an older printed telephone directory rack once hung.

Replacement parts remain available for the surviving unit through the cooperative, though there is no indefinite maintenance commitment. As of Sept. 18, the station has no approved plan to remove it during the current renovation work.

The cooperative records completed calls and equipment faults, but it does not collect a public list of callers or their conversations. A call total would not establish a count of distinct users. The cooperative says any review of the station phone would consider use, repairability and the service agreement, rather than call volume alone.

For now, station staff also have a routine for the times when the phone cannot help: They post a dated notice and report the fault, rather than leaving a disconnected receiver looking ready for use.

Alongside the cleaning, the spare paper and the pencil, that notice serves a practical purpose: letting travelers know whether the station’s remaining payphone is an option when they need to make a call.
