Wrenfold Authorizes First Organized Harvest of Public Orchard Fruit

WRENFOLD — The city's parks department has authorized the Orchard Table cooperative to organize this autumn's harvest at the East Fold public orchard, marking the site's first coordinated fruit collection, officials said.

The agreement covers 36 marked fruit trees, not every tree or ornamental planting in the surrounding park, according to the cooperative. Harvest sessions are planned for October, with exact dates depending on fruit condition and volunteer availability.

Under the arrangement, harvested weight will be split three ways: half to partner food pantries, one-quarter to participating volunteers as a group and one-quarter to community cooking sessions. The cooperative will record harvested weight and distribution after each session; no harvest total is available yet.

Parks staff will identify which trees are included in each session and will remain responsible for tree maintenance. The deal is a one-season harvesting arrangement, not a sale of the trees or a transfer of the park to the cooperative, and any continuation next year would require a new agreement based on a postseason review.

"The fruit is already part of the place; the new work is agreeing who gathers it and where it goes," said Vera Linn, an organizer with Orchard Table.

The cooperative said it will rely on its own registered volunteer sessions rather than open the orchard to unrestricted picking. Equipment use and any work above ground level will fall under the parks department's supervised-session rules.

A nearby residents group asked that the orchard remain available for ordinary walks while harvesting is underway. Under the resulting plan, the main path will stay open, with short, marked restrictions permitted around individual work areas during sessions.

"It is a harvest site for a few hours, but it is still our path to the rest of the park," said resident representative Darin Cole.

Partner pantries will decide what quantities they can accept on each delivery day rather than receive a fixed consignment automatically, the cooperative said. It has set aside storage crates and transport funds, though the city has not supplied a commercial value for the fruit it expects to be harvested.

The orchard includes several varieties listed in an old planting record, and identification of some individual trees remains incomplete, according to the cooperative.

The packet describing the program contains no certification that the fruit is organically grown and no nutritional assessment of the planned distribution. Separately, the city's litter removal team has previously cleared fallen fruit from orchard paths, but its records do not establish how much of that food could have been recovered for other use.

The cooperative said it will report on distribution totals and any practical problems to the parks committee after the season ends. The city has not announced plans for additional orchard harvests elsewhere and has not promised that every partner pantry will receive fruit each week.

A local school has offered to make labels for the storage crates, though it is not taking on responsibility for organizing the harvest itself.

The first sessions are expected to begin in early October, pending confirmation of dates by the parks department and the cooperative.
