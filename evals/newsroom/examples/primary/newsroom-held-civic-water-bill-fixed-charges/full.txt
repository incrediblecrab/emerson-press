# Why a Marshbar water bill can rise even when use falls

A Marshbar household can use less water and still owe more under a new tariff because the Water Board is raising the fixed quarterly network charge, not the price of each metered unit.

The fixed charge will rise from $18 to $26 for the residential account category covered by the tariff. The usage charge will remain $1.20 per cubic meter. That means savings from lower consumption can be outweighed by the $8 increase in the fixed charge.

As of Sept. 18, 2026, the change is still ahead. It takes effect for service periods beginning Jan. 1, 2027, not simply for every bill printed after that date.

## How less water can produce a higher bill

The board's worked example shows how the two parts interact. Under the old tariff, a household using 15 cubic meters pays $18 in usage charges. Add the $18 fixed charge, and the bill is $36 before any other items.

In the new period, the illustrative household uses 10 cubic meters. Its usage charge falls to $12, but the fixed charge rises to $26. The total is $38 before other items.

The household uses five fewer cubic meters and saves $6 on the metered portion. But the fixed charge increases by $8, leaving the total $2 higher.

This is an invented example for explaining the tariff, not a disclosed customer's account or a prediction of typical household behavior.

Using less water still saves money compared with using more under the same tariff. At the new rates, 15 cubic meters would produce a $44 bill before other items, compared with $38 for 10 cubic meters.

## What the fixed charge pays for

The board says the fixed charge supports network readiness, maintenance and financing costs that it does not allocate solely according to each household's consumption. Its budget identifies a planned pipe-replacement program and higher financing expenses as reasons for the increase.

The board considered recovering more costs through the usage rate but chose the larger fixed-charge change, according to its recorded decision.

Discussing the specified residential account category, finance director Pella Rane said, "Using less still reduces the metered part, but it cannot remove a charge that is the same for every connected account."

That uniform charge applies to connected residential accounts in the stated category, not every commercial or shared-meter account.

A renters association argues that a larger fixed share gives small households less room to reduce their bills through their own choices.

"People can change a habit more easily than they can change how the network is financed," association organizer Mavin Cole said.

## What to check on a bill

The new layout will list the fixed charge and usage calculation separately. Accounts crossing the tariff start date will show the service periods used in the calculation rather than assume one rate covered the whole quarter.

A prior balance, adjustment or another separately identified item can also affect the amount due. Those items are outside the board's simple example.

Residents sharing a building meter may receive a landlord's allocation rather than a direct utility bill matching this residential example. The tariff announcement does not set every landlord's internal allocation method.

The board's affordability assistance requires a separate application; lower usage does not automatically trigger help. The supplied information gives no eligibility thresholds or assurance that a particular applicant will qualify.

The board says it will publish a tariff worksheet at its customer desk and on its website before the first affected service period.
