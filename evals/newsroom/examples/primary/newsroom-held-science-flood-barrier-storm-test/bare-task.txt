Barrier Holds Firm in First Test Against Hurricane Vera, But Deaths Occur Outside Protection Zone

The Harrington Bay Storm Surge Barrier withstood its first operational test over the weekend, holding back a surge from Hurricane Vera that reached 10 feet (3.1 meters) in unprotected areas while limiting flooding inside the barrier's protection zone to about 16 inches (0.4 meters), officials said Sunday.

No flooding was reported inside the 24-square-kilometer — 24 square miles (62 square kilometers) — zone, which includes 38,000 residents and an estimated $14 billion in assessed property, the Harrington Bay Coastal Authority said.

But the storm proved deadly outside the barrier's reach. Storm surge caused 11 of the 14 deaths attributed to Vera in the Harrington Bay region, and all 11 occurred north of the barrier boundary, in areas the structure was not built to protect. Flooding was also reported at several points north of the boundary.

The barrier closed Oct. 31 as Vera made landfall at Category 2 strength, marking the first time the $4.8 billion structure has been activated since it opened in March after 11 years of planning and construction.

Benjamin Clarke, director of the Harrington Bay Coastal Authority, called the performance a validation of the project. "The barrier performed exactly as modeled," Clarke said. "We can now say with confidence that it will protect the city in storms of this category."

The barrier is designed to withstand storms up to Category 3 strength and surge events with a one-in-100-year probability — a higher bar than Vera's Category 2 landfall presented.

Ingrid Larsen, the authority's chief engineer, said the 30 surge gates reached full closure in 84 minutes after officials made the decision to activate, within the 90-minute design specification. Of the 30 gates, 14 closed fully while the remaining 16 closed to 75%, an approach Larsen said was intended to minimize disruption to water flow while still providing protection.

Larsen said two gate hydraulic actuators showed minor wear that will be addressed during a post-storm inspection but did not affect the gates' performance during the closure. The authority said a full engineering inspection of all gate mechanisms will be completed before the barrier's next activation, which could come at any point in the remainder of the Atlantic hurricane season.

Vera also dumped 5.8 inches (148 millimeters) of rain on the Harrington watershed in six hours. Stormwater drainage systems within the barrier zone operated without significant backup, the authority said.

The barrier's gates normally remain open to allow tidal exchange and close only when surge forecasts exceed a set threshold.

The test comes seven years after Hurricane Petra, a storm of comparable surge intensity, caused $6.8 billion in flood damage to the Harrington Bay area in 2017. A cost-benefit analysis commissioned by the authority in 2021 projected the barrier would prevent $1.2 billion in storm damage annually under median hurricane frequency projections.

The closure was not without environmental consequences. Simon Ward, a marine ecologist at Harrington University, said the 18-hour closure temporarily blocked passage for juvenile fish in the estuary, an effect now under post-storm monitoring. Ward said the larger long-term question is whether repeated closures over the barrier's 50-year design life could alter the estuary's salinity gradients and sediment dynamics.

Clarke said the authority will conduct a lessons-learned review and publish its findings before the 2025 hurricane season begins.

Two other major coastal barrier projects are under construction elsewhere on the East Coast, but neither has yet been tested by a storm, making Harrington Bay's barrier the first of its kind to face a real-world activation.
