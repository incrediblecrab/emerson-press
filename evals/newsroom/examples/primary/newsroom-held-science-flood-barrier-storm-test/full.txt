Barrier Holds in First Test Against Hurricane, Officials Say

HARRINGTON BAY — The Harrington Bay Storm Surge Barrier closed for the first time in an operational activation Oct. 31 as Hurricane Vera struck the coast, and officials said it prevented flooding across the 62-square-kilometer zone it protects.

Vera made landfall at Category 2 strength, producing storm surge of 3.1 meters in unprotected areas to the north. Inside the barrier's protection zone, which includes 38,000 residents and $14 billion in assessed property, surge levels reached only 0.4 meters. No flooding was reported within the protected area.

"The barrier performed exactly as modeled," said Benjamin Clarke, director of the Harrington Bay Coastal Authority. "We can now say with confidence that it will protect the city in storms of this category."

Storm surge caused 11 of the 14 deaths attributed to Vera in the region, and all 11 occurred north of the barrier boundary, outside the protected zone. Flooding was reported at several points in that area during the storm.

Chief engineer Dr. Ingrid Larsen said the barrier's 30 surge gates reached full closure in 84 minutes after the decision to activate, within the design specification of 90 minutes. Fourteen gates closed fully, while the remaining 16 closed to 75%, an approach Larsen said was designed to minimize disruption to water flow while maintaining protection.

Larsen said two gate hydraulic actuators showed minor wear that will be addressed during a post-storm inspection but did not affect performance during the closure. The authority said a full engineering inspection of all gate mechanisms will be completed before the barrier's next activation, which could come at any point in the remaining Atlantic hurricane season.

Vera also dropped 148 millimeters of rain over the Harrington watershed in six hours. Stormwater drainage systems within the barrier zone operated without significant backup, the authority said.

The barrier, which took 11 years to plan and build at a cost of $4.8 billion, opened in March. It is designed to withstand storms up to Category 3 strength and surge events with a one-in-100-year probability. Gates are normally kept open to allow tidal exchange and close only when surge forecasts exceed a defined threshold.

A 2021 cost-benefit analysis commissioned by the authority estimated the barrier would prevent $1.2 billion in storm damage annually under median hurricane frequency projections. Hurricane Petra, a storm of comparable surge intensity, caused $6.8 billion in flood damage to the Harrington Bay area in 2017.

Not all effects of the closure were contained to engineering performance. Dr. Simon Ward, a marine ecologist at Harrington University, said the barrier's 18-hour closure temporarily blocked juvenile fish passage in the estuary. The ecological effect is under post-storm monitoring, he said. Ward said the longer-term question is whether repeated closures over the barrier's 50-year design life will alter the estuary's salinity gradients and sediment dynamics.

Clarke said the authority will conduct a lessons-learned review and publish its findings before the 2025 hurricane season.

Two other major coastal barrier projects are under construction in the eastern United States. Neither has yet been tested operationally by a storm.
