# Caldwell weighs flood protection against estuary’s uncertain future

The proposed Caldwell Estuary Tidal Gate would protect 55,000 residents from storm surges, its chief engineer says. But the $3.1 billion barrier would also change the estuary in ways its designers cannot fully predict, leaving Caldwell City to weigh protection from flooding against risks to the species that live there.

The project is under final engineering review, with a decision on construction authorization expected in 2026. For engineers, ecologists and city leaders, the debate turns not only on whether the barrier can hold back water, but also on who will act if it harms the estuary.

Sara Oduya, the chief engineer who has spent seven years on the project, said the barrier would protect residents from surge events up to 3.5 meters, or about 11.5 feet. Its effects on tidal exchange are harder to model.

“We can tell you what the gate does to water levels. We can tell you a lot less about what it does to species that evolved in this estuary over ten thousand years,” Oduya said. “That uncertainty is real and we are honest about it.”

Marine ecologist Patrick Lund, who has studied the estuary for four years, said the barrier would reduce tidal flushing by approximately 30% during closures. Under future climate scenarios, those closures are projected to occur six to 10 times a year.

Less flushing would mean higher salinity in the upper estuary during dry periods and lower oxygen during wet ones, Lund said. The available data cannot establish how every important species would respond.

The estuary supports two species of conservation concern: the Caldwell silverside fish and a migratory shorebird that forages in the intertidal zone. The environmental impact statement concluded that both would face adverse effects, but that their survival would not likely be jeopardized. Three outside ecologists who reviewed the statement said its population models lacked sufficient baseline data.

Lund said the environmental review was compressed to meet political timelines.

“We needed three more years of baseline data. We got one,” he said.

A coalition of environmental groups has filed for an extended review period. The federal agency overseeing the review has not responded.

Mayor Anna Rios says the city cannot wait for perfect ecological certainty.

“We had $890 million in flood damage from the 2018 storm,” Rios said. “At some point, you build the thing, or you accept the losses.”

A local business association estimated that uninsured flood exposure — costs not covered by federal disaster funds — exceeded $200 million in that event. By its estimate, insurance savings would recover the barrier’s construction cost within 25 years.

Oduya said operational protocols would minimize closure times and maintain fish passage through fish locks. She acknowledged those measures are mitigations, not solutions.

The project’s mitigation package also includes a 50-year ecological monitoring commitment, an endowment for species research and adaptive management provisions allowing gate operations to change in response to monitoring data. Oduya called it the strongest achievable package.

Lund said he would not oppose the project if monitoring and adaptive management requirements are binding.

“Adaptive management is a promise,” he said. “It’s only as good as the institution keeping it over fifty years.”

Oduya said the final engineering review will take six months. She hopes the ecological and infrastructure questions can be resolved in parallel.

“The engineering is largely settled,” she said. “The governance — who decides what the monitoring means and what to do about it — that is not settled.”

Coastal engineers and ecologists in six other U.S. cities are watching. Caldwell’s outcome will influence how they frame environmental reviews of their own barrier proposals.
