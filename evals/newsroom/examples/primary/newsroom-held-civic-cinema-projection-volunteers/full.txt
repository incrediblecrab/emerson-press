# Before the first reel, Cinder Room volunteers prepare for a mixed-format season

Hessa Vane checks the condition of a film print's leader and compares the reel labels before a scheduled practice session at the Cinder Room cinema. Inside the community hall, the projector makes its intermittent mechanical sound, its beam catching dust above the empty chairs.

“A reel has a beginning before the audience sees the beginning. That is where a lot of our attention goes,” said Vane, a projection volunteer.

The Cinder Room is preparing a season that will combine digital screenings with films available on 16 mm prints. For its 12 volunteers, the work involves repairs, practice and bookings shaped by which films they can secure permission and equipment to show. Three members are trained to operate the film projector; the others manage tickets, seating and refreshments.

As of Sept. 18, preparations are still underway. The group plans an open rehearsal demonstration before the season begins, but its date must be worked out around the hall's other bookings.

A specialist contractor inspected the projector and supplied a repair list. A $5,400 community grant covers that listed work and replacement parts. It does not cover all future film-hire and screening costs, and the volunteers are not presenting the project as a machine they rebuilt entirely themselves.

Nor is the film projector replacing digital equipment. Programs supplied digitally will continue to be shown that way. Booking coordinator Perin Cole said the choice of format depends partly on available rights, copies and equipment, rather than aesthetic preference alone.

“We start with what we can actually show to a room of people, not with a list of titles we wish we owned,” Cole said.

One title in the coming season is “The Orchard Window.” A print and permission to screen it have been arranged through its distributor. Those arrangements are part of the preparation alongside the work on the projector: having a title in mind is different from having a copy the cinema is authorized and equipped to show.

The shared hall sets another practical limit. Ordinary community bookings restrict the time available for practice, and the cinema team must put its equipment and seating away afterward. Responsibility is divided, too: The caretaker remains responsible for the building's systems, while the volunteers handle their agreed screening equipment.

Recruitment centers on learning a reliable sequence with another operator present before taking responsibility for a public screening. The group also keeps an incident log when a screening is interrupted, giving the next operator an account of what happened instead of leaving them to rely on someone else's memory. A labeled cabinet holds spare lamps and maintenance records separately from the reels.

The team makes no promise that a restored projector or a nostalgic format will bring a larger audience. Attendance last season varied by program, with no comparative audience study available to establish a difference between film and digital presentation.

Near the entrance, an old ticket roll is on display. Current tickets use the hall's present booking system. For now, the new season is still in preparation, with attention on the equipment, the available practice time and the reel labels Vane checks before the chairs fill.
