# Before the first reel, Cinder Room volunteers prepare for a mixed-format season

At the Cinder Room cinema, the work of showing a film begins before an audience takes its seats. Projection volunteer Hessa Vane checks the condition of a print’s leader and compares reel labels ahead of a scheduled practice session.

The projector makes its intermittent mechanical sound, and its beam catches dust above empty chairs. Inside the community hall that houses the cinema, those preparations are part of getting ready for a season that will combine digital screenings with films available on 16 mm prints.

“A reel has a beginning before the audience sees the beginning,” Vane said. “That is where a lot of our attention goes.”

As of Sept. 18, the volunteer team is preparing equipment and operators, not celebrating a completed opening screening. Of its 12 members, three are trained to operate the film projector. Others handle tickets, seating and refreshments — the work that turns a projection into a public event.

The projector’s repair work has specialist support. A contractor inspected the machine and supplied a list of repairs. A $5,400 community grant covers that work and replacement parts, but it does not cover all future film rental and screening costs. The volunteers are not claiming to have rebuilt every component themselves.

Nor is the return to film an abandonment of digital projection. Programs supplied digitally will continue to be shown that way. Booking coordinator Perin Cole says the choice of format depends partly on screening rights, available copies and equipment, rather than aesthetic preference alone.

“We start with what we can actually show to a room of people, not with a list of titles we wish we owned,” Cole said.

One title in the coming season is “The Orchard Window,” for which a print and screening permission have been arranged through its distributor. Securing both is part of preparing a program the cinema can actually present.

The hall’s other uses shape the work, too. Ordinary community bookings limit opportunities to practice, and the cinema team must put equipment and seating away afterward. The group plans an open rehearsal demonstration before the season begins, but its date remains subject to hall bookings.

Recruitment focuses on learning a reliable operating sequence with another operator present before a volunteer takes responsibility for a public screening. With only three trained film operators, that preparation is a distinct part of the team’s work alongside its front-of-house duties.

A labeled cabinet holds spare lamps and maintenance records separately from the reels. When a screening is interrupted, the group keeps an incident log so the next operator can find out what happened without depending on someone’s memory. Responsibilities also have boundaries: The hall caretaker looks after the building’s systems, while the cinema group handles its agreed screening equipment.

Near the entrance, an old ticket roll is on display. Current tickets, however, go through the hall’s present booking system — another point where the cinema’s older equipment sits alongside current practice.

The team makes no promise that a repaired projector or a nostalgic format will bring larger audiences. Attendance varied by program last season, and there is no comparative audience study establishing an advantage for film over digital presentation.

For now, the preparations center on what the volunteers can control: checking prints, arranging permissions, learning the equipment and fitting the work around a shared hall’s schedule. The audience’s beginning is still ahead.
