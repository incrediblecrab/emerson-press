# Why a library e-book can have a waiting list even when the file never wears out

A digital book can reach a reader without a delivery truck or a trip to a library shelf. But at Talver Library, 27 accounts are waiting for Oren Vale’s novel “A Map of Last Ferries” — not because the file is wearing out, but because permission to lend it is limited.

As of Aug. 17, the library holds three active digital licenses for the novel. Each allows only one borrower at a time. That makes three simultaneous readers the limit, regardless of how easily a digital file could be transmitted.

“The file can reach my house instantly. The permission to borrow it is what I am waiting for,” said reader Neri Moss.

The distinction between a file and a lending license explains both the queue and a recurring expense for the library.

## What does the library buy?

For this title, each license costs $72 and expires after 24 loans or two years, whichever comes first. The library’s initial $216 purchase of three licenses therefore permits at most 72 completed loans before the loan-count limits require replacements. The time limit could end those permissions sooner.

The licenses are already approaching their loan-count limits. One has been used for 21 loans and the other two for 20 loans each, leaving three, four and four uses, respectively.

That is 11 remaining uses under the count limits — fewer than the 27 accounts currently waiting. The existing permissions cannot cover that entire queue without replacement or additional licenses.

Those limits come from this novel’s contract. Other publishers and titles use different licensing models; a 24-loan or two-year expiration is not a universal rule for library e-books.

## Why not just buy another copy?

The library can buy another concurrently active license to let an additional person read at the same time. But that requires more money, and replacing an expiring license does not automatically expand simultaneous lending capacity.

“Renewing what we already had and adding another copy are two different purchases,” librarian Iva Drel said.

Drel said popular titles can consume money both through added capacity and through license renewal. Talver’s annual digital-material budget is $42,000, which also covers databases and other titles.

The publisher says licensing receipts support rights payments and distribution. It has not provided title-level costs or margins, so the available figures do not establish how much it earns from this novel’s library licenses.

## How long will the wait be?

The normal digital loan lasts 14 days. But the 27-account queue does not translate into one exact wait for every reader.

Borrowers may return the book early, and unclaimed reservations can expire. The licenses’ remaining uses and any purchases to renew or expand access also matter. The queue describes demand, not a guaranteed delivery date.

## Is print an equivalent alternative?

A print copy costs the library $18 before processing expenses and can circulate until it needs replacement, rather than expiring at a contractual loan count.

But that lower purchase price does not make print interchangeable with digital access. A physical copy is not remotely available in the same way, and travel to a branch is a barrier for some readers. Moss uses enlarged digital text and says print is not an equivalent substitute for her needs.

Talver has lent e-books since 2011 and retains home delivery for some physical materials. Neither changes the central constraint on this title: The file does not wear out, but the permission to lend it runs out.
