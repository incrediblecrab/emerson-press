# Why a library e-book can have a waiting list

A digital file does not wear out, but a library's permission to lend it can run out. At Talver Library, readers waiting for an e-book are waiting for access under a contract that limits how many people can borrow it at once and how often each license can be used.

As of Aug. 17, 27 accounts are waiting for Oren Vale's novel "A Map of Last Ferries." The library has three active digital licenses for the title. Each allows one borrower at a time, so the library can lend the e-book to only three accounts simultaneously.

The normal loan lasts 14 days. That does not make the queue a reliable countdown for every reader: Borrowers may return the book early, and unclaimed reservations can expire. The 27 waiting accounts do not establish one exact waiting time.

For reader Neri Moss, the distinction between the file and the right to borrow it is the source of frustration.

"The file can reach my house instantly. The permission to borrow it is what I am waiting for," Moss said.

## What the library buys

For this novel, each digital license costs $72 and expires after 24 loans or two years, whichever comes first. The library's initial $216 purchase of three licenses therefore permits at most 72 completed loans before the loan-count limits require replacements. The time limit could end a license sooner.

Those limits are already approaching. The three licenses have been used for 21, 20 and 20 loans, leaving three, four and four uses under their respective loan-count limits — 11 in total.

That remaining allowance is smaller than the 27-account queue. It also illustrates two separate constraints: how many readers can borrow at once and how many more loans the existing licenses permit.

Replacing a license restores permission that is expiring. It does not automatically add another simultaneous lending slot. Buying another concurrently active license would increase capacity, but that requires additional budget.

"Renewing what we already had and adding another copy are two different purchases," librarian Iva Drel said.

Drel said popular titles can consume money through both purchases. Talver's annual digital-material budget is $42,000, which also pays for databases and other titles.

## Why print is a different choice

A print copy of the same novel costs the library $18 before processing expenses and can circulate until it needs replacement. Unlike this e-book license, it has no specified 24-loan or two-year expiration.

But the lower purchase price does not make print an equivalent option for every reader. A physical copy is not remotely available in the same way, and getting to a branch is a barrier for some readers. Moss uses enlarged digital text and says print does not meet her reading needs in the same way.

Talver, which began lending e-books in 2011, also retains a home-delivery service for some physical materials.

The publisher says licensing receipts support rights payments and distribution. It has not supplied title-level costs or margins.

Other publishers and titles use different licensing models; the terms for "A Map of Last Ferries" are not universal e-book rules. For this title, though, an intact digital file is only part of what lending requires. The library also needs an active license with a loan use remaining and a borrowing slot available.
