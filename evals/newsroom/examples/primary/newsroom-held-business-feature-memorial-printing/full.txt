**In Fernhollow, a Small Print Shop Builds Time Into Every Memorial Job**

FERNHOLLOW — The proof sat untouched on a shelf at Plain Bower Print for most of a week in June, waiting on one thing: a family had to confirm how a name was spelled before the shop would run the full job.

That pause, recorded in the shop's production notes, is not an exception at the four-person print business, which produces memorial programs, community notices and short-run event booklets. It is close to standard procedure.

"The deadline is usually fixed before we see the first page, so the checking has to have its own place in the schedule," said Leda Norven, the shop's owner.

Memorial orders typically run between 50 and 150 copies, according to the shop's job register for the first six months of 2026. A standard 100-copy folded program package costs $90 and includes one proof and one ordinary correction round. Additional pages, special paper and any changes made after printing begins are quoted separately, and are not part of every order.

The shop requires written approval — by email or on a marked-up paper proof — before it will produce the full run. Families can bring in a handwritten draft and photographs, though the shop charges separately when the work requires substantial scanning or layout.

Oren Mallen, who organized a family memorial this year, said he chose a plain folded design partly because it made the approval process easier among relatives who were reviewing it together.

"The paper proof let us point to the same line instead of describing it over the phone," Mallen said.

The shop's register shows 42 memorial orders between January and June of this year, compared with 29 in the second half of 2025. Norven attributed part of the shift to changes in referrals from two local service organizers during that period. The figures describe the shop's own workload; they do not by themselves indicate any change in local deaths or funeral attendance, and the shop has not supplied a breakdown of profit separating memorial jobs from its other short-run printing.

Rush requests are common, Norven said, and can displace less time-sensitive jobs already in the queue. But she said the shop does not promise to accept every same-day order, given the checking a memorial program requires even under pressure.

That checking extends past the initial run. The shop keeps approved production files for 30 days after printing, in case a family orders a reprint, then deletes them under its stated retention policy. If a reprint is ordered, staff use the version the family already approved unless a further correction is explicitly agreed to first — a later phone call alone, Norven said, does not substitute for a marked proof.

At the front counter, a rack of paper samples remains from the shop's earlier stationery business, which once handled weddings. The shop's current work sits closer to the other end of family record-keeping, one program at a time, checked against a name, a date, sometimes a single line a family wants to be sure is right before it is set in ink for good.
