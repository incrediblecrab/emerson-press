**DOE awards $14.2 billion contract for record U.S. offshore wind project**

WASHINGTON — The Department of Energy on Thursday awarded a $14.2 billion contract to Horizon Marine Energy to build an offshore wind array off the Cape Hartley coast, the largest single renewable energy development award in U.S. history, according to the department.

The project calls for 280 turbines rated at 15 megawatts each, with a combined nameplate capacity of 4,200 megawatts, located 80 kilometers offshore. The turbine class is not yet in commercial deployment; it is scheduled to enter production in 2026.

"This contract moves the United States from a follower to a leader in offshore wind," Energy Secretary Isabel Makin said. "At full output, it will power more than 1.8 million homes."

Independent analysts said offshore wind capacity factors typically range from 40% to 55%. At the low end of that range, the array would generate an average of about 1,680 megawatts of actual power, well below its nameplate capacity.

Horizon CEO Peter Strand said the company expects to begin offshore foundation installation in early 2027 and deliver first power to the grid by mid-2029. The contract includes financial penalties if the project fails to deliver commercial power by December 2030.

The array will connect to the mainland grid through a 92-kilometer undersea high-voltage direct current cable. Three commercial fishing associations said portions of the cable route cross productive lobster grounds and called for an independent impact assessment before construction begins.

Cape Hartley sits in a wind resource area that NOAA classifies as Excellent, with average offshore wind speeds of 9.2 meters per second at hub height. Regulators approved the project area under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act following a three-year environmental review.

The contract requires Horizon to meet domestic content requirements for at least 50% of major components, a threshold the department said will require substantial new U.S. manufacturing. Horizon has partnered with Strandvik Turbine Systems, a Norwegian manufacturer, to build the turbines at a new plant in Portview, Maine. The company has also contracted for two purpose-built installation vessels from a shipyard in Gulfport, saying the 15-megawatt turbine class requires vessels larger than any now operating in U.S. waters.

The department estimates the project will support 7,400 construction jobs and 600 permanent operations positions at peak activity. Horizon will pay the federal government $420 million in annual lease fees once the array is operational.

The coastal state's legislature passed a complementary grid integration bill in April requiring utilities to build additional onshore transmission to accommodate the new generation.

Strand said Horizon has $6.1 billion in committed financing and is seeking the remainder through a federal loan guarantee program.

As of May 2025, the U.S. has 3,800 megawatts of offshore wind operating, all on smaller arrays built along the Northeast coast. The Cape Hartley project would more than double that total once completed.
