# Behind a temporary venue, freelancers piece together a working day

After the audience had left a community concert in July, Foldstage Crew still had work to do. A work note records the crew labeling dismantled ticket-booth panels — the venue reduced to components that could be stored and used again.

That task sits at the less visible end of Foldstage’s business: making a temporary venue function for an evening, then taking it away. Public opening hours account for only part of the work. Crew bookings also cover delivery, setup, checks and removal.

“An empty square can mean nobody came, or it can mean the crew has already put the whole venue back in the van,” booking coordinator Neri Valen said.

Foldstage is a shared booking desk for 11 freelance event workers, not a company employing all its members on fixed weekly schedules. Members handle ticket booths, barriers, signs and front-of-house layouts for small events. Specialist rigging is contracted separately.

The arrangement brings workers together for bookings without guaranteeing them a minimum number of paid days. A shared availability calendar helps the desk coordinate who can take a job, but members must fit those commitments around other work.

Ceri Dorne combines Foldstage bookings with part-time employment at a furniture warehouse. A late change to an event’s finishing time can conflict with the next morning’s separate job, Dorne says.

“The audience buys an evening. I have to fit that evening between two other working days,” Dorne said.

For organizers, the distinction between an evening’s entertainment and a full crew booking matters to the budget. Foldstage’s quoted labor rate is $180 per worker for up to eight agreed working hours. Extra time is billed under the individual booking’s terms. The group does not publish a universal rate for transport, accommodation or specialist equipment.

Nor does every payment attached to a booking become crew wages. The reusable booth panels belong to three members and are hired to bookings through a separate equipment charge. That charge is not automatically divided among everyone working the event.

The desk’s June record illustrates another limit of the shared arrangement. It completed five bookings and lost a sixth when the organizer canceled before setup. A deposit covered reserved vehicle costs, but it did not replace all the income the crew had expected.

Valen says cancellation terms depend on when resources and workers were committed. In the canceled booking, covering a transport commitment and replacing lost earnings were different things.

A community organizer says knowing the full setup and removal cost helps in comparing an existing hall with a temporary venue. The comparison requires more than the hours when an audience is present. The reporting record contains no complete profit account and does not establish that temporary events are always cheaper than permanent venues.

Back at Foldstage’s store, the physical pieces have assigned places. Signs and booth panels are kept in a former coach garage, where painted outlines show where components belong.

The group has discussed a larger shared store. As of Sept. 8, it had neither signed a lease nor agreed on how to divide the cost. Like the panels labeled after July’s concert, storage is part of the work beyond the public opening hours — and another expense to settle before the next temporary venue takes shape.
