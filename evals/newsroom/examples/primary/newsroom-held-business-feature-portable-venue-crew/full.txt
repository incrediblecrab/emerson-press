# Behind an evening’s venue, freelancers juggle hours and costs

After the audience had left a community concert in July, Foldstage Crew was labeling dismantled ticket-booth panels. The task, recorded in a work note, came after the public part of the event was over, with the temporary venue being taken apart for reuse.

“An empty square can mean nobody came, or it can mean the crew has already put the whole venue back in the van,” booking coordinator Neri Valen said.

Foldstage is a shared booking desk for 11 freelance event workers, not a company employing all its members on fixed weekly schedules. Its bookings bring together the people and equipment needed to make a temporary venue work for an evening. The work extends beyond opening hours, and the money must cover commitments that can begin before setup and survive a cancellation.

Members handle ticket booths, barriers, signs and small-event front-of-house layouts. Specialist rigging is contracted separately. Organizers often see only the hours when an event is open to the public, while crew bookings also cover delivery, setup, checks and removal.

The quoted labor rate is $180 per worker for up to eight agreed working hours. Extra time is billed under the terms of the individual booking. Foldstage does not publish a universal rate for transport, accommodation or specialist equipment, so the labor quote alone does not describe the full cost of putting on an event.

The reusable booth panels have their own financial arrangement. Three members own them and hire them to bookings through a separate equipment charge. That charge is not automatically divided as wages among everyone working on the crew.

A June cancellation shows how a booking can leave some costs covered and some expected income lost. The shared desk completed five bookings that month but lost a sixth when the organizer canceled before setup. A deposit covered reserved vehicle costs; it did not replace all the income the crew had expected.

Valen said individual cancellation terms depend on when resources and workers were committed.

For worker Ceri Dorne, fitting an event into the calendar also means accounting for work outside Foldstage. Dorne combines crew bookings with part-time employment at a furniture warehouse and says a late change to an event’s finishing time can conflict with the next morning’s separate job.

“The audience buys an evening. I have to fit that evening between two other working days,” Dorne said.

The desk keeps a shared availability calendar, but it cannot guarantee members a minimum number of paid days. A calendar can establish who is available without securing how much work each person will receive.

Foldstage has discussed a larger shared store, though it has neither signed a lease nor agreed how the cost would be divided. The available record contains no complete profit account, leaving the group’s overall profitability unclear.

For now, the signs and booth panels are stored in a former coach garage. Painted outlines show where the components belong.
