Small New Mexico Town Divided as Nuclear Waste Site Wins Construction License

RIDGEMONT, N.M. — For 30 years, this fading town of 3,800 people has argued with itself over a hole in the ground 22 miles away. This month, the argument reached a turning point: The Nuclear Regulatory Authority granted a construction license for the Granite Basin deep geological repository, clearing the way for a facility meant to permanently store nuclear waste.

The decision did not settle anything in Ridgemont. It merely confirmed which side each resident had chosen years ago.

Claudette Moreau, 72, a retired teacher, has opposed the project since it was first proposed in 1994. She founded a citizens' group that has filed seven administrative challenges over three decades.

"My students graduated and moved away. I stayed because someone has to," Moreau said. "In thirty years of fighting this, I have not changed one vote. But I have made sure they were slow."

Eight miles from Moreau's home, rancher Hector Vega, 55, whose property borders the site boundary, has cautiously supported the project since 2019, when the government tied a $140 million economic development package to the repository's approval.

"I don't love having a nuclear waste facility next door," Vega said. "But I live next door to poverty too. And poverty doesn't come with $140 million in infrastructure money."

That package includes a new highway bypass, upgrades to the water treatment plant and a job training center. County officials estimate the repository will directly employ 180 people at an average salary of $68,000, with 400 temporary construction jobs preceding it. Permanent operations are expected to phase in by 2032, when waste acceptance begins.

The numbers matter in a town that has lost its grocery store, hardware store and two restaurants since 2015. Ridgemont's population has fallen 22% since 2000, and school enrollment is down 35%. County officials say that without the repository, the fiscal trajectory is continued decline.

The Ridgemont County Commission voted 4-1 to accept the development package, according to Commission Chair Leticia Durán. The dissenting vote cited concerns about transport risk along the county's two-lane state highway. But according to the licensing record, waste shipments will travel by existing rail lines, not the county highway.

Opposition has also come from the Kwena Nation, whose members hold treaty rights to land within 30 kilometers of the site. Tribal liaison James Silvercloud said the tribe was never meaningfully consulted, only notified.

"There is a difference between being consulted and being told what has already been decided," Silvercloud said. "We have always been told."

Silvercloud said the tribe intends to pursue legal remedies, though he does not expect to prevail. He said the effort is meant to build a record of inadequate consultation for future advocates.

The underlying science remains contested. The Nuclear Regulatory Authority found that Granite Basin's geology meets long-term isolation standards, a conclusion backed by three independent geologists who reviewed the record. Two others published dissenting papers questioning the water infiltration modeling used to reach that finding.

Moreau said she has made peace with the limits of her fight. "At my age, winning means the record is correct," she said. "It means someone reads these filings in a hundred years and knows this was not done with consent." She has voted in every election for 54 years and said she intends to keep doing so: "That's the only power I have. I'll use it."

Vega, meanwhile, said he plans to retire before waste deliveries begin, leaving the ranch's future to his son. "By then, my son will decide what comes next for the ranch," he said. "It's his problem now. I made it survivable for him to have a problem."

Despite living just eight miles apart and standing on opposite sides of the town's defining issue, Moreau and Vega have never met in person. Ridgemont's long debate over the repository has played out largely through public hearings and county newsletters — never in direct conversation between the two.
