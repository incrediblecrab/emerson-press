Rillhaven Rink Trades Skates for Stalls as Winter Market Takes Shape

By staff report

RILLHAVEN — The scrape of folding tables has replaced the whir of roller skates at the Rillhaven community rink this week, as workers convert the building into its seasonal indoor winter market.

The rink, which hosts roller skating for part of the year, is not being permanently turned into retail space. Under the community trust's schedule, the building shifts to market use for a set winter period before reverting to skating next season — a reverse conversion that will require its own preparation schedule, according to trust records.

Caretaker Oren Venn spent Wednesday labeling floor panels as a crew carried them in from storage, part of a sequence designed so the removable protective flooring can be fitted now and taken up again later without confusion.

"The room changes jobs, but it is still the same room," Venn said. "We have to leave ourselves a way to turn it back."

The transformation is visible in layers. A skate rail still runs above stacks of folded tables. A row of old skate sizes remains painted above the former hire counter, which is becoming the market's information point. Workers were marking out market aisles even as remnants of the rink's old identity remained in place.

The conversion involves more than laying down flooring. Venn's checklist includes revised entrances, public signage and toilet access, along with a stall layout designed to keep agreed public routes clear. The building's access assessment is being reviewed specifically for the market configuration, since organizers say a route that works for skating spectators is not the same as one that works for rows of stalls.

Storage space has emerged as its own logistical puzzle. Rails, panels and tables must be inventoried carefully, Venn said, so the rink equipment isn't buried behind the market's own supplies — a problem that would complicate the eventual switch back.

Market traders will operate under assigned pitches and equipment arrangements rather than set up wherever floor space looks open, under the trust's existing vendor rules. The market will include food and craft stalls under the trust's current vendor arrangements; it is not becoming a general retail operation run by the caretaker.

Financially, stall fees will contribute to the building's winter operating costs under the trust's budget. Trust records do not indicate that those fees cover every annual cost of running the building, nor that they make all skating sessions financially self-sufficient on their own.

For regular skater Elin Raye, the changeover carries a personal note. Raye still helps move portable barriers during the conversion but said she would prefer a longer indoor skating season.

"Thursday is still skating night in my head, even when the room has tables in it," Raye said.

The trust has discussed the possibility of occasional winter skating sessions but has not promised any within the current market layout, and it maintains separate booking lists for seasonal skating and market use rather than treating the two as interchangeable.

Trust officials have not supplied a final opening date for the market in current work records. Organizers say the first market day will follow completion of all conversion checks, rather than simply the delivery of the last table.

No figures were available on how the seasonal switch affects townwide retail spending or public physical activity levels.
