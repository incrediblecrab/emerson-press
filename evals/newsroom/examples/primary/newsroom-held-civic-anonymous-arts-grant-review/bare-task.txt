# How anonymous arts grant reviews work — and where their limits lie

The Briar Coast Arts Fund removes applicants’ names from the project descriptions its panel first reviews, but that does not mean every proposal is unrecognizable or that the entire grant process is anonymous.

The distinction matters as the fund considers 34 eligible applications for up to 10 awards from a $100,000 allocation. As of Sept. 18, 2026, the round has not reached final award acceptance. There is no confirmed list of winners.

Here is how the first-stage review works, what happens when a panelist recognizes an applicant and what remains to be checked before an award is final.

## Who reaches the panel?

The fund received 46 applications. Of those, 12 did not advance under the published eligibility and completeness rules. Those applications were screened out before artistic panel ranking; they were not eligible proposals that lost in competition with other projects.

The remaining 34 applications go to a five-member panel. Applicants may propose workshops, performances, exhibitions or other public arts activities within the same round.

Passing the eligibility check allows a proposal to be considered. It does not guarantee funding.

## What does the panel see?

At the first stage, panelists review project descriptions with applicants’ names and direct organizational identifiers removed. The papers use reference numbers so panel comments can be matched to the correct application without displaying a name.

The panel considers the proposed public activity, feasibility, artistic purpose and the relationship between the budget and the work described. The fund separates proposed project grant spending from applicants’ unrelated organizational income when checking budgets.

The selection is not a public popularity vote, and panelists do not choose projects by counting the supporters an applicant recruits.

Staff still hold identifying information to check eligibility and communicate with applicants. Anonymity therefore applies to the first-stage panel materials, not to everyone involved in administering the grants.

## What if a panelist recognizes someone?

Removing a name cannot erase every identifying clue. A distinctive earlier project or a proposed venue may allow a panelist to work out who is applying.

Fund director Elva Norr described that limit: “We can remove the name on the page. We cannot promise that a small town makes every project unrecognizable.”

When panelists recognize a relevant connection, they must disclose it and follow the fund’s recusal procedure. The procedure records recusals and involves the remaining panel members in the relevant decision rather than treating the connection as though it did not exist.

Artist Rena Vale said, “The useful question for me is how a connection is handled after someone notices it.”

The fund has not supplied evidence that removing names eliminates every form of bias or changes the award rate for any particular group. The anonymous stage should not be read as proof of either outcome.

## What happens before and after an award?

Before final awards, staff check an applicant’s capacity to accept the grant and the practical conditions attached to the proposed activity. Even a strong first-stage proposal may need clarification before an award can be made.

Unsuccessful applicants will receive feedback distinguishing eligibility problems from panel judgments about eligible proposals.

Once awards are accepted, the fund will publish recipients and funded activities. The first-stage anonymity is not a promise of permanent secrecy.

After the round, the fund plans to review the process using feedback from staff, panelists and applicants, rather than judging it only by the eventual recipient list.
