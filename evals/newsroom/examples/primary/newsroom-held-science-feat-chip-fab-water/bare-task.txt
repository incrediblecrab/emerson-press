# Arizona chip plant raises a desert question: Who gets the water?

For 38 years, Jack Dunbar has farmed 3,200 acres in Arizona’s Sonoran Valley. In 2022, his water allocation was cut 18% to help meet downstream Colorado River commitments. Over the past three years, he has reduced his alfalfa acreage by 600 acres.

Now, Nexus Semiconductor is building a chip factory in the valley that will consume up to 14 million liters of ultra-pure water a day at full production.

“I gave up water so the valley could survive. Now they're giving it to a chip factory. That's a choice somebody made. Nobody asked me,” said Dunbar, 64.

Nexus broke ground in January on a project state economic development officials say represents $6.8 billion in direct investment. The facility promises 2,400 jobs with wages averaging $78,000 a year, in a county where median household income is $48,000. The figures measure different things — individual wages and household income — but illustrate the scale of the economic opportunity.

They also sharpen a debate about allocating scarce water in a valley that averages 28 centimeters of rainfall annually and depends on Colorado River allocations reduced three times since 2019.

State officials say the plant could anchor a semiconductor cluster and attract more companies. They describe water as a solvable engineering problem, not a reason to forgo investment.

Nexus’s state water allocation is contingent on achieving 80% recycling efficiency within two years of full production, a target the company says the facility is designed to meet.

Sofia Reyes, Nexus’s water management director, said the facility’s closed-loop ultra-pure water system recycles 83% of water used in chip rinsing. The remainder evaporates or is treated to municipal discharge standards. That chip-rinsing figure describes a specific process, rather than establishing the plant’s overall recycling performance.

“We are not a conventional industrial water user. Our economic output per liter of water consumed is higher than virtually any other use, including agriculture. That comparison matters in a scarcity context,” Reyes said.

Reyes said proximity to semiconductor supply chain partners in Phoenix helped determine the location. Water was a known risk that Nexus engineers said they could manage.

Mira Espinoza, a water policy researcher at Sonoran University, said the economic-value argument is real but incomplete. It leaves out the cultural and food security value of agricultural water rights that predate the facility, she said.

“The state is betting that new industrial users will be more efficient than legacy agriculture. Historically, that bet has sometimes not paid off,” Espinoza said.

She called the recycling target technically achievable but operationally challenging, noting that chip facilities often run below target efficiency during ramp-up and maintenance.

The permit requires quarterly water use reporting and allows regulators to reduce Nexus’s allocation if recycling efficiency falls below 75%. It does not specify what happens to the facility if regulators impose that reduction.

A state water official said the recycling requirement is stricter than any previously applied to an Arizona industrial user. The official declined to comment on enforcement precedent.

Nexus has also agreed to fund restoration of 4,200 acre-feet of Colorado River flows through a conservation-purchase program. The supplied agreement figure has no stated time frame, limiting comparison with projected daily plant consumption.

Dunbar says he is not opposed to semiconductor manufacturing in principle, but questions its place in the desert Southwest. He is studying whether drip-irrigated specialty crops could work on some of his land, a transition he calls expensive and uncertain. Two neighbors have sold to residential developers in the past year.

For Espinoza, those choices are inseparable from the engineering.

“Scarcity is not only a technical problem. It is a question of who gets less.”
