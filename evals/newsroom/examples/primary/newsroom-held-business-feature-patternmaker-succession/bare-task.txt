# At Merrin Patterns, a digital future needs more than the shape of the past

In an Aug. 4 workshop note, Ila Merrin was checking a wooden pump-housing pattern against a paper drawing marked with handwritten allowances. The task captures a problem facing her small business: Moving a shape into a digital file does not necessarily preserve everything needed to use it.

Merrin, who opened Merrin Patterns in 1988, plans to cut her working week from five days to three in 2027. She intends to keep taking orders, not announce a closing sale. But as of Sept. 8, there is no completed sale or guaranteed successor, and a scanning pilot has shown that preserving the workshop’s work requires more than recording its wooden forms.

“The shape can go into a file. The reason for that extra lip still needs to go with it,” Merrin said.

The workshop makes patterns that foundries use to form molds for casting replacement pump parts and other industrial components. The patterns are not the finished metal parts. Their dimensions account for requirements of casting and subsequent machining — distinctions that make the instructions accompanying a pattern important to its reproduction.

Merrin Patterns occupies two rooms behind a former agricultural dealership, with customer names painted on storage racks. It holds 120 active customer patterns, along with a separate shelf of retired patterns retained for reference.

A pilot scanned 14 patterns for which permission had been obtained. Ten could be used with existing written instructions. Four needed further annotations about casting allowances and machining references, information beyond the visible outside shape.

Those results do not establish that every stored pattern can be reproduced accurately from a scan alone. They instead identify part of the work that must accompany conversion: making sure the file is supported by the information needed to interpret it.

One of the workshop’s two assistants, Toma Venn, joined 18 months ago from a general machining job. Venn keeps a job notebook linking customer drawings to changes made after trial castings. Those links document not just what changed, but the casting work associated with the revision.

“I am not learning a secret by watching once. I am learning which question to ask before cutting,” Venn said.

That learning is part of the succession problem, even if the workshop obtains digital copies of its patterns. A stored shape and the judgment needed to work from it are not the same thing.

Permission and payment also remain unresolved parts of the transition. Some customer agreements require approval before a pattern or drawing can be supplied to an outside scanning service.

A quoted $18,000 digitization package includes the first year of software access, with renewal quoted separately at $2,000 annually. Customers have not agreed on how much they will pay through revised orders or storage fees.

For foundry customer Keri Calden, reliable replacement patterns can matter more than speed when maintaining equipment whose original manufacturer no longer supplies parts. Calden, however, has not committed to buying the business or financing all of its conversion costs.

Merrin’s planned shorter week leaves the workshop facing a practical test of continuity: preserving usable patterns, the instructions that explain them and the ability to ask the right questions while orders continue. The scanning pilot offers a start, but the four patterns needing more annotations show why the wooden form is only part of what must survive.
