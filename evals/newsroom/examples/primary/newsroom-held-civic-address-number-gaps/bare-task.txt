Why Westhollow's Street Numbers Skip Around — And Why the City Isn't "Fixing" It

WESTHOLLOW — Residents puzzled by missing numbers on their block, or by shops that seem to share an address, are getting an explanation from the city's addressing office: the gaps are usually intentional, not evidence of lost buildings or clerical error.

The Westhollow addressing office assigns identifiers to entrances and individual properties under a local numbering plan that is maintained separately from land-title records. On most streets in the current plan, odd numbers run along one side and even numbers along the other, meaning two consecutive numbers are not necessarily neighboring entrances. Older streets in town retain numbering patterns inherited from earlier plans that don't line up exactly with the current system, adding to the appearance of irregularity.

The office said it has fielded a wave of questions after two former shop units were combined into one and a nearby property was approved for a new entrance — changes that altered the visible sequence of numbers on that block. In an example the office uses to explain the system, units formerly numbered 12 and 14 became a single space using number 12 after the combination, with 14 simply left unused rather than triggering a renumbering of the whole street. A separate example shows two approved entrances using 20A and 20B, a format applied when inserting ordinary whole numbers would conflict with addresses that already exist nearby. Officials stressed these two examples illustrate how the system works and are not the actual addresses involved in the recent questions.

"A missing number may tell you about an old division of the street, not a missing building," said Tessa Linn, the addressing officer who has been fielding public inquiries.

The office also noted that a new owner, shop name or business tenant does not, by itself, require a new street number. Address changes are evaluated case by case, weighing an entrance's physical position, the numbers already assigned to neighboring properties, and the needs of emergency responders and delivery services that rely on the identifiers to find a location.

Even when a change is approved, the transition isn't instant everywhere. The city passes updates to relevant municipal services, but individual businesses are responsible for updating their own signage, delivery instructions and customer-facing materials — and private maps or delivery databases may lag behind the official record.

"The city's record changed in one step. Our invoices, labels and delivery notes did not all change with it," said Orin Fale, a shopkeeper affected by a recent change.

The office draws a distinction between a building's official street number and the internal suite or apartment numbers used inside it. Changing internal unit numbers can still confuse emergency responders or delivery drivers, so the office asks property managers to check in before making such changes on their own.

Officials emphasized limits on what the system does. It does not determine property ownership or resolve disputes over a shared entrance, matters left to other city or legal processes. The office also keeps records of earlier address references, in some cases matching old files that used the name of a defunct pub or workshop rather than a number, so that historical planning documents can still be tied to current locations.

The explanatory push comes as the city has fielded repeated requests to fill in apparent numbering gaps purely for the sake of visual tidiness. Officials said no townwide renumbering is planned. The office has not released figures on emergency-response delays or failed deliveries tied to numbering confusion, and it cautioned that Westhollow's specific rules should not be assumed to match addressing practices in other towns.
