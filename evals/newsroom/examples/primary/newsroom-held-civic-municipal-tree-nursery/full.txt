Headline: Before the Planting Day: Inside Bellmarsh's Nursery for Future Street Trees

BELLMARSH — The city's street trees do not begin on a sidewalk. They begin in rows behind a former parks depot, where the municipal nursery holds about 800 young trees at various stages of growth for future public planting.

Not all of them are close to leaving. The nursery's current planning list identifies about 200 as candidates for the next planting season — a fraction of the total stock, and a distinction nursery supervisor Hessa Dale said the public often misses.

"People see the planting day," Dale said. "We are working on the years that have to happen before that day is possible."

On a recent morning, Dale walked the rows with a clipboard, linking each batch to its origin, potting history and intended size range. Staff moved one batch to new spacing so branches and root containers would have room as the trees grow. The work, Dale said, includes watering, condition checks and updating records — not simply planting seeds and waiting.

A tree's presence at the nursery does not guarantee it will suit every proposed street location. The planting team weighs available space, overhead and underground utility lines, and expected maintenance when matching stock to a site. Some trees stay at the nursery for another season if their condition or the destination site's preparation is not ready.

The nursery is not the city's only source of trees; Bellmarsh also buys stock from outside growers.

Its records distinguish stock held, stock allocated and trees actually planted, and an allocation can shift if a planned street project is delayed, rather than forcing a tree into an unsuitable time or an unfinished site. Staff also track trees that no longer remain suitable for planting, rather than counting every seedling or purchased sapling as a future completed street tree.

Neighborhood volunteer Rena Sore helps record street planting requests and has visited the nursery to see the choices behind them.

"On the street we ask for a tree this winter," Sore said. "Here you can see how far back somebody had to begin."

The nursery sits beside a former parks depot and shares some storage and delivery access with the city's maintenance team. It operates on a current city budget, though future street-planting projects require their own site and maintenance funding — separate allocations, Dale said, that do not follow automatically from a tree's readiness.

Dale keeps examples of older plant labels on hand, a way to explain how batch names have changed over time without losing the link to earlier records — part of what she described as keeping a tree's history intact from seedling to sidewalk.

The nursery is not open for unrestricted public collection; community planting requests go through the city's parks process. A planned public visit will show the growing stages and record system, city officials said, rather than promise each visitor a tree to take home.

City officials did not provide a canopy-growth target or a survival-rate figure for the 800 trees now in the nursery, and no estimate was available for how many pending planting requests the current stock can ultimately meet.
