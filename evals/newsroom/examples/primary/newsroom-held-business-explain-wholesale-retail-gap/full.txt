**Wholesale produce prices are down 20%. Grocery bills aren't following at the same rate — here's why**

By [Staff Writer]

The Bramble Exchange's wholesale produce index stood 20% below its year-earlier level in July, the exchange reported. Shoppers scanning that headline may expect grocery prices to fall by roughly the same share. Reporting notes gathered as of Aug. 17 show why that comparison doesn't hold.

The exchange index tracks bulk market transactions across 40 produce categories. It is not a complete household grocery basket, and it excludes costs that show up on a store's books but not on a trading floor: retail labor, store rent, refrigeration, packaging and the value of goods that spoil or go unsold before they can be bought.

At Spot Garden Market, the wholesale shift showed up as a lower crate price. A 10-kilogram tomato crate that recently cost the store $30 now costs $24, according to the shop's supplied handling records. Those records also show a typical crate yields about 8 kilograms of saleable tomatoes in both comparison periods — the rest is trimmed, damaged or otherwise unsellable.

The store's retail price has stayed at $4 a kilogram. If all 8 kilograms sold, that would bring in $32. Subtracting the crate's purchase cost, the gap between that potential revenue and what the store paid widened from $2 to $8 per crate.

That $8 is not the shop's profit. It doesn't account for retail overhead, and the records don't show that every crate sells out completely. Store owner Keri Holt said refrigeration repairs and higher rent have absorbed part of the improved buying margin, though the shop has not supplied complete accounts independently showing how much of that margin those costs took up.

"The crate got cheaper before the shop did," Holt said.

Other grocers face a different constraint entirely. Orchard Thread, another local store, is locked into a three-month tomato supply contract at $28 a crate through August. A lower spot-market quote at the exchange doesn't change an already-signed contract price.

Shoppers notice the disconnect. Customer Oren Venn said he sees bulk-price headlines but shops across fresh food, cleaning products and packaged staples — categories the produce index doesn't touch.

"My basket is not the same basket as the number in the headline," Venn said.

A local consumer group's own tracking illustrates the same gap from another angle. Its 10-item retail basket, which uses a different product mix than the exchange index, fell from $48 to $47 over its comparison period — a far smaller move than the wholesale index reported. The group's figures reflect listed shelf prices, not every household's actual purchases or promotional discounts, which can diverge from either number.

Neither comparison — the exchange index or the consumer group's basket — isolates a single cause for the gap between wholesale and retail prices. The exchange operates before dawn out of a former freight yard, trading in bulk lots; retail shops buy through a range of different supply arrangements, from spot purchases to fixed contracts, each moving on its own timeline.

The reporting record contains no finding of illegal price coordination among retailers and no proof that stores' profits moved together as wholesale costs fell. What the record does show is a chain with several separate links — a trading floor, a supply contract, a store's overhead, a shelf price — and no evidence that a change at one end of that chain arrives, in full, at the other.
