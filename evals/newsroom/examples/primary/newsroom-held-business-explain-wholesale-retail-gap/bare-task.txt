**Wholesale produce prices fell 20%. Grocery shoppers still aren't seeing that.**

A wholesale produce index reported a 20% drop from a year earlier in July 2026, but shoppers at neighborhood stores say their bills look much the same. The gap illustrates why a single market number rarely maps cleanly onto a grocery receipt.

The Bramble Exchange's index tracks bulk transactions in 40 produce categories bought and sold before dawn at a former freight yard. It is not a household grocery basket. It excludes retail labor, store rent, refrigeration, packaging and the cost of goods that spoil or go unsold — all expenses that show up at checkout but never enter the wholesale ledger.

At Spot Garden Market, the effect of the drop is visible but partial. A 10-kilogram tomato crate that once cost the shop $30 now costs $24. The shop's own handling records show that a typical crate yields about eight kilograms of saleable tomatoes in both periods, with the rest lost to trimming, bruising or spoilage. At the unchanged retail price of $4 per kilogram, those eight kilograms could bring in $32 if every bit sold. That leaves a gap between potential revenue and purchase cost that widened from $2 to $8 per crate. That $8 is not profit. It is a gross figure that still must cover retail overhead — and the record does not show whether any given crate sells out completely.

Owner Keri Holt said refrigeration repairs and higher rent have eaten into the improved buying margin. "The crate got cheaper before the shop did," she said. The shop has not supplied complete accounts independently confirming how much of that gap those costs consumed, so her explanation, while plausible, remains unverified by outside records.

Timing also matters. Orchard Thread, a different grocer, is locked into a three-month tomato supply contract at $28 a crate through August. A lower spot-market quote does not automatically change an already-agreed contract price, meaning some retailers are simply not yet exposed to the wholesale swing at all.

Shoppers, meanwhile, are comparing different baskets altogether. Customer Oren Venn said he notices bulk-price headlines but does his own shopping across fresh food, cleaning products and packaged staples — a mix the wholesale index does not track. "My basket is not the same basket as the number in the headline," he said.

A local consumer group's own tracking illustrates the mismatch. Its ten-item retail basket fell from $48 to $47 over its comparison period — a decline of roughly 2%, far short of the wholesale index's 20%. But the group's basket uses a different product mix than the exchange index, and its prices reflect listed shelf prices rather than what any household actually paid after promotions or discounts.

Taken together, the two numbers — a 20% wholesale drop across 40 bulk categories and a one-dollar shift in a ten-item retail basket — are not measuring the same thing, and neither comparison isolates a single cause for the space between them. Retail shops also buy through varied supply arrangements, some spot-market, some contracted, which spreads out how and when any wholesale change reaches shelves.

Nothing in the available record indicates illegal coordination among retailers or shows that stores' profits moved in lockstep. The wholesale number changed quickly and visibly. What happens next — through contracts, overhead, spoilage and store-by-store decisions — happens more slowly, unevenly, and largely out of public view.
