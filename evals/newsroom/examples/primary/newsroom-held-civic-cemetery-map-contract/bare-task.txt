Rillgate Council Approves $74,000 Contract to Reconcile Cemetery Maps and Burial Records

RILLGATE — The Rillgate Council has approved a $74,000 contract to compare cemetery ground plans, burial registers and existing plot markers, aiming to resolve years of conflicting records at the town's burial grounds.

The project covers the old municipal cemetery and its later eastern extension, which use different row-numbering systems, according to council records. The contractor will build a digital working map for cemetery staff, with a separate public location map planned as a later step.

The contract does not authorize excavation or movement of remains, and no such work is included in the current plan.

Cemetery staff have identified conflicting references in several inherited records but have not supplied a verified total of how many burials may be wrongly recorded. The older plans include annotations made over several decades, with no consistent record of who added each note.

"A blank space on a plan is not evidence that nothing is there," cemetery superintendent Neris Pell said.

Rather than filling gaps with an assumed name or location, the contractor will flag unresolved discrepancies for staff review. Physical markers will be photographed from existing paths where possible, with arrangements made for work near scheduled services.

The contractor estimates an eight-month schedule beginning in November, though unresolved records may require additional work afterward. Funding comes from the cemetery's accumulated maintenance reserve rather than a new charge on families using the grounds.

A cemetery friends group has asked that the staff map preserve old spellings and explain corrections rather than replacing earlier entries without a trace.

"Families sometimes arrive with a name written differently on three pieces of paper, and those differences can matter," said Oren Cade, secretary of the friends group.

The working database will retain links to scanned source pages so staff can see the basis for each reconciled entry. Those scans will not all be published online automatically; staff must first decide which details belong in the proposed public map. Questions about a family's existing burial documents will continue to be handled through the cemetery office rather than resolved by a map screen alone.

The old and eastern sections will remain open during most of the mapping work, with short local restrictions announced when needed. The grounds include a row of chestnut trees planted along the former boundary between the two sections.

Officials said the contractor is not being asked to judge the historical importance of particular graves or to select monuments for removal.

Volunteers will be invited to supply copies of relevant records, though any submitted material will require comparison with existing sources before it is used.

The council will receive progress reports on completed comparisons and unresolved discrepancies, rather than a single percentage described as certainty, officials said.

No launch date or final search features have been approved for the public map. Officials said further decisions on that phase will follow completion of the staff-facing reconciliation work.
