# Plastic rises in Clearwater River as researchers study risks to fish

For 40 years, Louis Renard farmed the Clearwater River's upper reach without thinking of the plastic used in agriculture as a water quality problem.

"I thought about fertilizer runoff. I thought about pesticides. Nobody was talking about plastic film going in the river," said Renard, 71, now retired.

Researchers at Northview University's River Sciences Institute have been measuring microplastics in the Clearwater watershed since 2019. Their five-year dataset documents rising concentrations and helps identify where the particles come from. It does not yet answer whether they are harming the river's fish.

Microplastic concentrations in the main channel increased 34% from 2019 to 2024, said lead researcher Elena Vasquez. The steepest increases occurred in agricultural sections downstream of irrigation return water, which carries water from fields back toward the river.

"We can trace the plastic to its sources in many cases. Agricultural mulch film and drip tape fragments dominate the farm sections. Fiber fragments dominate the urban sections. The river is a mirror of the landscape," Vasquez said.

Fishery biologist Kenji Yamamoto, who is collaborating on the study, has found microplastic particles in 92% of water samples from spawning gravel beds, the shallow, oxygenated zones where salmon eggs develop.

Laboratory studies show that particles smaller than 50 micrometers can enter developing fish eggs, Yamamoto said. Field studies examining whether the particles reduce hatching success in the Clearwater remain underway and have not produced statistically conclusive results.

"We're finding the particles where the eggs are. We don't yet know if that matters to the fish. That uncertainty is not reassurance. It's a gap we need to close," he said.

The basin has a federally protected steelhead trout population. A confirmed impact on steelhead reproduction would trigger additional regulatory review under the Endangered Species Act.

Yamamoto said the population is recovering from a historic low. Any new stress on spawning success would be identified through population monitoring, he said, but with a lag of two to three years.

One possible response would target the route plastic takes from farms to the river. Fragments wash into drainage ditches and travel with irrigation return water. Vasquez said simple filtration at return points could address that pathway at an estimated cost of $8,000 per site. Across the Clearwater's 34 registered return points, the estimated cost would be $272,000.

No regulatory mechanism currently requires that filtration. The basin's clean water permit system predates recognition of microplastics as a monitoring priority. The state environmental agency said it is reviewing the institute's data as part of an ongoing water quality assessment; no regulatory action has been triggered.

Irrigation district manager Frank Olsen said his district has begun a voluntary buyback program for agricultural plastic film to reduce what remains in fields after harvest. Participation reached 18% of members in its first year.

"We're trying. But we're asking farmers to add a cost with no direct benefit to them. That's a hard sell every time," Olsen said.

He would support a modest filtration cost-share program if the state provided 70% of the funding. The state program that might cover the work does not currently list microplastics as a priority pollutant.

Yamamoto said most rivers lack the baseline measurements needed to detect trends or attribute changes. The Clearwater researchers can say concentrations are rising because they began measuring early.

"We know what's there now. Before 2019, nobody did," Vasquez said. "That's the first step. It's not the last."
