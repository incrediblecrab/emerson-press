Community volunteers repaint fading wings on Lark Ford mural, working around what the wall can't fully recover

Hessa Noll knelt beside a faded painted wing on the mural along the Lark Ford underpass Friday, holding an older reference sheet up to the wall as volunteers marked sections that needed attention. It was the mural's annual assessment, the yearly session in which Noll, the piece's original artist, returns with neighbors to decide what maintenance the wall needs and what it doesn't.

The mural, painted on a wall beside the pedestrian underpass, depicts river birds and working boats. Under the maintenance agreement covering the artwork, the wall itself remains city property, but any change to the mural's design requires Noll's approval. The agreement allows specified maintenance work; it does not authorize volunteers to add a new character or slogan.

Before deciding what to repaint, the team first sorts out what it's looking at. Surface dirt, genuine paint loss and marks added after the mural was finished don't get treated the same way, and not every visible difference is a reason to repaint an entire section.

"We have a record of the colors, but the wall has also had several winters," Noll said. "Matching one little patch is not always a simple act of going back."

Orin Vale, a resident who helped paint one of the original boat shapes when the mural was first created, brought an earlier project photograph to Friday's session. The photo serves as a reference point for the team, not as proof that every color on the wall today exactly matches what went up originally.

"I recognize the part I worked on even when I can no longer remember the exact shade that came out of the tin," Vale said.

The mural's original design drew on drawings gathered from residents before Noll assembled the final version, which is part of why people recognize different small elements as their own. That history doesn't extend to settling questions about who added later marks to the wall or why; the record does not support a conclusion that every later mark came from the same person or the same motive.

A small neighborhood fund covers the cost of agreed-upon materials for the session, while city staff handle the temporary barriers around the work area. Organizers planned the timing around the underpass's busiest school and commuting periods, rather than treating the wall as a space set apart from daily foot traffic; a marked alternative route keeps the underpass open to pedestrians while the team works.

Some residents have raised the idea of adding a new panel to the mural in a future project, but that proposal falls outside this year's approved maintenance session and was not addressed Friday.

Once the day's work is finished, the team records what was done and passes along any unresolved questions to Noll and the city's building-maintenance staff. The maintenance notes are kept alongside the mural's original design records, so that future sessions can identify which areas were changed and when.

The city has not produced any study showing the mural has reduced vandalism nearby or raised property values in the area, and no permanent repainting schedule or expanded design beyond the current maintenance arrangement has been approved.

By early afternoon, Noll was back at the faded wing, comparing it once more against the reference sheet before deciding how much of it, if any, would be repainted this year.
