Headline: Seconds Count: How Earthquake Early Warning Systems Work — And Where They Fall Short

An earthquake early warning doesn't stop the ground from shaking. It buys time — sometimes just a few seconds — before the worst of it arrives.

These systems work by exploiting a quirk of physics. When a fault ruptures, it sends out two types of seismic waves: P-waves, which move fast but cause little damage, and S-waves, which travel slower but carry most of the destructive energy, according to reporting notes on how the technology functions. Sensors detect the P-wave first, and computers use it to estimate the earthquake's magnitude and location. That information is then transmitted electronically — faster than the S-wave itself — reaching people and machines before the damaging shaking does.

How much warning depends on distance. Areas far from the epicenter can get more than a minute's notice. Areas close to it may get only a few seconds, or nothing at all. That second scenario has a name: the "blind zone," the region near the epicenter where S-waves arrive before the system can process data and send an alert.

Even a short warning has value. Five to 10 seconds is enough for automated systems to slow trains, open fire station doors, halt industrial equipment or alert surgeons to pause a procedure.

"The goal is not to prevent an earthquake," said Dr. Kwame Osei, a seismologist at Pacific Basin University. "It is to give automated systems and prepared individuals a window to take a protective action."

In the United States, the U.S. Geological Survey operates ShakeAlert, which covers California, Oregon and Washington using about 1,500 seismic sensors and has cost roughly $460 million to build, including software and integration with emergency alert systems. Cell phone emergency alerts can reach millions of people at once without a separate app, and they sound even on a silenced phone.

Japan has the longest operational track record, with a system dating to the early 2000s. During a catastrophic magnitude 9.0 subduction earthquake, it sent an alert about 90 seconds before strong shaking reached a major population center, giving residents and transit operators time to react.

Sensor density matters. More sensors shrink the blind zone and sharpen magnitude estimates, but they also raise maintenance costs. That tradeoff helps explain why much of Latin America, Africa and Southeast Asia — regions with high seismic risk — still lack functional coverage.

The systems have real limits. They cannot predict an earthquake before it starts; a warning is issued only after a rupture is already underway. Initial magnitude estimates can also change as more sensors report data, so an alert may be updated within seconds of being issued. And false alerts, while designed to be rare, carry a cost of their own: they can erode public trust, creating a tradeoff between sensitivity and reliability.

For an individual who gets a warning, the recommended response is simple: drop, cover and hold on under something sturdy. The window is usually too short to evacuate a building.

Some limits are physical, not technical. Osei said research is underway on machine-learning methods that could estimate final magnitude from just the first half-second of P-wave data, potentially extending warning times. Instrumented high-rises could eventually receive building-specific alerts tailored to their sway characteristics.

But Osei said there's a hard ceiling. In a megathrust earthquake directly beneath a city, he said, even perfect detection would provide no warning at all to the people living above the fault. The physics simply doesn't allow it.
