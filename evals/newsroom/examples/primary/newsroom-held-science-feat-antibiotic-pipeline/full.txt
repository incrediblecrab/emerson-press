# Antibiotic developer has promising trial results, but only 14 months of cash

For Areteia Pharma, the next step toward bringing a new antibiotic to market carries a price tag of $60 million to $90 million. The company has 34 employees, three antibiotic candidates in clinical development and, as of March 2025, enough cash to operate for 14 months.

Its most advanced candidate cleared a resistant bacterium in 14 of 18 patients in a small trial. But the money needed for the next stage has not followed.

"We have drugs that work against bacteria that nothing else kills. We cannot raise the Series C to bring them to market," said Dr. Carlos Fuentes, Areteia's CEO, referring to its planned next investment round. "That is not a scientific failure. It is an economic failure."

Areteia's predicament reflects a problem in antibiotic development: New drugs are used sparingly to delay resistance, limiting the sales developers need to recover their costs. A proposed federal payment system would reward availability rather than prescription volume, but it remains unenacted as the company's cash runs down.

Areteia has no commercial product and no revenue from drug sales. It depends entirely on grants and investors. The National Institutes of Health provided $14 million in preclinical funding for its lead candidate, and private investors have contributed $38 million across two funding rounds.

Fuentes said the lead candidate targets Acinetobacter baumannii, a bacterium that causes severe hospital infections and is classified by the World Health Organization as a critical priority pathogen.

The 18 patients in the Phase 2 trial had infections that had not responded to any other antibiotic. Although the candidate cleared the pathogen in 14 patients, the trial was too small to be statistically definitive.

Dr. Josephine Amin, an infectious disease specialist, called those results scientifically encouraging but insufficient for Food and Drug Administration approval. Reaching Phase 3, the next stage of clinical testing, requires $60 million to $90 million — money Fuentes said Areteia cannot raise under current market conditions.

Amin said the company's situation is representative of the broader field. She cited the WHO's 2024 pipeline assessment, which found only 12 antibiotics in Phase 3 trials globally, four of them addressing priority resistant pathogens.

"The pipeline is not empty. It is not funded. There is a difference," Amin said. "The science works. The business model doesn't."

The proposed U.S. PASTEUR Act would change how antibiotic developers are paid. Under its subscription model, the government would pay companies up to $3 billion over 10 years to keep an approved antibiotic available, regardless of how many prescriptions are written.

As of March 18, 2025, the bill had passed committee but had not been enacted.

Fuentes said Areteia cannot wait for it.

"It's been in Congress for four years. We have 14 months," he said. "Legislation that hasn't passed doesn't pay salaries."

Amin said she has seen three promising antibiotic programs discontinued over the past decade for economic rather than scientific reasons.

"It is a preventable loss," she said. "We are choosing not to prevent it."

For Areteia, a partnership offers another possible route. Fuentes said the company is discussing licensing its Acinetobacter program with two large pharmaceutical companies. Neither has made a formal offer.

He would not describe the situation as hopeless.

"The drugs work. The need is real. We are one policy decision or one partnership away from a different outcome," Fuentes said. "That's both encouraging and maddening."
