**Soil Carbon Payments Fell Short of Projections, and a Sandover County Farmer Wants Answers About Why**

By [Staff Writer]

SANDOVER COUNTY — Carmen Delgado did the math four years ago: switch 900 of her 1,400 acres to no-till farming, enroll in a voluntary soil carbon program, and collect $18 for every tonne of carbon her fields locked into the ground.

The projections she was shown put her total at 800 tonnes over four years. This month, an audit came back with a different number: 340 tonnes, about 40% below what the model had predicted.

"They showed me projections of eight hundred tonnes. I signed up for eight hundred tonnes' worth of effort," Delgado, 48, said. "I got six thousand dollars less than I planned on. That's real money."

Delgado, who was paid for the 340 tonnes and did not dispute the figure, is one of 22 farmers among 38 enrolled in the Sandover County program whose payouts came in below initial projections, according to preliminary program records. Twelve farmers received payouts close to their projections, and four received more than expected.

The auditor, soil consultancy Groundworks Analytics, says the shortfall is not evidence of fraud but a reflection of natural variability in soil carbon storage — and of models that tend to overestimate sequestration in drier-than-average years.

"The model gives an expected value under average conditions. The last four years have not been average. Two of them were drought years," said Maria Lindqvist, a soil scientist with Groundworks. "Drought years store less carbon."

Lindqvist said the underlying science holds up, but the way it was explained to farmers did not. "Projections were presented as targets, not estimates," she said. "That gap in framing caused real problems for this farmer and many others."

Delgado said she read her contract carefully but did not grasp what its language meant in practice. "It said 'estimated based on projected conditions.' I didn't know projected conditions meant 'if it's a normal year, which it may not be,'" she said.

The program administrator, TerraPay Carbon, said it is now revising how it communicates projections to farmers, presenting them as ranges instead of single-point estimates. The change applies only to new enrollments, not existing contracts like Delgado's.

Dr. Anita Bauer, a soil carbon policy researcher at Westfield Agricultural Institute, said the Sandover results, while frustrating for farmers, are valuable as a warning sign. "Better to find this out at scale in a voluntary program than after mandatory carbon accounting systems are built on the same model assumptions," she said.

Bauer also pointed to a structural problem underlying the dispute: physical soil sampling, the most reliable way to verify carbon storage, costs $300 to $600 per sample location, and credible field-scale estimates require multiple samples — a cost that can rival or exceed payments on small farms. TerraPay charges enrolled farmers a flat $1,200 audit fee; Delgado said she accepted it because her net payment still exceeded what she expected when she first signed up.

Despite the shortfall, Delgado said the no-till switch was worthwhile on its own, citing reduced erosion, better water retention and lower fuel costs from not running tillage equipment. "I would have switched without the payment," she said. "The payment was supposed to make it faster. Instead it made it confusing."

Bauer said the episode highlights a tension in voluntary carbon markets: farmers who would benefit most from candid uncertainty estimates are often the ones most likely to walk away if the ranges look too wide.

Lindqvist said Groundworks will fold the Sandover data into its model calibration, which she said may improve projections in similar dryland conditions going forward.

TerraPay said 78% of Sandover County enrollees surveyed said they would re-enroll for a second term. Delgado is among them, now armed with a clearer sense of what the numbers mean. "I know what it means now," she said. "It means: if the weather cooperates."

Asked for her final assessment of the program, Delgado did not offer a simple verdict. "It works. It just doesn't work the way I thought it did," she said. "That's my fault and their fault together."
