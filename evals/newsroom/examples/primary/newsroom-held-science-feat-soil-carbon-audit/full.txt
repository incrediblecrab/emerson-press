**Soil Carbon Payments Fell Short of Projections. A Sandover County Farmer Says the Real Failure Was in the Fine Print**

By [Staff Writer]

SANDOVER COUNTY — Carmen Delgado switched 900 of her 1,400 acres to no-till farming in 2020 and enrolled in a voluntary program that pays farmers for carbon their soil locks away. The pitch, she said, promised roughly 800 tonnes of sequestered carbon over four years.

An audit released this month found her fields had sequestered 340 tonnes — about 40% below the program's projection.

Delgado, 48, accepted the lower figure without dispute. She was paid $18 per tonne for the 340 tonnes measured. But she said the gap between what she was shown and what was verified revealed something about how the program was sold.

"They showed me projections of eight hundred tonnes. I signed up for eight hundred tonnes' worth of effort. I got six thousand dollars less than I planned on," Delgado said. "That's real money."

The audit was conducted by Groundworks Analytics, the soil consultancy that models carbon sequestration for the program. Maria Lindqvist, a Groundworks soil scientist, said the shortfall does not reflect fraud but the natural variability of soil carbon and a tendency for projections to run high in drier-than-average years.

"The model gives an expected value under average conditions. The last four years have not been average. Two of them were drought years," Lindqvist said. "Drought years store less carbon."

Lindqvist said the science behind the model held up but that farmers were not told how uncertain the projections were. "Projections were presented as targets, not estimates," she said. "That gap in framing caused real problems for this farmer and many others."

Delgado said her contract described figures as "estimated based on projected conditions" but that she did not understand what that meant. "I didn't know projected conditions meant 'if it's a normal year, which it may not be,'" she said.

Sandover County has 38 farms enrolled in the program. Preliminary records show 22 received payouts below initial projections, 12 close to projections and four above projections.

TerraPay Carbon, the program administrator, said it is revising how it communicates with farmers, presenting projections as ranges rather than single-point figures for new enrollments.

Dr. Anita Bauer, a soil carbon policy researcher at Westfield Agricultural Institute, called the Sandover results a useful, if painful, correction. "Better to find this out at scale in a voluntary program than after mandatory carbon accounting systems are built on the same model assumptions," she said.

Bauer said physical soil sampling, the most reliable verification method, costs $300 to $600 per sample location, and credible estimates require multiple samples — a cost that can approach or exceed payments for small farms. She added that farmers who most need honest uncertainty framing are often the ones most likely to be discouraged by it.

Delgado said the no-till switch was worth it regardless of the payment, citing reduced erosion, better water retention and lower fuel costs. "I would have switched without the payment," she said. "The payment was supposed to make it faster. Instead it made it confusing."

She paid TerraPay's flat $1,200 audit fee and said the program still paid more than she expected when she enrolled. She plans to re-enroll for a second four-year period under the new framing. "I know what it means now," she said. "It means: if the weather cooperates."

TerraPay said 78% of surveyed Sandover enrollees, including Delgado, said they would re-enroll.

Her verdict on the program was mixed. "It works. It just doesn't work the way I thought it did," she said. "That's my fault and their fault together."
