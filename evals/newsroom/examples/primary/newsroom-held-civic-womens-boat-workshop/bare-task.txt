**Headline: Women-Led Crew Restores Donated Skiff, One Careful Step at a Time**

By [Staff Writer]

SABLE QUAY — On a recent evening inside the community boat shed at Sable Quay, a small group of women gathered around a wooden rowing skiff, comparing a worn rail against its intended replacement before deciding how to proceed.

The scene is a regular one this fall. Each week, members of a women-led repair group meet under supervision to work on the donated wooden skiff, a project that has become a fixture in the shed alongside the other activities scheduled there by the local boating association, which oversees the space.

The sessions are led by Hara Senn, who brings professional woodworking experience to the group. Other members arrive with a range of backgrounds and are learning the boat-specific work as they go.

"The question at the bench is what the boat needs next," Senn said. "Everyone should have a chance to understand the answer."

That approach has shaped how the group documents its progress. Members keep a repair notebook so that people attending on different weeks can see what has been assessed, removed or left unresolved — a practical necessity given that participation varies from session to session. A chalkboard near the door lists that week's available tasks, including items waiting on specialist input.

Limited storage space in the shed adds another layer of discipline to the work. Members mark and put away removed fittings, such as the ones labeled during a recent session, so the work area is ready for whoever uses it next.

Elva Rill is one of those members. She spent years helping organize club events without taking part in the hands-on workshop jobs, she said, before deciding to join the repair sessions.

"I knew how to get the meeting ready," Rill said. "I wanted to know what was happening on the other side of the shed door."

The project has some outside support. It received a materials grant, and members contribute session fees to cover ordinary shed costs. Tools are introduced through the shed's existing supervision arrangements rather than made available for unrestricted use.

Organizers are careful about what the project does and does not represent. It is a community learning effort, not a course that confers a professional trade qualification. And while the skiff's wood has been coming together under the group's attention, Senn has asked members to record uncertainties about older repairs rather than fill gaps in the boat's history with confident guesses. The boat's earlier owner provided photographs but no complete record of repairs made over its life.

The skiff itself has not been declared ready for launching or for carrying passengers. A separate condition and launch-readiness review is planned once the repair work is finished, rather than assumed from how the finished wood looks. The group has also held off on promising a launch date, saying instead that members will discuss a shared use for the boat once it clears that review. The skiff will remain the property of the community project rather than passing to Senn or any other individual.

The boating association, which schedules other shed users around the group's sessions, said the project has prompted questions from others interested in future workshop access, though it has not conducted a survey of that interest.

Organizers describe the effort as specific to this group and this boat — not a claim about women's experience in boating generally, and not a projection of the skiff's eventual value once repairs are complete.
