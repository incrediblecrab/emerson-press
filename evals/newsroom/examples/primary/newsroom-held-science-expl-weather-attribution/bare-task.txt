# How scientists measure climate change’s role in extreme weather

After a heat wave, flood or powerful storm, a familiar question follows: Did climate change cause it? Scientists generally ask a more precise question: Did human-caused warming make the event more likely or more severe — and by how much?

The field that tackles those questions, extreme event attribution, uses statistics, observations and climate models to estimate how warming changed the odds or intensity of a particular event.

“We cannot say any single storm was caused by climate change. We can say whether climate change stacked the odds and, if so, by how much,” said Ingrid Bjornsson, a climate attribution scientist at Nordic Climate Center.

## Comparing two worlds

At the heart of an attribution study is a comparison between two populations of simulated weather events. One comes from models representing today’s climate. The other represents a hypothetical world without human greenhouse gas emissions.

Researchers compare how often an event of a given intensity appears in each population. If it is 10 times more likely in today’s climate, they conclude that climate change increased its probability tenfold. They can also examine how warming affected an event’s intensity.

Results are often expressed as probability ratios or changes in “return period” — the average time between events of a given magnitude. A 100-year event has a 1% chance of occurring in any given year. That does not mean such events arrive on a fixed, century-long schedule.

The underlying physics is well established: Warmer surface temperatures intensify heat waves, while warmer air holds more moisture, increasing the potential for flood-producing and storm rainfall. But those principles alone do not establish how much warming influenced a particular event.

## Some events are easier to assess

Heat waves are among the most straightforward events to attribute. Multiple published studies have found that some recent heat waves would have been virtually impossible without human-caused climate change.

Rainfall is harder. It depends on atmospheric dynamics that models have more difficulty simulating accurately, so confidence in rainfall attribution is generally lower than for heat.

For hurricanes and typhoons, studies typically focus on intensity, including wind speed and rainfall rate, rather than whether a storm formed. Formation depends on factors beyond sea surface temperature alone.

Drought presents another challenge: time. Detecting a climate change signal in a single drought year is harder than identifying one in a multiyear trend.

## How much confidence should readers place in a finding?

A study’s strength depends on the quality and resolution of its models, the length of observational records and how well the models represent the affected region.

Different models can produce different probability ratios for the same event. Using a wider range of models can produce wider confidence intervals, making the uncertainty more visible.

Researchers must also account for other influences, including deforestation and urban heat island effects, to isolate the greenhouse gas signal.

Speed matters, too. Rapid attribution analyses can appear within days of a major event. The World Weather Attribution group publishes rapid findings and then submits them for peer review. Bjornsson said those results should be treated as preliminary; fuller analyses using larger collections of models and longer observational records may differ.

## Why the distinction matters

Attribution findings have been cited in court decisions concerning governments’ or companies’ duties to reduce emissions. Insurers and reinsurers also use the research to assess and price climate-related risks.

Yet studies finding no significant climate change influence on a particular event receive less media attention.

Bjornsson said the field has become a standard scientific tool, but its credibility rests on communicating uncertainty rigorously. The goal is not to attach climate change to every disaster. It is to explain what the evidence supports — without claiming more than the data can show.
