**At Nettleford College, Repair Trainees Learn Patience While Schools Wait for Instruments**

NETTLEFORD — On a workshop bench in July, trainee Omi Keld pressed a new pad into a clarinet's tone hole, then carried the instrument to an instructor's inspection station rather than call the job finished.

It was fitted, but fitted was not the same as fixed. The instrument still had to prove itself, one key press at a time, before it could go back to a school music room.

That extra step — the gap between "the part fits" and "the instrument works" — sits at the center of how Nettleford College's instrument-repair program operates, and at the center of a tradeoff school music departments have learned to live with: slower turnaround in exchange for supervised, in-progress training.

The one-year program enrolls 24 adult trainees drawn from varied work and education backgrounds, and it does not sell itself to schools as an express repair service. Participating music departments send in instruments for supervised repair work, and every instrument that comes back must first pass a functional check from an instructor, according to the program's stated process.

During the 2025-26 teaching year, the workshop accepted 180 school instruments. It returned 150 after repair. Twenty were still waiting on parts at the year-end count, and 10 were assessed as beyond what the workshop could fix. College officials note the figures count instruments, not distinct schools or trainee qualifications, and the college has not published a study matching its repair outcomes against commercial providers over the long term.

Schools pay a $35 handling contribution per accepted instrument, plus any parts charge agreed on in advance. That fee does not cover the program's full teaching cost, which is otherwise supported by the college budget and a $20,000 annual grant. By comparison, one commercial shop quoted $120 for a comparable clarinet repair with a shorter turnaround — a single quote for a single job, not a standing price list, and not a figure the college treats as a benchmark.

The college asks schools to plan for a six-week turnaround, with longer waits possible when parts are unavailable.

Band teacher Rella Brill has built her rehearsal schedule around that reality. She keeps spare instruments on hand and reshuffles practice groups while students' own instruments are at the workshop.

"A cheaper repair is useful only if I can keep the student playing while we wait," Brill said.

For trainees, the wait built into the process is the point. Keld, who previously repaired household appliances, said adjusting an instrument requires a different kind of checking than fixing a machine — a pad can sit correctly and the instrument can still signal, through tone or resistance, that the work is not done.

"The part can fit and the instrument can still tell you the job is not finished," Keld said.

Instructor Teri Sorn said trainees sometimes repeat a task more than once before an instructor accepts it, adding learning time that a commercial repair quote would not typically allow. That repetition, she said, is built into the program's design rather than treated as delay.

The workshop shares a building with evening music classes, which use a separate set of instruments and do not draw from the school-repair queue.

Completing the program earns trainees a college certificate, not a guaranteed job, and not an assurance that every graduate can repair every family of instrument.

The arrangement's future shape is not settled. The next renewal of the program's $20,000 grant is pending, and the $35 school contribution is not guaranteed to hold at its current level, leaving open how the balance between training time and turnaround speed might shift in the coming year.
