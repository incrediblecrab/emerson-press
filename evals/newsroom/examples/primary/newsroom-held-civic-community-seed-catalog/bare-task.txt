# At Ashmere seed library, volunteers keep seed stories grounded in records

A paper packet bearing a family's informal name for a bean offers one kind of clue. Another, identified only as a red flower, offers much less. At the Ashmere seed library, volunteers document both without filling the gaps with answers they cannot support.

The small donated packets are kept in drawers inside a community garden's meeting shed. Volunteer cataloger Mara Vell asks donors for the name they use, the harvest year and what they know about where the seed came from. An incomplete description stays incomplete, rather than becoming a confident commercial variety name.

“The name on the envelope is part of the story, but it may not be enough to tell the next gardener exactly what will grow,” Vell said.

The labels distinguish a donor's description from information checked against another supplied record. That leaves room for a family's seed history while showing the next gardener which details have additional support and which do not.

Donor Eda Noll brings a copy of a handwritten record with her latest bean donation. She has kept track of beans grown through several seasons, providing a history to accompany the packet. But that history has limits.

“I can tell you which summer they came from. I cannot promise your garden will give you the summer I had,” Noll said.

The packet contains no germination test result or guarantee of yield. The library does not present its collection as certified commercial stock or verified genetic preservation. Its records document the donated seeds, not a promise about how they will perform.

That distinction carries through the volunteers' storage practices. Older and newer donations are kept separate enough to retain their collection dates, rather than being combined under a fresh label. A packet may be withdrawn if its record or condition raises a question volunteers cannot resolve.

The library began as a seed-swap table at the garden's annual gathering. It later acquired the drawers from a closed stationery shop. The shed now opens during staffed garden sessions; it is not an unattended public seed dispenser.

Under the garden's current sharing arrangement, visitors may take the offered small packets without paying a purchase price. A future donation is welcome, but taking seeds does not create a debt that every recipient must repay with the same quantity or variety. Volunteers limit packet sizes so the available stock can reach more than one grower.

A simple stock list helps the team avoid advertising packets that have already been taken. It does not identify individual recipients or count every seed as a separate participating gardener.

The garden also invites recipients to bring back observations. Those accounts are treated as reports from particular growing conditions, not controlled comparisons. Like Noll's handwritten history, they can add context without establishing what another gardener should expect.

As of Sept. 18, 2026, a planned display would explain some labels and donor histories, with permission, rather than publish donors' private contact details. The garden has not supplied a quantified effect on townwide biodiversity or household food costs.

For now, the collection's records offer a more limited account: what donors call their seeds, when they harvested them and what is known about their origins. Where those answers stop, the volunteers leave the uncertainty visible.
