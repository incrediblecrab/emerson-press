Port Calloway strike enters third day as $180 million in cargo sits idle

CALLOWAY — Dockworkers at Port Calloway remained off the job Wednesday for a third day, leaving an estimated $180 million in cargo idle at one of the region's busiest shipping hubs.

The strike began Monday when members of International Longshore Workers Union Local 14 rejected a management offer and walked off the job. The 890 striking workers are seeking a 28% wage increase over four years; the port authority's current offer is 18% over the same term. The union's previous contract, which expired Dec. 31, 2024, had provided a 12% increase over four years.

"We move every container that keeps this economy running, and we have been offered less than inflation in real terms," Local 14 President Maria Reyes said.

Port Calloway authority chief Donald Hsu said management wants a deal but noted the cost of the standoff. "We remain committed to reaching a fair agreement, but this disruption is costing everyone," he said.

A second dispute centers on automation. The union wants the port to pause all new automation investment during negotiations. The port authority plans to install six automated container cranes, which it estimates would cut labor needs by about 18% per shift once operational. Reyes called automation a "red line" for the union. Hsu said the cranes are necessary for the port to stay competitive with neighboring facilities.

About 2,100 containers are sitting at the port that would normally have been loaded or unloaded, port records show. Roughly 12% of those containers hold perishables, including strawberry and lettuce shipments.

Port Calloway handles about $4.2 billion in cargo annually and is a principal entry point for electronics, auto parts and refrigerated agricultural products. The Calloway Regional Chamber of Commerce estimated Wednesday that the strike is costing the regional economy about $60 million a day in delayed commerce, including downstream supply chain disruptions. Three electronics importers and two auto parts distributors told reporters they had missed delivery deadlines; none agreed to be named publicly.

The port's terminal operator has redirected four arriving cargo ships to Port Westfield, 80 miles up the coast, adding an estimated $2,300 per container in handling and trucking costs.

The Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service sent a team to Calloway on Tuesday. Team leader Gerald Watts said the two sides had agreed to talk but had not yet entered formal mediated sessions. California's governor's office offered to assign a state mediator; the union said it was open to that, but port management said it preferred the federal process.

The ILWU's national leadership has not declared a formal national strike; Local 14 is acting independently under its own collective bargaining agreement.

Port authority officials said Wednesday that despite the labor costs at stake, the port ended fiscal year 2024 with a $28 million operating surplus.

The last major work stoppage at Port Calloway was in 2011 and lasted nine days; that dispute was also resolved through federal mediation.

No new talks were scheduled as of Wednesday afternoon.
