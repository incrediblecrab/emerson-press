# Port Calloway strike enters third day with $180 million in cargo idle

Dockworkers at Port Calloway entered the third day of a strike Wednesday with an estimated $180 million in cargo sitting idle as the union and port authority remained divided over wages and automation.

The 890 striking workers are seeking a 28% wage increase over four years, compared with the port authority's offer of 18%. The union also wants a pause on all new automation investment during contract negotiations, while the authority plans to install six automated container cranes.

Federal mediators arrived Tuesday. Gerald Watts, the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service team leader, said the parties had agreed to talk but had not yet entered formal mediated sessions.

The stoppage began Monday, April 14, when International Longshore Workers Union Local 14 rejected management's offer and walked off the job. About 2,100 containers that would have been loaded or unloaded under normal operations were sitting at the port Wednesday.

"We move every container that keeps this economy running, and we have been offered less than inflation in real terms," Local 14 President Maria Reyes said.

The union's previous contract expired Dec. 31, 2024. It provided a 12% wage increase over four years. The current wage proposals are 10 percentage points apart, though the dispute also turns on how automation would change the work.

Reyes called automation a "red line" for the union. The six planned cranes are estimated to reduce labor requirements by about 18% per shift once operational. That estimate does not establish how many jobs would be eliminated.

Port authority chief Donald Hsu said the cranes were necessary for the port to remain competitive with neighboring facilities.

"We remain committed to reaching a fair agreement, but this disruption is costing everyone," Hsu said.

The authority reported a $28 million operating surplus in fiscal 2024. The port handles about $4.2 billion in cargo annually and is a principal entry point for electronics, auto parts and refrigerated agricultural products.

The Calloway Regional Chamber of Commerce estimated Wednesday that the strike was costing the regional economy about $60 million a day in delayed commerce, including downstream supply chain disruptions. That measure describes delayed economic activity, rather than a tally of permanently lost revenue.

The port's terminal operator redirected four arriving cargo ships to Port Westfield, 80 miles up the coast. The diversions added an estimated $2,300 per container in handling and trucking costs.

Perishable cargo, including strawberry and lettuce shipments, accounted for roughly 12% of the stranded containers, according to port records.

The California governor's office offered to assign a state mediator. The union said it was open to the offer, but port management said it preferred the federal process.

Local 14 is acting independently under its own collective bargaining agreement. The ILWU's national leadership has not declared a formal national strike.

Port Calloway's last major work stoppage, in 2011, lasted nine days and was resolved with federal mediation.
