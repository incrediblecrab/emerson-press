Private Equity Firm Completes $380 Million Purchase of Tribune Valley Media, Plans to Cut 85 Newsroom Jobs

CLAYFIELD, Iowa — Broadreach Capital Partners has completed its $380 million acquisition of Tribune Valley Media Group, taking ownership of 14 regional newspapers across the rural Midwest and immediately announcing plans to eliminate 85 editorial positions.

The cuts represent about 22 percent of Tribune Valley's 390-person editorial staff, the company said. The 14 papers have a combined paid circulation of approximately 340,000, including daily and Sunday editions.

The sale ends more than six decades of ownership by the Larkin family, which founded Tribune Valley in 1964 and sold its controlling stake as part of the deal. The company is headquartered in Clayfield.

Jeffrey Crane, a managing partner at Broadreach, defended the job cuts as necessary to the company's survival.

"Sustainable local journalism requires operating discipline that a family ownership structure can no longer afford in this environment," Crane said.

Tribune Valley CEO Sarah Holloway, who will remain in her role under the new ownership, pushed back against the scale of the reductions in an internal memo obtained by reporters.

"I will not sign off on cuts that destroy the editorial capacity of these papers," Holloway wrote. Her statement stopped short of threatening resignation.

The Newspaper Guild Local 203, which represents journalists at six of the 14 papers, filed an objection with the National Labor Relations Board in February, arguing that Broadreach failed to bargain over the announced job cuts before the deal closed.

The acquisition drew scrutiny from the Department of Justice, which reviewed the deal but did not challenge it before closing.

Broadreach has acquired two other regional newspaper groups in recent years. Both experienced editorial staff reductions of more than 30 percent within 18 months of the acquisitions, according to journalism industry records — a track record that has fueled skepticism among Tribune Valley employees about the company's plans.

Several Tribune Valley journalists reached by reporters said they intend to seek other jobs if the cuts proceed as announced.

The purchase price of $380 million amounts to roughly 2.7 times Tribune Valley's 2024 revenue of $141 million. That revenue picture reflects a broader shift in the company's business: digital advertising revenue rose 18 percent last year to $42 million, while print advertising revenue fell 31 percent over the same period.

Crane said Broadreach intends to invest in digital infrastructure at the papers but declined to specify an amount. The company also declined to answer questions about editorial policies or whether the papers would begin sharing content across titles.

The uncertainty comes as some Tribune Valley papers have built a reputation for consequential local reporting. Three of the company's papers have won regional journalism awards over the past two years, and two investigations led to the resignations of county officials.

The Local News Alliance, a journalism advocacy organization, criticized the deal, calling it "the latest chapter in a pattern of private equity consolidation that has hollowed out local newsrooms." The group estimates that Tribune Valley's papers are the primary source of local news for about 510,000 residents in communities that have no other local news outlet.

That reach is at the center of the debate over the acquisition: whether a private equity owner focused on trimming costs can sustain the reporting capacity that many small communities depend on for coverage of schools, courts and local government, even as the industry's economics continue to erode.

For now, both the company and its unionized employees are bracing for what comes next, with the promised layoffs still to be worked out and the Guild's labor complaint pending before federal regulators.
