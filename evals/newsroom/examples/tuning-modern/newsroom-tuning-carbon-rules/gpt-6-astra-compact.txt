# Agency orders power plant emissions cuts, drawing threat of legal challenge

The Environmental Compliance Agency issued a rule Wednesday requiring covered coal and natural gas plants to cut carbon dioxide emissions 40% from 2024 levels by May 2029 or cover emissions above that limit with approved offset credits.

The Interim Clean Generation Standard applies to approximately 340 facilities with capacity of at least 100 megawatts. It takes effect May 9, 2025, with the emissions requirement due May 9, 2029. The initial price for offset credits is $45 per metric ton, subject to annual revision.

The American Power Producers Alliance said it would challenge the rule in the 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, arguing that the agency exceeded its statutory authority. A separate challenge by three states to the agency's use of emergency rulemaking authority is already pending.

"The climate math is simple: we have run out of time for voluntary measures," agency Administrator Thomas Beckwith said.

The agency projects that full compliance will reduce annual carbon emissions from covered facilities by approximately 280 million metric tons. Independent energy analysts at the Meridian Policy Institute estimate compliance costs of $18 billion to $31 billion over four years, depending on offset credit prices.

The rule gives plants four compliance options: reduce emissions, purchase approved offsets, retire the facility or demonstrate financial hardship and apply for an 18-month extension. Nuclear, solar and wind generation facilities are not covered.

Key details of implementation remain unfinished. The agency has not published its list of approved third-party certifiers for offset credits, though it said the list will appear in a supplemental Federal Register notice before the May 9 effective date. The agency's enforcement framework will be addressed in another supplemental notice expected by July.

The use of offsets means the 40% requirement does not necessarily translate into a 40% reduction in emissions at each covered plant. A plant may instead purchase approved credits to cover emissions above its limit.

Alliance President Roger Holt said the rule "bypasses congressional intent and will raise electricity rates for millions of families." The alliance's planned lawsuit would add a challenge to the substance of the rule alongside the pending dispute over how it was adopted.

The agency invoked emergency authority to replace its ordinary 180-day consultation schedule with an expedited process. It held a 45-day comment period earlier this year and received approximately 12,000 written comments.

Harwick, Dunmore and Selby filed their challenge to that use of emergency authority in February. The case remains pending.

Political and environmental groups offered sharply different assessments. Two Democratic senators from coal-producing states called the rule "procedurally flawed and economically reckless" in a joint statement. The Clean Energy Now coalition, which represents 34 environmental organizations, called it "a necessary and overdue first step."

The previous standard, adopted in 2018, required an 18% emissions reduction from 2014 levels by 2030. The new rule uses 2024 emissions as its baseline, so the two percentage targets cannot be directly compared without accounting for emissions changes between those years.
