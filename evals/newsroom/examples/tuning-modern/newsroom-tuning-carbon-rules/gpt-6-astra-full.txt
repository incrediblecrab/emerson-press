# Agency orders power plant emissions cuts, drawing pledge of legal challenge

The Environmental Compliance Agency issued a rule Wednesday requiring covered coal and natural gas plants to cut carbon dioxide emissions 40% from 2024 levels by May 9, 2029, or purchase approved offsets.

The Interim Clean Generation Standard applies to approximately 340 generation facilities with capacities of at least 100 megawatts. It takes effect May 9, 30 days after publication, but details of its enforcement framework and a list of approved offset certifiers remain forthcoming.

The American Power Producers Alliance said it would challenge the rule in the 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, arguing that the agency exceeded its statutory authority. A separate challenge by three states to the agency's use of emergency rulemaking authority is already pending.

Agency Administrator Thomas Beckwith defended the requirement as necessary to address climate change.

"The climate math is simple: we have run out of time for voluntary measures," Beckwith said.

The agency projects that full compliance will reduce annual carbon emissions from covered facilities by approximately 280 million metric tons. Independent energy analysts at the Meridian Policy Institute estimate compliance costs of $18 billion to $31 billion over four years, depending on offset credit prices.

Under the standard, plants can reduce emissions directly or cover emissions above the limit with approved carbon offset credits. The rule sets an initial credit price of $45 per metric ton, subject to annual revision.

Plants also can retire or demonstrate financial hardship and apply for an 18-month extension. The rule does not apply to nuclear, solar or wind generation facilities.

The offset option leaves an important compliance detail unresolved: The agency has not published its list of approved third-party certifiers. It said that list will appear in a supplemental Federal Register notice before the May 9 effective date. A separate supplemental notice addressing enforcement is expected by July.

Alliance President Roger Holt said the rule "bypasses congressional intent and will raise electricity rates for millions of families." The cost estimate from Meridian addresses compliance expenses, not how much household electricity bills might change.

The agency invoked emergency authority to replace its ordinary 180-day consultation schedule with an expedited process. It held a 45-day comment period earlier this year and received approximately 12,000 written comments.

Harwick, Dunmore and Selby filed their challenge to the use of emergency authority in February. That case remains pending, separate from the lawsuit the power producers alliance said it plans to file.

Two Democratic senators from coal-producing states also criticized the rule, calling it "procedurally flawed and economically reckless" in a joint statement.

The Clean Energy Now coalition, which represents 34 environmental organizations, welcomed the standard as "a necessary and overdue first step."

The previous standard, adopted in 2018, required an 18% emissions reduction from 2014 levels by 2030. The new rule moves the compliance deadline to 2029 and uses 2024 emissions as its baseline. Because the standards use different baseline years, their percentage targets alone do not show how much additional emissions reduction the new rule requires.
