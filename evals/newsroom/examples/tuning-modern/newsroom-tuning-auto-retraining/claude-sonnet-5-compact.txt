**In Michigan Factory Town, Retraining Program Beats the Odds — and Still Falls Short**

HARTWELL, Mich. — James Petrov spent 19 years running machines at the Keystone Motors plant here, earning $31 an hour before the assembly line went dark for good on Dec. 31, 2021. Now he runs a CNC machine at Clearline Manufacturing for $23 an hour.

"I am working. My house is paid for," Petrov, 52, said. "But I will not make what I made at Keystone again."

His experience captures the mixed record of Hartwell Works, the city-backed retraining program built to catch the roughly 2,800 workers Keystone laid off — and the 1,400 additional supplier and service jobs economists estimate the closure cost this city of about 43,000, which built its economy around the plant for more than five decades.

According to an April 2025 report from the program, about 1,100 former Keystone workers enrolled in Hartwell Works since it began accepting applicants in March 2022. Of those, 780 completed a certification track, a 71 percent completion rate. Of the completers, 499 — about 64 percent — found relevant employment within six months.

The Michigan Department of Labor said Hartwell Works has the highest per-enrollee completion rate of any plant-closure response program it has funded in the past decade. The program, a partnership among Hartwell Community College, Vertex Systems and Clearline Manufacturing, has cost about $14.2 million, financed through state and federal Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act grants.

But the numbers thin out at the edges. About 1,700 former Keystone workers never enrolled; administrators estimate roughly 400 left the Hartwell area, about 300 retired early and the remaining 1,000 are unemployed or working part time. Among enrollees over 50 — 40 percent of the total — the six-month employment rate for completers fell to 51 percent. Information technology and computer-skills tracks completed at just 38 percent, which administrators attributed to workers lacking a computer background, compared with an 83 percent completion rate and 72 percent placement rate in the manufacturing tracks: CNC machining, industrial electrical and welding.

Wages have not caught up either. Completers now earn an average of $22.50 an hour, compared with $28.40 at Keystone before it closed — a gap of $5.90 an hour. Vertex Systems has hired 64 Hartwell Works completers; a company spokeswoman called the arrangement "mutually beneficial" but declined to say whether the jobs pay comparably to Keystone.

Mayor Sandra Alvarado said the results leave her uncertain.

"Sixty-four percent would be a good graduation rate at a lot of colleges," Alvarado said. "For a workforce-crisis response, I do not know if it is enough."

The city's finances have felt the closure too. Combined property and income tax revenue fell 19 percent from 2021 to 2024, straining street maintenance and the fire department. The 65-acre former plant site, demolished in 2023, has been listed for sale at $8.5 million with no buyers in 18 months.

Petrov said he tried to persuade two former colleagues to enroll. Both declined. One, he said, was 58.

"He told me the math didn't work," Petrov said. "I don't think he was wrong."

The state has renewed Hartwell Works for two more years, and program director Carmen Ruiz said she expects to enroll 200 additional workers from other industries facing layoffs. Alvarado said the city has applied for a federal Economic Adjustment Assistance grant to fund a three-year study of the program and publish a guide for other communities considering the model.
