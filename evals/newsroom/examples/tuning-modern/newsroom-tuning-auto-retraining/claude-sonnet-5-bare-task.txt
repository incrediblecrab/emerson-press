Hartwell's Retraining Program Beat the Odds — but Couldn't Beat the Wage Gap

HARTWELL, Mich. — Three years after Keystone Motors shut its assembly plant here, the city's flagship response to that shock, a retraining program called Hartwell Works, is finishing its run with the best completion rate of any plant-closure program the state has funded in a decade. It has also left many graduates earning nearly $6 less an hour than they did on the assembly line.

An April report on the $14.2 million program, financed by the Michigan Department of Labor and federal workforce grants, found that of about 1,100 former Keystone workers who enrolled, 780 completed a certification track, a 71 percent completion rate. Of those, 499 found relevant employment within six months, about 64 percent.

The numbers illustrate both the promise and the limits of retraining as a response to industrial job loss in a city of about 43,000 people that built its economy around the plant for more than five decades.

"Sixty-four percent would be a good graduation rate at a lot of colleges," Mayor Sandra Alvarado said. "For a workforce-crisis response, I do not know if it is enough."

Keystone's December 2021 closure eliminated 2,800 direct jobs and an estimated 1,400 more at suppliers and service businesses. Hartwell Community College, Vertex Systems and Clearline Manufacturing launched Hartwell Works in March 2022 to retrain displaced workers for new careers.

The program's manufacturing tracks — CNC machining, industrial electrical and welding — performed best, with an 83 percent completion rate and 72 percent placement rate. Computer-skills tracks lagged badly, completing just 38 percent of enrollees; administrators attributed the gap to workers with no prior computer background.

Age also mattered. Enrollees over 50 made up 40 percent of the program, but only 51 percent of completers in that group found relevant work within six months, compared with 64 percent overall.

James Petrov, 52, worked at Keystone for 19 years, earning $31 an hour. He completed the CNC operator track in 2023 and now works at Clearline Manufacturing for $23 an hour.

"I am working. My house is paid for," Petrov said. "But I will not make what I made at Keystone again."

Across the program, completers who found jobs are earning an average of $22.50 an hour, compared with $28.40 at Keystone before the plant closed — a gap of $5.90 an hour.

Petrov said he tried to persuade two former colleagues to enroll. Both declined. "He was 58," Petrov said of one. "He told me the math didn't work. I don't think he was wrong."

Roughly 1,700 of Keystone's 2,800 former workers never enrolled in Hartwell Works at all. Program administrators estimate about 400 left the region, 300 retired early, and roughly 1,000 remain unemployed or in part-time work.

Vertex Systems has hired 64 program completers. A company spokeswoman called the arrangement "mutually beneficial" but declined to say whether the jobs pay wages comparable to Keystone's.

The ripple effects extend to city finances. Hartwell's combined property and income tax revenue fell 19 percent from 2021 to 2024, straining street maintenance and the fire department. The 65-acre former Keystone site, demolished in 2023, has been listed for sale at $8.5 million for 18 months without a buyer.

Despite the wage losses, state officials point to the completion numbers as a success. The Michigan Department of Labor said Hartwell Works had the highest per-enrollee completion rate of any plant-closure program it had funded in the past decade.

The city has applied for a federal Economic Adjustment Assistance grant to fund a three-year study of the program and publish a guide for other communities facing similar closures.

Hartwell Works has also been renewed for two more years of state funding. Program director Carmen Ruiz said she expects to enroll about 200 additional workers from other local industries now facing layoffs.

For Alvarado, the program remains a qualified success. "We kept people working," she said. "We did not keep them whole."
