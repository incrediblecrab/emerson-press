# New drone delivery rule: Where flights can go and what remains unresolved

*Fictional explainer based on a reporting snapshot as of March 20, 2025.*

Commercial delivery drones will be allowed to fly beyond an operator's sight in designated corridors across 12 large U.S. cities under a federal rule taking effect Sept. 1.

The Federal Airspace Safety Commission's Airspace Corridor Rule 14-CR-2025, issued this month, creates the first permanent framework for such flights in urban airspace. It sets altitude, cargo and operating limits, but leaves questions about recorded footage, noise and air traffic monitoring unresolved.

For residents, the change does not mean delivery drones can fly anywhere. Operators will need permits, must stay within designated corridors and will face restrictions even inside those boundaries.

## What changes under the rule?

The prior framework generally required operators to keep drones within their line of sight, with exceptions under limited temporary waivers. Commercial drone delivery has operated experimentally in fewer than a dozen U.S. locations under those waivers.

The new rule provides a permanent route to flights beyond visual line of sight within designated urban corridors. It initially covers 12 cities with populations exceeding 1 million. Additional cities may apply for corridor designation beginning in January 2026.

"These corridors create a predictable, safe operating environment that protects both the public and emerging commercial operators," commission Chair Janet Ortega said.

## Where and when can drones fly?

Under the rule, drones may fly between 200 and 400 feet above ground level within designated corridors. Commercial operations are limited to 6 a.m. to 9 p.m. local time.

Drones cannot operate within one mile of hospitals, schools, prisons or emergency response facilities, regardless of corridor boundaries.

The commission determines corridor boundaries in consultation with local aviation authorities. Municipalities have 30 days to object before a designation becomes final.

According to the commission, operators flying outside designated corridors or permitted hours face civil penalties of up to $25,000 per violation.

## What can they carry, and who can operate them?

The maximum payload is 5 pounds. That excludes larger grocery orders and many appliances, but it does not prohibit grocery deliveries: An order weighing 5 pounds or less falls within the payload limit.

Operators must hold a Remote Operator Certificate and a corridor-specific operating permit. The permit costs $500 per year per operator.

Skyward Logistics, QuickDeliver and AirRoute have filed permit applications. Those filings are not permit approvals.

## What about privacy, noise and other aircraft?

The rule permits cameras and sensors necessary for navigation but does not require operators to delete footage captured during deliveries.

Alan Thorpe, research director at the advocacy organization Digital Rights Watch, said: "A drone at 300 feet with a package is also a drone with a camera, and the rule says almost nothing about what operators can record."

The rule does not set noise standards. The commission said noise limits would be addressed in a separate rulemaking expected in 2026.

Local air traffic controllers' unions also raised concerns that the rule does not address how drone traffic in corridors will be monitored and separated from low-altitude manned aircraft.

## What benefits are projected?

The commission estimates that corridor deliveries could reduce short-haul delivery truck trips in covered cities by 5% to 12% if widely adopted. That is a conditional projection, not a measured reduction.

The Commercial Drone Alliance said the rule would "unlock hundreds of millions of dollars in investment in same-day delivery infrastructure." That, too, is a forecast rather than investment already made.
