# New drone delivery rule opens urban flight corridors, leaves privacy questions

Commercial delivery drones will gain a permanent route to fly beyond their operators’ sight in designated urban corridors under a federal rule taking effect Sept. 1. The framework sets limits on altitude, package weight and operating hours, but leaves questions about privacy, noise and air traffic oversight.

The Federal Airspace Safety Commission issued Airspace Corridor Rule 14-CR-2025 this month. It initially covers 12 U.S. cities with populations exceeding 1 million. Additional cities may apply for corridor designation beginning in January 2026.

Here is what the rule will allow — and what remains unresolved.

## What changes?

Until now, commercial drone delivery has operated on a limited experimental basis in fewer than a dozen U.S. locations under temporary waivers. Outside those exceptions, operators had to keep drones within their visual line of sight.

The new rule creates the first permanent framework permitting commercial drones to fly beyond an operator’s line of sight in urban airspace. That permission applies within designated corridors, not throughout a covered city.

“These corridors create a predictable, safe operating environment that protects both the public and emerging commercial operators,” commission Chair Janet Ortega said.

## Where and when can drones fly?

Drones may operate between 200 and 400 feet above ground level inside designated corridors. Commercial flights are limited to 6 a.m. to 9 p.m. local time.

Even within a corridor, drones cannot operate within one mile of hospitals, schools, prisons or emergency response facilities.

The commission determines corridor boundaries in consultation with local aviation authorities. Municipalities have 30 days to object before a designation becomes final.

Operators that fly outside designated corridors or permitted hours face civil penalties of up to $25,000 per violation, according to the commission.

## What can they carry, and who can operate them?

The maximum payload is 5 pounds. That rules out larger grocery orders and many appliances, but not a grocery order weighing 5 pounds or less.

Operators must hold a Remote Operator Certificate and a corridor-specific operating permit. The permit costs $500 a year per operator.

As of March 20, Skyward Logistics, QuickDeliver and AirRoute have filed applications for corridor operating permits. Those filings are applications, not approvals.

The Commercial Drone Alliance said the rule would “unlock hundreds of millions of dollars in investment in same-day delivery infrastructure.”

The commission estimates drone deliveries could reduce short-haul delivery truck trips in covered cities by 5% to 12% if widely adopted. That is a conditional projection, not a guaranteed reduction.

## What about privacy and noise?

The rule permits cameras and sensors necessary for navigation, but does not require operators to delete footage captured during deliveries.

“A drone at 300 feet with a package is also a drone with a camera, and the rule says almost nothing about what operators can record,” said Alan Thorpe, research director at the advocacy organization Digital Rights Watch.

Noise standards are also outside the rule’s scope. The commission said noise limits would be addressed in a separate rulemaking expected in 2026, after the corridor framework takes effect.

## What safety concerns remain?

Local air traffic controllers’ unions have raised concerns that the rule does not address how corridor drone traffic will be monitored and separated from low-altitude manned aircraft.

The commission processed 3,400 public comments during the rulemaking period, with a majority coming from residents of the initial 12 cities.

For residents and operators, the September start establishes a permanent legal framework with defined flight restrictions. It does not settle every question about how expanded drone deliveries will affect city life.
