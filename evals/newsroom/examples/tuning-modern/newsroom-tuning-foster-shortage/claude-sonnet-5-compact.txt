**In a Texas county, foster children are living in hotels — and a state ultimatum is forcing a reckoning**

ALCOTT COUNTY, Texas — Marcus is 9 years old. For four months, he has lived in a hotel room, one of 83 children in this county's foster care system who have nowhere else to go.

The children are housed in hotels, county office suites and, in at least one case, a staff member's private home, because Alcott County Child Welfare Services cannot find them family placements. The agency has 847 children in its care and lists 312 licensed foster homes.

"We are running a de facto shelter system inside a child welfare agency, and that is not what any of these children need," said Beverly Chen, the agency's director.

The Texas Department of State Health and Family Services cited the county in March for maintaining what it called non-compliant placements and ordered it to cut the share of children in non-family settings below 10% of its total caseload within 90 days. Raymond Park, the state regional director who issued the citation, said the county could face a state takeover of its foster care operations if it does not comply.

With the caseload fixed at 847, the county would need to keep no more than 84 children in non-family settings to hit that threshold. Its 83 current placements already put it at about 9.8%, but Park's citation also targeted the conditions of those placements, not just their number — meaning the numerical threshold alone would not resolve the county's problem.

The strain traces in part to staffing. The agency has 98 funded caseworker positions, but 34 are vacant, a vacancy rate of roughly 35%. Starting pay for caseworkers here is $41,000 a year, compared with roughly $54,000 in neighboring states. Foster families, meanwhile, are leaving at a rate of about 28% a year, and the county reimburses them $29 a day per child, below the state average of $38.

The county's foster care budget was cut 12% in the 2024 fiscal year after a property-tax revenue shortfall forced broader county cuts. Chen said she had warned the county commissioners' court about the agency's deteriorating finances and operations since mid-2023, but received no additional money until after the state's citation. Commissioners have since allocated an emergency $750,000, which Chen called "helpful but not sufficient to resolve the structural problem."

In January, the county signed an 18-month, $2.1 million contract with Bright Futures Partners, a private placement firm, to manage the hotel and office placements. Chen described the arrangement as "a stopgap," saying the agency has identified no long-term fix for children it cannot place with families.

State lawmakers are weighing a possible partial remedy. House Bill 1402 would raise the statewide foster parent reimbursement rate to $52 a day. It has cleared committee but has not been scheduled for a floor vote.

The county has also launched a recruitment campaign, using paid social media ads since February to draw new foster families. It has logged 67 inquiries and approved 11 new homes. Elena Bell, a child welfare researcher at South Plains College, said recruitment alone is unlikely to solve the county's problems and pointed to reimbursement rates and caseworker support as areas needing attention. She did not evaluate the county's new campaign directly.

The advocacy group Foster Care Reform Alliance has called for a state takeover of the county's foster care system.

Licensing a new foster home now takes an average of seven months, down from a peak of 10 months in 2022 — a modest gain, Chen said, but not enough on its own to end the county's reliance on hotel rooms and office suites for children like Marcus.
