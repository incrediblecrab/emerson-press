Foster Care System in Alcott County Buckles as State Threatens Takeover

Eighty-three children in Alcott County's foster care system are living in hotel rooms, county office suites and, in one case, a staff member's private home — a stopgap arrangement that has drawn a state citation and the threat of a takeover.

The Texas Department of State Health and Family Services cited Alcott County Child Welfare Services in March for maintaining non-compliant placements and gave the agency 90 days to bring the share of children in non-family settings below 10% of its caseload. Regional Director Raymond Park, who issued the citation, said the county could face state receivership of its foster care operations if it fails to comply.

"We are running a de facto shelter system inside a child welfare agency, and that is not what any of these children need," said Beverly Chen, the agency's director.

The math behind the citation is close. The agency has 847 children in its care, and the 83 in temporary placements amount to about 9.8% of that caseload — already under the 10% threshold. But state officials say the citation is aimed at the conditions of those placements, not just their number, meaning bringing the percentage down further would not by itself resolve the violation.

One of those children, referred to in county records as Marcus, is 9 years old and has spent four months living in a hotel. Staff say they have not found a family placement that meets his needs.

The agency lists 312 licensed foster homes, though the notes reviewed do not specify how many available beds those homes offer or how many could accept children with needs like Marcus's.

Behind the placement crunch is a staffing shortage. The agency has 98 funded caseworker positions but 34 vacancies — a vacancy rate of roughly 35%. Starting caseworkers in Alcott County earn $41,000 a year, compared with an average of about $54,000 in neighboring states. Foster families, meanwhile, are reimbursed $29 per day per child, below the state average of $38. Annual turnover among the county's foster families runs about 28%.

Chen said she had been warning the county commissioners' court about the agency's financial and operational strain since mid-2023 but received no additional funding until after the March citation. Commissioners then approved an emergency $750,000 allocation, which Chen called "helpful but not sufficient to resolve the structural problem." The foster care budget had been cut 12% in fiscal year 2024 amid a broader round of county reductions tied to a property-tax revenue shortfall.

In January, the agency signed an 18-month, $2.1 million contract with Bright Futures Partners, a private placement firm, to manage the hotel and office placements. Chen described the contract as "a stopgap," saying the agency has not identified a long-term solution for children it cannot place in family homes.

State lawmakers are weighing a possible fix to one piece of the puzzle. House Bill 1402 would raise the statewide foster parent daily reimbursement rate to $52. The bill has cleared committee but has not been scheduled for a floor vote.

The agency has also tried to recruit more foster families, launching a paid social media campaign in February that has drawn 67 inquiries and led to 11 newly approved homes as of April. Elena Bell, a child welfare researcher at South Plains College, said recruitment alone won't solve Alcott County's problems and urged officials to also examine reimbursement rates and caseworker support.

The county, with a population of about 210,000, has no homeless shelter; the hotels housing foster children are commercial properties billed to the county at market rates. The average time to license a new foster home is now seven months, down from a peak of 10 months in 2022.

The Foster Care Reform Alliance, an advocacy group, called for a state takeover of the county's foster care system in a statement issued after the March citation.
