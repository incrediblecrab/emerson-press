# With foster homes scarce, Texas county houses children in hotels and offices

For four months, a hotel has been home to a 9-year-old identified in Alcott County records as Marcus. County staff say they have not found a family placement that meets his needs.

He is one of 83 foster children living in hotels, county office suites and a staff member’s private home in the Texas county, where a shortage of suitable placements has brought state scrutiny and the threat of a takeover.

“We are running a de facto shelter system inside a child welfare agency, and that is not what any of these children need,” said Beverly Chen, director of Alcott County Child Welfare Services.

As of April 3, the agency has 847 children in its care and lists 312 licensed foster homes. Those numbers do not establish the county’s total capacity: A home can accommodate more than one child, and the available figures do not show how many licensed beds are open or suitable for particular children.

The crisis extends beyond recruiting more families. About 35% of the agency’s funded caseworker positions are vacant, foster families leave the system at an annual rate of about 28%, and the county cut its foster care budget by 12% in fiscal 2024 after a property tax revenue shortfall.

In March, the Texas Department of State Health and Family Services cited the county for noncompliant placements. It ordered the county to bring the share of children in nonfamily settings below 10% of its caseload within 90 days.

The county’s current figures already meet that numerical threshold. Regulators count all 83 temporary placements as nonfamily settings, about 9.8% of the caseload. With 847 children in care, the county could have no more than 84 in such settings and remain below 10%.

But meeting that percentage does not resolve the citation over placement conditions. State Regional Director Raymond Park, who issued the citation, said the county had 90 days to comply or face potential state receivership of its foster care operations.

The Foster Care Reform Alliance called for a state takeover after the citation.

For now, the county is paying for an improvised system. In January, the agency signed an 18-month, $2.1 million contract with private placement firm Bright Futures Partners to manage hotel and office placements. The commercial hotels bill the county at market rates. Alcott County, with about 210,000 residents, has no homeless shelter.

Chen called the contract a “stopgap” and said the agency had identified no long-term solution for children it cannot place with families.

The county commissioners allocated an emergency $750,000 after the citation. Chen said she had raised the financial and operational crisis with the commissioners’ court since mid-2023 but received no additional funding until March.

The emergency money was “helpful but not sufficient to resolve the structural problem,” she said.

The agency has 34 vacancies among 98 funded caseworker positions. Starting pay is $41,000 a year, compared with approximately $54,000 in neighboring states. Foster parents receive $29 a day per child, below the state average of $38.

A recruitment campaign launched in February using paid social media advertising had received 67 inquiries and approved 11 new foster families as of April. Licensing a new home takes an average of seven months, down from 10 months in 2022.

Elena Bell, a child welfare researcher at South Plains College, said recruitment alone would not solve the county’s problems and recommended examining reimbursement and caseworker support.

One potential change remains pending in the Legislature. House Bill 1402 would raise foster parent reimbursement to $52 a day statewide. It has passed committee but has not been scheduled for a floor vote.

For Marcus, no family placement has yet been found.
