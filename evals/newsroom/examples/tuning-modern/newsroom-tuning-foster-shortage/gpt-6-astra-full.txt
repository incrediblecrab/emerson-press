# Foster children wait in hotels as Texas county struggles to find homes

For four months, a hotel has been home to a 9-year-old identified in Alcott County records as Marcus. County staff say they have found no foster family placement that meets his needs.

He is one of 83 children housed in hotels, county office suites and a staff member's private home as the Texas county struggles to find suitable places for children in its care. The hotels are commercial lodging properties, billed to the county at market rates.

A state citation issued in March has given the county 90 days to comply or face a potential state receivership of its foster care operations. An emergency funding allocation and a recruitment campaign have offered some help, but the agency says it has no long-term solution for children it cannot place with families.

"We are running a de facto shelter system inside a child welfare agency, and that is not what any of these children need," said Beverly Chen, director of Alcott County Child Welfare Services.

The agency has 847 children in its care and lists 312 licensed foster homes. Those figures alone do not establish its placement capacity: Homes can accommodate more than one child, and the available figures do not show total licensed beds or vacancies suitable for particular children.

The Texas Department of State Health and Family Services cited the county for maintaining noncompliant placements and ordered it to bring the share of children in nonfamily settings below 10% within 90 days. State Regional Director Raymond Park, who issued the citation, said failure to comply could lead to receivership.

The county is already below that numerical threshold. Regulators count the 83 temporary placements as nonfamily settings, about 9.8% of the caseload. With 847 children in care, the county could have at most 84 in such settings and remain below 10%. But meeting that percentage does not resolve the citation over placement conditions.

The agency faces staffing shortages as well. Of its 98 funded caseworker positions, 34 are vacant, roughly 35%. Starting salaries are $41,000 a year, compared with approximately $54,000 for comparable positions in neighboring states.

Foster families receive $29 per child per day, below the state average of $38. Their annual turnover rate in the county is approximately 28%.

The county cut its foster care budget by 12% in fiscal 2024 amid broader reductions following a property-tax revenue shortfall. Chen said she had alerted the commissioners' court to the financial and operational crisis since mid-2023 but received no additional funding until the March citation.

Commissioners then allocated an emergency $750,000. Chen called it "helpful but not sufficient to resolve the structural problem."

In January, the agency signed an 18-month, $2.1 million contract with private placement firm Bright Futures Partners to manage hotel and office placements. Chen described the arrangement as a "stopgap."

A paid social media recruitment campaign launched in February had generated 67 inquiries and 11 approved foster families by early April. Those totals do not establish the campaign's effect on available placements. Licensing a new foster home takes an average of seven months, down from a peak of 10 months in 2022.

Elena Bell, a child welfare researcher at South Plains College, said recruitment alone would not resolve the county's problems and recommended examining reimbursement and caseworker support.

Texas House Bill 1402 would raise foster parent reimbursement to $52 a day statewide. It has passed committee but has not been scheduled for a floor vote. The Foster Care Reform Alliance, meanwhile, called for a state takeover after the citation.

For Marcus, the unresolved question remains where he can live with a family. County staff say they have yet to find that placement.
