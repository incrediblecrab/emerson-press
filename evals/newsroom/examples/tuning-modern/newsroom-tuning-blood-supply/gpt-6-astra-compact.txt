# O-negative blood shortage: Why supplies are low and what donors can do

The National Blood Reserve Network issued its highest shortage alert Tuesday, July 15, saying its O-negative blood inventory had fallen to 2.1 days' supply, below its five-day operational safety minimum.

The Level 3 alert comes as Calloway Medical Center postponed 14 elective surgeries requiring transfusions. Emergency and urgent procedures will continue using existing stockpiles, the hospital said.

The network estimates it needs approximately 1,200 additional O-negative units per week nationally to restore safe inventory levels. Here is what the shortage means for patients and potential donors.

## Why is O-negative blood important?

O-negative red blood cells are commonly used in emergency red-cell transfusions when a patient's blood type is unknown, according to the network's reporting materials. That does not make every O-negative blood component universally compatible. Clinical protocols and compatibility testing still apply.

Only people with O-negative blood, approximately 7% of the U.S. population, can contribute to the O-negative supply, according to the network.

"O-negative blood cannot be manufactured. When donations drop, there is no alternative," said Dr. Patricia Osei, the network's chief medical officer.

## What is behind the shortage?

June O-negative donations were 18% below January's level, according to network data.

The network's records also show a broader summer decline: From 2022 through 2024, monthly summer donation totals were 15% to 25% below January totals in the same year. Those figures combine all blood types, rather than measuring O-negative donations alone. Network staff attributed part of the pattern to workplaces, universities and schools suspending collection drives.

Four of the network's six regional collection centers reported inventory below 40% of their operational targets Tuesday.

Blood also has storage limits. Under the methods described in the network's background sheet, refrigerated red-cell units may be stored for up to 42 days. Platelet products have shorter labeled storage periods and a separate inventory system.

## How are hospitals and the network responding?

Beyond Calloway's surgery postponements, trauma surgeons at several hospitals contacted by the network reported adjusting protocols to reserve O-negative blood for situations in which no other compatible type is available.

A Level 3 shortage activates direct donor phone outreach and deployment of mobile collection units to high-traffic locations. Osei said the network expected to appeal to regular donors by the end of the week and was working with major employers to organize workplace drives.

The network's last comparable shortage, in summer 2022, triggered a lower, Level 2 alert. It was resolved within four weeks following a coordinated donation drive. That history does not establish how long the current shortage will last.

## Who can donate, and where?

The network accepts donations at its regional centers and mobile collection events, with appointments available at nbrn.example.org.

Its centers require at least 56 days between whole-blood donations and screen for other eligibility requirements. Having O-negative blood does not waive the waiting period or screening.

In a network-commissioned survey, 650 of 1,000 adult respondents said they believed they were eligible to donate but had never done so. Those responses do not establish clinical eligibility or the share of all Americans who could donate.

Calloway said it would consider directed donations from relatives for some elective patients. But those donations are not an immediate workaround: The hospital estimated processing would take at least 48 hours, without guaranteeing availability then. Related donors and their units still require eligibility, compatibility and safety screening.
