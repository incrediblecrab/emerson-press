Six years later, the neighborhoods that moved do not flood — LOWLAND COUNTY BUYOUT AT A CROSSROADS

**LOWLAND COUNTY** — Where Shoreline Community Elementary once stood, marsh grass now grows over 340 acres of restored wetland. A few miles away, Delia Marsh still lives in the house her parents left her, one of 112 properties whose owners refused to sell.

"This is where I was born and this is where I intend to stay," said Marsh, 71.

The divide between those two pieces of ground captures what Lowland County's buyout program has and hasn't resolved, six years after Hurricane Marion tore through this coastal community.

The Category 3 storm struck in September 2017, flooding about 800 homes, many for the second or third time. In early 2019, the county launched a buyout program funded by $62 million from a FEMA Hazard Mitigation Grant and an $18 million county match — $80 million in all. Homeowners were offered 105% of their property's pre-flood assessed value, with the extra 5% meant to help cover relocation costs.

As of April, the county had completed 240 buyouts. The purchased homes, along with the shuttered elementary school and the Shoreline Diner, a 38-year-old gathering spot, have been demolished and replaced with restored wetland.

"Six years later, the neighborhoods that moved do not flood," said Robert Yates, the county's emergency manager. "The ones that stayed do."

Those who stayed have paid for it. The 112 owners who declined to sell saw their properties flood again in both 2019 and 2022, with average repair costs of about $34,000 per storm, according to county figures. Marsh said she has repaired her home twice since 2017 and carries flood insurance, though she declined to discuss what she has spent or collected.

Two of the holdout owners have since asked the county about selling. There is currently no program to accommodate them; the $3.1 million in unspent FEMA funds reverted to the federal government in January.

The program took twice as long as planned — six years instead of three — partly because title disputes on 47 properties required legal resolution.

County engineers say the wetland has measurably reduced flood risk behind it, modeling lower peak flood levels than a scenario without restoration, though they stop short of guaranteeing protection in any given storm. A 2024 consultant's model estimated the wetland could retain about 280,000 gallons of runoff in one specified rain event — a projection for that scenario, not a storm-surge figure.

Yates estimated that repeated disaster recovery and insurance costs for the 240 bought-out properties would have run about $112 million over six years, compared with the program's $80 million budget.

Neighboring Coastal Pines County took a different path, spending $45 million on levee repairs and beach nourishment instead of retreat. It flooded significantly again in 2022.

Dr. Maya Santos, a researcher at North Coastal University who has studied Lowland County's program for four years, said its long-term success depends on where displaced residents land. Her research found that about 60% of the 240 households that sold stayed in the county, while 40% relocated elsewhere, some leaving North Carolina entirely.

"Whether this works depends heavily on whether people find housing they can afford," Santos said.

Congress is now weighing whether to require localities to adopt managed retreat plans to keep participating in the National Flood Insurance Program. Lowland County officials have testified in favor. FEMA and the Coastal Resilience Coalition, an advocacy group, have both cited the county as a model.

The county has no plans to expand the program. For Marsh and the other holdouts, that leaves the choice they made in 2019 as the one they'll keep living with.
