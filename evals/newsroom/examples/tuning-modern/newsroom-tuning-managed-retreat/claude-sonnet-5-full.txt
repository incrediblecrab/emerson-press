**Six Years After the Storm, a North Carolina County Bet on Retreat — and Split Its Neighbors**

LOWLAND COUNTY, N.C. — Delia Marsh, 71, has repaired the house her parents left her twice since Hurricane Marion flooded it in September 2017. She has no plans to leave.

"This is where I was born and this is where I intend to stay," Marsh said.

Down the road, the houses that once stood alongside hers are gone. In their place: roughly 340 acres of restored wetland, part of a $80 million buyout program that Lowland County launched in 2019 and completed, by its own April 2025 accounting, this spring.

The county's experience is emerging as a test case in a national debate over "managed retreat" — the practice of paying homeowners to leave flood-prone land rather than rebuild on it. Congress is weighing whether to require localities to adopt such plans as a condition of staying in the National Flood Insurance Program, and Lowland County officials have testified in favor.

Marion, a Category 3 storm, flooded about 800 homes in the county in 2017, many for the second or third time. The county used $62 million from a FEMA Hazard Mitigation Grant, plus an $18 million county match, to offer homeowners 105% of their pre-flood assessed property value — the extra 5% meant to help cover moving costs.

By April, the county had completed 240 buyouts. It razed the houses, along with the former Shoreline Community Elementary School, closed since 2016 for flood damage, and the Shoreline Diner, which had served the community for 38 years.

"Six years later, the neighborhoods that moved do not flood. The ones that stayed do," said Robert Yates, the county's emergency manager.

Not everyone moved. Of the roughly 800 homes originally targeted, 112 owners declined to sell. Those properties flooded again in 2019 and 2022, with average repair costs of about $34,000 per storm, according to county figures. Two of those holdout homeowners have since asked the county about selling; there is currently no program to accommodate them.

Yates estimated that repeated disaster recovery, infrastructure repair and flood-insurance costs for the 240 bought-out properties would have run about $112 million over six years had the county not acted — a comparison he draws against the program's $80 million authorized budget, not a final expenditure figure.

The program took twice as long as planned, six years instead of three, partly because title disputes on 47 properties required legal resolution.

County engineers say modeling shows lower peak flood levels behind the restored wetland than in a scenario without it, though the county has not produced a single storm-surge reduction figure or a guarantee for future storms. A 2024 consultant's model estimated the wetland would retain about 280,000 gallons of runoff in one specified rain event — a modeled figure for that scenario, not a broader promise.

Not every county chose retreat. Neighboring Coastal Pines County rejected the approach after Marion and instead spent $45 million on levee repairs and beach nourishment. It flooded significantly again in 2022.

Dr. Maya Santos, a researcher at North Coastal University who has studied Lowland County's program for four years, said its long-term success will hinge on where displaced residents land. Her research found that about 60% of the 240 households that sold stayed in the county; the other 40% moved elsewhere, some leaving North Carolina entirely.

"The long-term effectiveness of managed retreat depends heavily on where displaced residents relocate and whether they find comparable housing affordability," Santos said.

The county, population about 18,000, absorbed an estimated $340 million in property damage from Marion. It has no plans to expand the buyout program. The remaining $3.1 million in unspent FEMA funds reverted to the federal government in January.

Marsh said she has kept flood insurance and repaired her home twice since 2017. She declined to say how much she has spent, or collected in insurance payments, to stay.
