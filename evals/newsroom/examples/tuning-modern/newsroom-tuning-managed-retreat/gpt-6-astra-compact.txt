# After flood buyouts, a coastal county weighs what it saved and lost

Delia Marsh has lived her entire life in the Lowland County home she inherited from her parents. When the county offered to buy it after repeated flooding, she refused.

"This is where I was born and this is where I intend to stay," said Marsh, 71.

She has repaired the house twice since 2017 and has flood insurance. She declined to say how much she spent or received in insurance payments.

Her decision sits at the center of a question facing this coastal North Carolina county: What does it mean for a flood buyout program to succeed? An April 2025 county review documents 240 completed purchases and approximately 340 acres of restored coastal wetland. But the program also scattered residents, removed gathering places and left some homeowners seeking help after the money was no longer available.

Lowland County launched the program in early 2019, following Hurricane Marion. The Category 3 storm struck in September 2017, flooding approximately 800 homes, many for the second or third time. Total property damage in the county of roughly 18,000 people was estimated at $340 million.

The county offered homeowners 105% of their property's pre-flood assessed value, with the extra 5% intended to help with relocation. A $62 million Federal Emergency Management Agency grant and an $18 million county match provided an $80 million budget.

The approach, known as managed retreat, removes buildings from flood-prone land rather than trying to keep water away from them.

"Six years later, the neighborhoods that moved do not flood. The ones that stayed do," County Emergency Manager Robert Yates said.

The distinction is that the purchased properties no longer have buildings to flood. County engineers also modeled lower peak flood levels behind the restored wetland than in a comparison without restoration, but those results offer no guarantee of protection in every storm.

Of the roughly 800 homes initially targeted, 112 owners declined to sell. According to the county review, those properties flooded in both 2019 and 2022, with repair costs averaging approximately $34,000 per storm. The available reporting does not establish the status of the other initially targeted properties beyond the completed purchases and refusals.

The work took six years instead of the projected three, partly because title disputes on 47 properties required legal resolution. Among the structures demolished were the former Shoreline Community Elementary School, closed since 2016 because of flood damage, and the Shoreline Diner, which had operated for 38 years.

Maya Santos, a North Coastal University researcher who has studied the program for four years, said managed retreat's long-term effectiveness depends heavily on where residents move and whether they find comparably affordable housing.

Her research found that roughly 60% of the 240 households that sold stayed within the county. The other 40% moved to other regions, some outside North Carolina.

Yates estimated that repeated disaster recovery, infrastructure repairs and flood insurance supplementation for the purchased properties would have cost approximately $112 million over six years. That is an estimate of costs had the buyouts not occurred, not documented savings. His comparison used the program's $80 million authorized budget; a final expenditure total was not supplied.

The county's experience now figures in a congressional debate over whether communities should need managed retreat plans to renew participation in the National Flood Insurance Program. Lowland County officials have testified in favor.

At home, however, the program is closed. The county has no expansion plans, and $3.1 million in unspent FEMA funds reverted to the federal government in January.

Two homeowners who initially refused have since asked to sell. The county has no current program to accommodate them. Marsh still intends to stay.
