# In Lowland County, flood buyouts leave wetlands and unfinished choices

Delia Marsh has repaired her home twice since 2017. At 71, she still intends to stay in the house she inherited from her parents, even as Lowland County has bought and demolished hundreds of flood-prone homes.

"This is where I was born and this is where I intend to stay," Marsh said.

Her decision is one of the unresolved questions behind the North Carolina coastal county's six-year effort to move residents out of repeatedly flooded neighborhoods. An April 2025 county review records 240 completed home buyouts and approximately 340 acres of restored coastal wetland. It also documents owners who declined to sell and a program that has no plans to expand.

The county began buying homes in early 2019, after Hurricane Marion flooded approximately 800 homes in September 2017, many for the second or third time. The Category 3 storm caused an estimated $340 million in property damage in a county of roughly 18,000 people, according to the county's reporting.

The buyout program offered homeowners 105% of their property's pre-flood assessed value, with the additional 5% intended to help cover relocation costs. Its $80 million budget combined a $62 million FEMA Hazard Mitigation Grant with an $18 million county match.

The approach, known as managed retreat, removes homes from exposed land rather than trying to protect them in place.

"Six years later, the neighborhoods that moved do not flood. The ones that stayed do," County Emergency Manager Robert Yates said.

On the acquired properties, the homes are gone. County engineers have modeled lower peak flood levels behind the restored wetland than in a comparison without restoration, but that finding does not establish protection in every storm.

The county review identifies 112 property owners who declined buyouts. Those properties flooded in both 2019 and 2022, with owners facing average repair costs of approximately $34,000 per storm event. The supplied record does not classify the other properties among the roughly 800 originally targeted.

Marsh said she had flood insurance but declined to say how much she had spent on repairs or received in insurance payments.

Leaving carried costs beyond the price of a house. Structures on the acquired land included the former Shoreline Community Elementary School, closed since 2016 because of flood damage, and the Shoreline Diner, a gathering place that had operated for 38 years.

Maya Santos, a researcher at North Coastal University who has studied the program for four years, said managed retreat's long-term effectiveness depends heavily on where residents relocate and whether they find comparably affordable housing. Her research found that roughly 60% of the 240 households stayed within the county. The rest moved to other regions, some outside North Carolina.

The purchases took twice the projected three years, partly because title disputes involving 47 properties required legal resolution.

Yates estimated that repeated disaster recovery, infrastructure repair and flood insurance supplementation for the 240 properties would have cost approximately $112 million over six years. He compared that estimate with the $80 million authorized budget, not a final expenditure total. The comparison does not establish realized savings.

Lowland County officials have testified in favor of a proposal Congress is debating that would require managed retreat plans as a condition for renewing participation in the National Flood Insurance Program.

At home, however, the county has no current program for additional sellers. The remaining $3.1 million in unspent FEMA funds reverted to the federal government in January.

Two owners who originally declined have since asked the county to buy their properties. For now, there is no program to accommodate them.
