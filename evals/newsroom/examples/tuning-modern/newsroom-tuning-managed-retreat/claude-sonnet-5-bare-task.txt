**In North Carolina County, a Retreat From the Coast Pays Off — for Some**

LOWLAND COUNTY, N.C. — Six years after Hurricane Marion tore through this coastal county, flooding roughly 800 homes, county officials say a controversial decision to abandon rebuilding in favor of retreat is showing results — even as it leaves behind hard questions about who benefits and who gets left behind.

An April review by county officials shows Lowland County has completed 240 home buyouts since launching the program in 2019, demolishing the structures and converting the land into about 340 acres of restored coastal wetland.

"Six years later, the neighborhoods that moved do not flood," said County Emergency Manager Robert Yates. "The ones that stayed do."

Hurricane Marion, a Category 3 storm, struck in September 2017, causing an estimated $340 million in property damage in a county of about 18,000 residents. Many homes flooded for the second or third time.

The buyout program was funded with $62 million from a FEMA Hazard Mitigation Grant and an $18 million county match, for an $80 million total. Homeowners were offered 105% of their property's pre-flood assessed value, with the extra 5% meant to help cover relocation costs.

Among the properties acquired were the former Shoreline Community Elementary School, closed since 2016 due to flood damage, and the Shoreline Diner, a gathering spot that had operated for 38 years.

Not everyone left. Of the roughly 800 homes originally targeted, 112 owners declined to sell. Those properties flooded again in both 2019 and 2022, with owners facing average repair costs of about $34,000 per storm.

Delia Marsh, 71, is among them. She inherited her home from her parents and has lived there her whole life.

"This is where I was born and this is where I intend to stay," Marsh said. She declined to disclose how much she has spent on repairs or received from flood insurance, though she said she has rebuilt twice since 2017.

The program took six years instead of the projected three, partly because of title disputes on 47 properties that required legal resolution.

County engineers say models show lower peak flood levels behind the restored wetland compared with a scenario without restoration, though officials caution there is no guarantee of protection in every storm. A 2024 consultant's model estimated the wetland could retain about 280,000 gallons of runoff during one specified rain event.

Dr. Maya Santos, a researcher at North Coastal University who has studied the program for four years, said its long-term success depends heavily on where displaced residents end up. Her research found that about 60% of the 240 households that sold stayed within the county, while 40% relocated elsewhere, some leaving North Carolina altogether.

The county's approach contrasts with that of neighboring Coastal Pines County, which rejected managed retreat after Marion and instead spent $45 million on levee repairs and beach nourishment. It flooded significantly again in 2022.

Yates estimated that continuing to rebuild the 240 bought-out properties, rather than buying them out, would have cost the county roughly $112 million over six years in recovery, infrastructure repair and insurance costs — compared with the program's $80 million authorized budget.

Still, the county has no plans to expand the effort. Its remaining $3.1 million in unspent FEMA funds reverted to the federal government in January. Two of the 112 holdout homeowners have since asked to sell, but no program currently exists to accommodate them.

Lowland County's experience has drawn national attention, with FEMA and the Coastal Resilience Coalition citing it as a model for communities facing repeated flooding. Congress is now debating whether to require localities to adopt managed retreat plans as a condition of participating in the National Flood Insurance Program. Lowland County officials have testified in favor of the measure.

For Marsh, the policy debate is beside the point.

"I intend to stay," she said.
