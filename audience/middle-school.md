# Audience: Middle School

Reader is in grades 6 through 8. Two moves define the band. A counterclaim now belongs in the draft rather than being optional, and sources start being named and told apart rather than blurred into `studies show`. WIDA expects writers here to put a claim forward for others to contest, answer the objections that come back, and tell a primary source from a secondary one.

## Detect

### Under-reach

- One-step cause where the situation has several.
- No source or evidence language anywhere: no according to, no said, no named origin for a fact the writer could not have observed.
- Every term over-explained, as though the reader shares none of the writer's schooling.
- Facts listed with no grouping or contrast.
- A counterclaim that exists only to lose — built weak so it can be knocked down.
- Narrative names events but not viewpoint, tension, motive, or stakes.

### Over-reach

- Stacked nominalizations: `the utilization of renewable energy sources demonstrates significant potential for the mitigation of environmental degradation`.
- Clauses nested past two layers.
- Discipline terms used with no cue that the writer knows they are technical.
- A hedge wall standing in for a position: `it could be argued that this may potentially suggest`.
- Citation apparatus imported for its own sake — a works-cited page attached to a piece that names no sources in the prose.
- Vocabulary that would not survive the question "what does that mean?"

### Either way

- Evidence quoted and then abandoned, never connected to the point it was meant to prove.
- Sources treated as interchangeable, with no named origin distinguishing one from another.
- Comma splices joining two full sentences. This is the signature error of the band.

## Write

- Assume the reader can track theme, systems, data, and competing views when the text signposts them.
- Define new academic terms in context, then reuse them without redefining.
- Keep sentences direct. Add a dependent clause only where it buys precision.
- Make relationships explicit: because, however, as a result, in contrast.
- For inform and explain, group facts by class, by part and whole, by sequence, or by cause.
- For argument, state the claim, give the strongest honest version of the other side, then answer it with evidence.
- Say where a source came from: who said it, where it was published, when. Whether it deserves trust is the next band's question.
- Tell a primary source from a secondary one and say which you are using.
- For narrative, orient the reader, control pace, and close the arc.
- Own the sentence. One clear qualifier beats three stacked ones.
- Reaching above grade level is fine when you control it. If you cannot define the term, or say why the sentence needs that clause, cut it.

## Mechanics

Reliable control expected by the end of this band:

- Repairing comma splices and run-ons — join with a conjunction and comma, a semicolon, or a full stop.
- Commas separating items in a simple series, in whichever form the domain in force takes.
- A colon introducing a list or a quotation longer than one sentence.
- Hyphenating a compound modifier before a noun when leaving it open would mislead.
- Commas and periods placed inside closing quotation marks; a comma rather than a period before an attribution tag.
- `said` as the default attribution verb, carrying no verdict on truth.
- Spelling out ordinals first through ninth, figures from 10th.
- Ages hyphenated as modifiers; money in figures with the sign.
- Months abbreviated only beside a specific day; a full date's year bracketed by commas; seasons lowercase.
- Common-noun elements dropping to lowercase on later reference.
- Spelling out an abbreviation on first use.
- `its` and `it's`; possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe; fewer for counts and less for bulk; affect as the verb and effect as the noun.
- Active voice as the default.

## Evidence

Rung 2. Any claim beyond the writer's own direct observation carries a named voice. `said` becomes the reflexive attribution verb, crowding out livelier synonyms that smuggle in a verdict. Interrogating a source — who funded it, what its sample leaves out — belongs to later bands.

Where this tier and a `domain/` evidence floor disagree, the higher of the two governs. The tier sets register; it never lowers the standard of proof a domain requires.

## Boundaries

Below, upper elementary attaches a reason to a claim but does not yet name who said what. Here the source acquires a name and the argument acquires an opponent. Above, high school demands the warrant — the sentence that says why this evidence supports this claim — and enough identifying detail about a source that a reader could judge its credibility.

## Examples

Before: The river changed the town and it was important.

After: The river changed the town in two ways. First, boats brought goods to the market. Later, floods damaged the same streets that helped trade grow.

Before: Phones should be allowed because students like them.

After: Students should keep phones in lockers, not at desks. Phones do distract people during class, which is the strongest case against them. But a locker rule still lets students reach family after school.

Before: The utilization of renewable energy sources demonstrates significant potential for the mitigation of environmental degradation.

After: Solar and wind power could cut pollution sharply. Germany's grid ran on more than half renewable power in 2023, and its emissions fell over the same period.

Before: The Civil War was caused by slavery, it was a very important conflict in American history.

After: The Civil War was caused by slavery. Historians debate how much economic and states-rights arguments mattered alongside it, but the seceding states named slavery in their own declarations.

Before: Studies show that sleep is important for teenagers.

After: A 2014 policy statement from the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended that middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m., because adolescent sleep cycles shift later during puberty.
