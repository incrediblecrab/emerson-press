# Education: Lower Elementary

An optional classroom preset for kindergarten through grade 3. Use concrete anchors, clear relationships and manageable sentences when those help the actual reader. Age, language background and reading fluency do not establish topic knowledge or put a ceiling on ideas.

WIDA distinguishes K, 1 and 2-3 in its classroom framework. This broad house preset is not a validated reading-level test or an individual proficiency label. Use the task, familiarity and available support to choose the explanation; do not automatically write to the youngest possible reader.

## Detect

### Under-reach

- Labels with no sentence built around them.
- A recount with no time order — events in the order they occurred to the writer rather than the order they happened.
- An opinion with no reason attached.
- The same connector repeated for every relationship: everything is `and`.

### Over-reach

- A packed noun group that hides a needed action or relationship. A simpler example must preserve scope: a transportation system is not necessarily just buses.
- More information in a sentence than this reader can follow.
- Clause nesting that obscures the connection between ideas, not a fixed number of clauses.
- Abstract nouns with no picture, example, or action anchoring them.
- Vocabulary the writer cannot explain if asked.

### Either way

- Topic drifts because the subject is never renamed — no repetition, no traceable pronoun, no sequence word.
- Cause, comparison, or claim asserted without the plain words that carry it: because, so, but, same, different.

## Write

- Keep topics concrete: people, places, objects, actions, observed events.
- Name the topic early, then repeat or rename it so the thread stays visible.
- Prefer direct sentences and explicit connections. Keep a useful dependent clause when the relationship remains clear.
- Name a known actor when needed. A clear passive is better than an invented actor.
- Anchor every new word to a label, an example, a step, or something the reader can see or do.
- For an opinion: state the view and a genuine reason. Do not add a repetitive conclusion or a factual claim to dignify a preference.
- For a recount: put events in the order they happened and mark the order with first, then, next, last.
- For a description: name the thing, then give its parts or qualities.
- Keep a big word if you can say what it means. Drop one you cannot.

## Mechanics

Useful teaching checks when relevant to the assignment, not prohibitions on other conventions:

- A sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with a mark.
- Question marks for questions. Exclamation points for real surprise, not for routine emphasis.
- Capital letters on names of people and places.
- Capitalized day and month names, spelled out.
- Add `'s` to make a single owner possessive.

Check the school's actual curriculum before assessing a student's attainment. Useful attribution, dialogue punctuation and number conventions are not reserved for later grades. The selected genre or venue controls publication mechanics, including numeral thresholds and the serial comma.

## Evidence

The legacy `evidence_rung: 0` names a practice focus: connect a view with a reason and distinguish observation from preference. It is not a lower truth standard. Name a source in understandable language when useful; a child can learn that a book, observation and opinion are different kinds of support.

Keep the selected domain's safeguards. Simplifying can narrow the explanation, never turn an unknown into a fact or demand new research merely because a preset was selected.

## Boundaries

This is an optional classroom scaffold, not a claim about what younger children can produce or what a reader may understand. Other presets offer different practice emphases, not gates that unlock syntax, sources or subject matter.

## Examples

These independent synthetic briefs supply the details for each rewrite.

**Concrete access without changing the system**

**Supplied facts:** This passage concerns neighborhood bus routes that take children to school and families to a store. It is not a description of every kind of transport.

Before: The neighborhood's transportation system affects daily routines by changing access to community resources.

After: These buses help people get places. A child can ride a bus to school. A family can ride a bus to the store.

**Explaining a supplied plant diagram**

**Supplied facts:** The lesson's bean-plant diagram shows roots taking in water, a stem supporting the plant and leaves helping it make food. The parts work together.

Before: Plants survive because roots, stems, and leaves perform different functions within a connected system.

After: This bean plant has parts. Roots take in water. The stem holds the plant up. Leaves help the plant make food. The parts work together to keep the plant alive.

**A preference without a health claim**

**Supplied facts:** The author likes recess because it is a chance to run outside with friends. No effect on classroom fatigue or health has been measured.

Before: I think recess is beneficial for student wellbeing.

After: I like recess because I can run outside with my friends.

**Preserving the order of a visit**

**Supplied facts:** The author went to a farm, saw cows, ate lunch, visited the barn and went home, in that order, and enjoyed the day. The lunch location and transport home are unknown.

Before: We went to the farm and we saw cows and we had lunch and we saw the barn and then we went home and it was fun.

After: First we went to the farm and saw the cows. Then we ate lunch and visited the barn. Last, we went home. It was a good day.

**A cancellation without an invented replacement**

**Supplied facts:** Rain caused an outdoor activity to be canceled. The activity's name and any replacement plan are not supplied.

Before: The precipitation event resulted in the cancellation of our outdoor activity.

After: The outdoor activity was canceled because it rained.
