# Education: Lower Elementary

Reader is in kindergarten through grade 3. Sentences are mostly simple or compound. Meaning rides on concrete nouns and action verbs, not on packed noun groups. K to 3 is a long climb: a kindergartner labels and recounts, a third grader can write a short organized text with headings, sequence, cause, comparison, and a reason attached to a claim.

WIDA splits that climb into three clusters — K, 1, and 2-3 — and one tier covers all three, so nothing here separates a kindergartner from a third grader. Where the difference matters, write to the younger end unless you know the grade.

## Detect

### Under-reach

- Labels with no sentence built around them.
- A recount with no time order — events in the order they occurred to the writer rather than the order they happened.
- An opinion with no reason attached.
- The same connector repeated for every relationship: everything is `and`.

### Over-reach

- Nominalization where an action verb would do. `The transportation system affects access` instead of `buses take people places`. Turning an action into a thing hides the doer and packs the sentence, and a K-3 reader tracks the sentence with the doer in it. WIDA lists condensing nominalization as a grades 4-5 feature, so it is above band here — but above band is not the same as wrong. If a writer at this band uses one and can say what it means in their own words, leave it. Rewrite it when the actor has vanished and the reader cannot get it back.
- Three ideas held inside one sentence.
- More than one dependent clause, or a clause stack the reader must unwind.
- Abstract nouns with no picture, example, or action anchoring them.
- Vocabulary the writer cannot explain if asked.

### Either way

- Topic drifts because the subject is never renamed — no repetition, no traceable pronoun, no sequence word.
- Cause, comparison, or claim asserted without the plain words that carry it: because, so, but, same, different.

## Write

- Keep topics concrete: people, places, objects, actions, observed events.
- Name the topic early, then repeat or rename it so the thread stays visible.
- Prefer short simple or compound sentences. One connector at a time.
- Put the doer in the subject slot and the action in the verb.
- Anchor every new word to a label, an example, a step, or something the reader can see or do.
- For an opinion: state the view, give one reason, restate the point.
- For a recount: put events in the order they happened and mark the order with first, then, next, last.
- For a description: name the thing, then give its parts or qualities.
- Keep a big word if you can say what it means. Drop one you cannot.

## Mechanics

What a writer should reliably control by the end of this band:

- A sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with a mark.
- Question marks for questions. Exclamation points for real surprise, not for routine emphasis.
- Capital letters on names of people and places.
- Capitalized day and month names, spelled out.
- Add `'s` to make a single owner possessive.

These are the highest-signal conventions, not the full grade-level scope. Where a state has adopted the Common Core Language standards or a close derivative, those standards put commas in a series at grade 1 (L.1.2c) and quotation marks in dialogue at grade 3 (L.3.2c), so do not treat their absence as on-band for an upper-K-3 writer. Codings differ by state and get revised — New Jersey renumbered its Language standards in the 2023 NJSLS-ELA — so check the framework in force rather than the CCSS code. Finer numeral thresholds belong to later bands. Whether a series takes a comma before the conjunction is a house-style question and belongs to the domain in force, not to a reader tier.

## Evidence

Below rung 1 of the evidence ladder. A writer learns here that an opinion takes a reason at all, and that the reason belongs in a sentence of its own. The reason may be anything the writer can point to — a thing seen, a thing done, a preference honestly held. Where the reason came from is not yet in question.

Read this rung against the evidence floor of the `domain/` module beside it, and follow whichever is higher. A tier changes register. It never lowers the standard of proof a domain requires.

## Boundaries

Below this band there is no written composition to calibrate. Above it, upper elementary adds the relative clause, a wider range of tenses for placing events against each other, a topic frame before the facts, and the first real handling of evidence: a claim supported by a reason that points at something outside the writer's own opinion.

## Examples

Before: The neighborhood's transportation system affects daily routines by changing access to community resources.

After: Buses help people get places. A child can ride a bus to school. A family can ride a bus to the store.

Before: Plants survive because roots, stems, and leaves perform different functions within a connected system.

After: A plant has parts. Roots take in water. The stem holds the plant up. Leaves help the plant make food. The parts work together to keep the plant alive.

Before: I think recess is beneficial for student wellbeing.

After: Recess is the best part of the day. We run outside, so we are not tired in class. Recess helps us.

Before: We went to the farm and we saw cows and we had lunch and we saw the barn and then we went home and it was fun.

After: First we went to the farm. We saw the cows in the field. Then we ate lunch by the barn. Last, we rode the bus home. It was a good day.

Before: The precipitation event resulted in the cancellation of our outdoor activity.

After: It rained hard. Recess was inside.
