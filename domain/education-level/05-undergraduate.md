# Education: Undergraduate

Reader is an undergraduate, or an instructor reading undergraduate work. The job is analysis inside a discipline's conventions: enter a conversation that is already running, and take a position in it that a reader could dispute.

It is not a dissertation. Padding toward that register is the most common failure at this level and the easiest to detect, because the prose acquires subordination without acquiring ideas.

WIDA stops at grade 12, so this tier rests on writing-studies sources instead: the AAC&U VALUE rubric for written communication, and the CWPA Outcomes Statement. That statement is mid-transition. Version 3.0, from 2014, still sets what most first-year writing programs assess against. Version 4.0, approved March 4, 2026, replaces universal outcomes with five knowledge domains and will not map onto a 3.0 rubric one for one. Until a program says which it follows, treat 3.0 as operative and 4.0 as the direction of travel.

## Detect

### Under-reach

- Opens by restating the prompt: `this essay will discuss`.
- Five-paragraph structure surviving into college.
- Paragraphs organized by source rather than by idea: Smith says X, then Jones says Y.
- Sources named but never compared — parallel summary with no synthesis.
- Evidence quoted and then abandoned; no analysis of the quotation itself.
- No counterargument, complication, or limitation anywhere.
- No motive: the essay answers a question no one was asking. The thesis may be perfectly arguable and still carry no stakes — nothing establishes why it needed arguing.
- Conclusion restates the introduction instead of stating what was shown.

### Over-reach

- Dissertation drag: a literature-review posture over four sources.
- Territory moves compressed to a clause, terms of art left unglossed, as though writing for peers who already share them. Showing the reader you know it is part of the task here, so compression reads as evasion rather than authority.
- Theory names invoked as decoration, doing no work a plain term would not.
- Hedges stacked on ordinary classroom-scope claims: `it could perhaps be tentatively argued that`.
- Sentence length and subordination rising while idea density stays flat.
- Claims outrunning the evidence: `proves`, `demonstrates conclusively`, `the definitive reading`, for a single-text argument.
- Terminology worn as costume — a word the writer could not define under questioning.

### Either way

- Genre conventions ignored: a lab report written like an essay, a close reading written like a policy brief.
- Sources cited but not evaluated. No sense of which is stronger, or why.
- Uniform paragraph length and shape, each opening the same way.
- Numbers reported with no baseline. Compared with what?

## Write

- Make the claim arguable. If no informed reader could disagree, it is a summary.
- Establish the motive early: the tension, gap, or problem that makes the thesis worth arguing. A claim can clear the arguability bar and still be pointless. The move is usually a `but` — the received view says X, but the evidence here does not fit it.
- Put sources in conversation. Say how they relate — agree, extend, conflict — before saying what you think.
- Analyze the evidence you quote. The quotation begins the paragraph's work rather than ending it.
- Name at least one real counterargument or complicating case and answer it.
- Follow the genre your discipline actually uses, and signal that you know which one you are in.
- Use a technical term when it does work plain wording cannot. Otherwise use plain wording.
- Reserve hedges for claims where evidence is genuinely limited. State well-supported points plainly.
- Keep sentence complexity proportional to idea complexity. A simple claim earns a simple sentence.
- Interrogate a source, do not just cite it: who funded it, how it was collected, what its sample leaves out, whether correlation is being sold as causation.
- Name the limits of your own argument before a reader has to.
- Close by stating what the essay established, not by re-summarizing.
- Keep the hard term when you can define it and it does work no plain phrase can. Drop it when it is only difficulty for its own sake.

## Mechanics

The writer is fluent in everything below. This band adds:

- Essential and nonessential appositives, where the commas depend on how many candidates the phrase could refer to.
- Parentheses treated as a signal the sentence needs rebuilding.
- Fractions under one spelled out and hyphenated in running text; the rule stops at a quotation, which keeps the form the speaker used.
- Composition titles: which get quotation marks, which get nothing, and when a foreign title is translated.
- Academic degrees preferred as a phrase over an abbreviation; `bachelor's` with an apostrophe, `Bachelor of Arts` without.
- Departments lowercase unless containing a proper noun.
- Acronyms not imposed on a reader who would not recognize them.
- Quotations never altered. If a quote is unusable as-is, paraphrase around it rather than flagging the error inside it.
- `compose`, `comprise`, and `constitute`; descriptive versus possessive compounds.

Note the style-family split. AP omits the serial comma, spells out one through nine, and puts most titles in quotation marks. Chicago and MLA disagree on all three; APA disagrees on the serial comma and on titles but takes the same one-through-nine threshold. The publication picks the family, so those are `domain/` and `citation/` decisions rather than schooling ones. Pick one family and hold it — mixing them is the most reliable sign a draft changed hands.

## Evidence

Rung 4. The writer interrogates the source rather than only citing it: funding and motive, sample adequacy, what a margin of error actually licenses, the difference between a number changing and a rate changing. A figure without a baseline asserts nothing.

Read this rung against the evidence floor of the `domain/` module beside it, and follow whichever is higher. A tier changes register. It never lowers the standard of proof a domain requires.

## Boundaries

Below, high school states the warrant linking evidence to claim, and says enough about a source for a reader to judge it. Here the writer must put sources in conversation with one another and press them on their construction, and must obey a disciplinary genre rather than a general essay form.

Above, graduate work identifies a specific nameable gap in a defined literature and warrants an original contribution to closing it. The evidence demand changes with it: at this tier you interrogate someone else's uncertainty, and at the next you quantify your own — what the design licenses, what it does not, and how much weight the conclusion can bear. The audience changes from an instructor evaluating mastery to a committee of specialists who will check the claim against the literature themselves.

## Examples

Before: Social media is a very important topic in today's society. Many studies have looked at how it affects teenagers. This essay will discuss some of the effects of social media on mental health.

After: Research on adolescent social media use complicates the simple claim that more screen time causes worse mental health. Twenge links rising use to rising depression rates; Odgers and Jensen argue the correlational data are too weak to carry a causal story. This essay takes the second position: the strongest available evidence points to displacement, social media crowding out sleep and in-person contact, rather than to platform content itself.

Before: It could perhaps be tentatively suggested that, within the broader epistemological framework of post-structuralist discourse, the aforementioned phenomenon may potentially problematize existing paradigmatic assumptions regarding textual signification.

After: A structuralist reading assumes the poem has one stable meaning. This poem resists that assumption: its central image changes meaning every time it recurs. That instability is the essay's evidence, not a reason to hedge the argument.

Before: Smith discusses the causes of the decline. Jones also discusses the decline. Additionally, Lee examines a related aspect. Patel further explores this topic.

After: Smith and Jones agree the decline is real but split on mechanism: Smith points to cost, Jones to habit. Lee's survey data support the habit account, which is why this paper follows Jones.

Before: Foucault talks about power. This relates to the prison system in important ways that show how power works in society.

After: Foucault is useful here for one specific reason: he treats surveillance as productive rather than restrictive. That reframing explains why the county's electronic monitoring program expanded even as its jail population fell.

Before: The author uses imagery. "The sea was gray and vast." This shows the mood of the passage.

After: The sea is "gray and vast," and both adjectives withhold information. One drains color; the other refuses a boundary. The description tells us nothing concrete, which is the point: Mira cannot yet see what she is facing.
