# Domain: Academia

Argument for readers who will check you. Chicago mechanics unless the field sets another. A citation module is required here; which one is a fact about the discipline, not a preference.

Scholarly writing is an intervention in a conversation. That sentence is worn smooth from use, but its consequences are not widely drawn. If a piece is an intervention, then it must be possible to say what it changes — what a reader believed before and should believe after. Writing that cannot answer that is not a weak contribution; it is a summary wearing the apparatus of one.

This is where machine-written academic prose fails, and it fails structurally rather than superficially. The grammar is fine. The citations are formatted. What is missing is *stance*, in Hyland's sense: the writer's position toward the propositions — carried by hedges, boosters, attitude markers and self-mention. Its companion, *engagement*, is the separate business of positioning the reader, and a model fails at both for one reason. A model has no stake in a field. It has read the conversation and has no seat in it. So it produces the one genre that requires no position — the survey — and produces it fluently: *Smith argues X. Jones argues Y. Patel notes the complexity of the issue.* Each source treated with equal, neutral, appreciative attention, because there is no ground from which to prefer one.

That is why the fix is never "add more analysis." The paragraph has to be built from a position it does not currently have. The observable symptom is the sequence; the disease is the missing seat at the table.

The second structural failure is the missing warrant. Toulmin's terms are useful precisely here: a claim and its grounds are both easy to generate, and the warrant — the principle that licenses moving from this evidence to this claim — is the part that requires knowing the field. Student writing and machine writing fail in the same place for opposite reasons. The student has the warrant in mind and does not know it needs stating; the model does not have one.

## Detect

### Argument

- A thesis that announces instead of claims: *This paper will explore the various factors...* If nobody could disagree with it, it is a topic.
- The so-what test unanswered. Nothing in the piece says what changes if the argument is right.
- Sources queued rather than synthesized — *Smith argues, then Jones argues* — with no argument running between them.
- Paragraphs that open with a topic instead of a claim.
- Evidence and claim adjacent with no warrant connecting them. The quotation is there, the assertion is there, and the sentence explaining why the first licenses the second is missing.
- Counterargument absent, or built weak enough to knock over.
- A conclusion that restates the introduction rather than saying what follows.

### Sources

- Contestable assertions left uncited; settled ones cited to demonstrate reading.
- A quotation dropped in unframed, or a block quotation carrying the point the prose should have made.
- Quotation used where paraphrase would be more precise. Quote for the wording; paraphrase for the content.
- Sources characterized in ways their authors would not recognize.
- Citation of a source through another source's account of it, unmarked.
- Volume of citation standing in for engagement.

### Register

- Signposting inflation: *In this section I will now turn to a discussion of...*
- Nominalization chains where verbs would carry the sentence.
- Hedges stacked without a resolution condition: *it could perhaps be suggested that there may be some indication.*
- Boosters compensating for thin evidence: *clearly, obviously, it is undeniable that.*
- First person avoided by reflex, leaving agentless passives that hide who chose the method.
- Terms of art the writer could not define if stopped and asked.

## Write

Open with a claim someone in the field could argue with, in one sentence, placed where a reader can find it. In an essay that is the first page; in an article it is the end of the introduction.

Answer the so-what. Say what is different if you are right, and say it in the introduction rather than saving it for a conclusion nobody reaches.

Put sources in conversation with each other. Organize by question rather than by author, and say where they part and why. Two scholars who disagree because they measure different things is a paragraph; two scholars summarized in sequence is a list.

State the warrant. When you move from evidence to claim, name the principle that licenses the move — the disciplinary assumption, the methodological commitment, the definition being relied on. This is the single highest-value sentence in most academic paragraphs and the one most often absent.

Frame every quotation and then interpret it. A quotation is evidence for your sentence, never a substitute for it. If the interpretation only restates the quotation in other words, cut one of them.

Hedge where the evidence is genuinely uncertain, and name what would settle it. Hedging is a disciplinary requirement, not a weakness — it reports the state of knowledge. An unresolvable hedge is the failure, not a hedge.

Write *I* when the sentence is about a choice you made. *I coded the transcripts* is more accurate and more accountable than *the transcripts were coded*. Reflexive avoidance of the first person is a school rule that most fields abandoned; check the field, not the folk memory.

Steelman the objection. Give the strongest version, in the terms its holders use, and then answer it. An argument that only defeats a weak opponent has demonstrated nothing.

## Mechanics

Chicago, restated, with the field's own conventions taking precedence where they conflict.

- Serial comma. Title case for titles of works. Whole numbers spelled out through one hundred, and round multiples above it.
- One citation system throughout — notes-bibliography or author-date, never both in a piece. See `citation/chicago18.md`, which carries the divergences.
- Block quotations at roughly five lines or a hundred words, set off without quotation marks, with the citation after the closing punctuation.
- Ellipses for omission inside a quotation; square brackets for anything you added, including a changed capital.
- *Sic* only where the reader would otherwise suspect your transcription.
- Terms defined at first use, then used consistently. One concept, one name.
- Epigraphs, acknowledgments and funding statements carry disclosure obligations in most fields; treat them as part of the argument's honesty rather than as front matter.
- Generative-AI use is now a disclosable item at most venues, and the rule is converging: tools are not authors, because authorship requires accountability a tool cannot hold. Disclose use and location — drafting, code, analysis, language editing — in the method or acknowledgments as the venue directs, and check the venue's policy rather than assuming it. The author remains answerable for every claim and every citation in the submitted text, including any the tool supplied.

## Formats

### Essay

Thesis on the first page, one claim per paragraph, no literature review. The argument is yours and the sources work for it. The essay is the genre where the writer's position is most exposed, because there is no apparatus to hide behind: no methods section, no data, no gap in the literature to justify the exercise. Its characteristic failure is the five-paragraph reflex, which produces three parallel examples of the same point where an argument needed three consecutive steps.

Argument by accumulation is not argument. If the paragraphs could be reordered without loss, they are a list.

### Research paper

Swales's three moves, in order. Establish the territory — what is known, in one or two paragraphs, not a survey. Establish the niche — the gap, the tension, or the counter-claim, stated as a problem rather than as an absence. *Nobody has studied X* is only interesting if somebody should have. Occupy the niche — say what you did and what you found.

Methods in enough detail to repeat, in the past tense, with the choices attributed to the people who made them. Report results before interpreting them, and keep the two separable so a reader can disagree with your reading while accepting your data.

Limitations stated as a reviewer would raise them, not in a register that makes them sound harmless. Scale the same structure for a thesis or dissertation: the moves do not change, the territory section gets longer, and the gap has to carry more weight.

## Evidence

Rung 4 as a floor: sources are interrogated rather than merely named. Who produced this, under what method, with what interest, and what would have to be true for it to be reliable.

Rung 5 for research writing, where uncertainty is quantified, design is weighted for how much the conclusion can bear, and the reader is given what they need to disagree productively.

The floor holds regardless of audience tier. A `high-school` audience shifts what can be assumed and how the prose reads; it does not license citing a source you have not examined. Where `audience/` and `domain/` appear to conflict, audience governs register and domain governs the obligation.

This is a floor, not a setting. Where the audience tier sits at a higher rung, the tier governs; where this floor is higher, it governs. Plain writing for a non-specialist is a change of register and never a lower standard of proof.

## Boundaries

Against `technical`. Both are precise and both are checked, but technical prose is task-ordered and non-narrative, and its reader is mid-task rather than mid-argument. Documentation that argues is documentation that has lost its way; an essay that only instructs is a manual.

Against `press`. Both are evidence-bound. Press carries its sourcing inside the sentence for a reader with one pass and no bibliography; academic prose can defer to an apparatus because its reader will chase a reference.

Against `legal`. Both cite heavily, and the citation does different work. A legal citation encodes the authority's weight and the writer's confidence in it; an academic citation locates a claim in a conversation.

Against `medical`. Clinical and health research carries obligations this module does not state: the reporting guidelines, trial registration, and the risk-communication rules governing how a result may be phrased. `medical` governs the numbers and the claims made from them; this module governs the argument built around them and the conversation it enters.

With `audience/`. Pairs with `undergraduate`, `graduate` or `professional` most often. The common miscalibration is an undergraduate reaching for peer register — compressed territory moves and unglossed terms of art — which reads as evasion rather than authority, because the demonstration of competence is exactly what an undergraduate is being asked for.

## Examples

**Thesis that announces**

> This paper will explore the various factors that contributed to the decline of the guild system in early modern Europe.

> Guilds in the Low Countries collapsed a generation before those in the German states, and the difference was access to credit rather than competition from rural production.

**Sources queued instead of synthesized**

> Smith (2019) argues that wages stagnated in this period. Jones (2021) argues that wages rose. Patel (2022) argues that measurement is difficult.

> Smith and Jones disagree because they are measuring different things: Smith tracks hourly pay, Jones total compensation including employer-paid insurance. Patel shows that the gap between those two series accounts for most of the apparent dispute, which means the substantive question is not whether wages rose but whether insurance counts as wages.

**Missing warrant**

> Forty percent of the parish's households were headed by day laborers by 1780. The enclosure acts therefore remade rural labor.

> Forty percent of the parish's households were headed by day laborers by 1780, against twelve percent in 1750. Day labor is the category historians use to mark households with no claim on common land, so a shift of that size in thirty years is a change in tenure rather than in occupation — which is what the enclosure acts would predict, and what customary tenure would not.

**Quotation left to argue on its own**

> Ruiz notes that "the enclosure acts remade rural labor." This shows the importance of the enclosure acts.

> Ruiz calls the enclosure acts a remaking of rural labor. The parish records support the claim narrowly: day laborers rose from 12% of households to 41% in thirty years, but only in parishes that actually enclosed. In the unenclosed parishes of the same county the figure barely moved, which makes the mechanism tenure rather than the general agricultural depression Ruiz also invokes.

**Hedge with no resolution condition**

> It could perhaps be suggested that the policy may have had some effect on enrollment, although further research would be needed.

> The policy probably raised enrollment, but the two districts that kept usable records are also the two that advertised the change, so the estimate confounds policy with publicity. District-level data for 1994, which the state archive holds, would separate them.

**Register borrowed rather than earned**

> This analysis interrogates the multivalent discursive formations through which institutional legitimacy is constituted and contested.

> Accreditation reports are written to be approved, not to be read, and that shapes what they can say. I compare what three colleges told their accreditor with what they told their trustees in the same year.
