# Domain: Fiction

Invented narrative within the creative brief. Preserve the intended voice, viewpoint, plot and degree of explicitness. Fiction can use summary, a stated theme, direct dialogue or an open ending deliberately; no single device is automatically slop.

A scene can lose force when a redundant explanation repeats what its action already conveys. That is a craft diagnosis, not proof of authorship or an established universal account of model behavior.

So the characteristic machine failure in fiction is a scene that works, followed by a sentence explaining that it worked. Clumsiness would be easier to fix.

## On show and tell

The received advice is a bad binary and it produces bad correction. Fiction needs summary. A novel that dramatized every meal would be unreadable, and Burroway's actual distinction is between scene and summary as tools with different jobs, not between a virtue and a vice.

The variable that matters is Gardner's psychic distance: how far the narration stands from the character's consciousness. It runs from the long view — *In the spring of 1911 a woman in a gray coat crossed the yard* — through the named and characterized — *Ada had never trusted the river in March* — to the interior, where the character's idiom takes over the syntax entirely and the narrator disappears.

Any position on that scale is legitimate, including a consistent distance across an entire piece. Change it when the story benefits, not to satisfy a variation quota. Investigate unintended drift rather than automatically replacing distance with interiority.

Free indirect discourse can carry a character's diction and judgment in third-person narration. A filter such as *she noticed* can increase distance or make a change in awareness explicit; remove it only when that effect is unwanted. Do not add a memory or fact to replace the filter unless the brief permits it.

## Detect

### Explanation

- The emotion named directly after it is shown: *She slammed the door. She was furious.*
- A closing line that redundantly states a theme the brief intends to leave implicit.
- A paragraph glossing the beat above it — the narrative equivalent of a laugh track.
- Dialogue followed by an account of what the speaker meant.
- Symbolic objects identified as symbolic.
- A character reaching a realization the reader reached two pages earlier, and articulating it.

### Distance and focalization

- Filter verbs creating unwanted distance, not their mere presence: *she saw, he felt, she noticed, he realized, she watched as*.
- Unintended shifts in psychic distance inside a paragraph.
- Head-hopping mid-scene. Note that omniscience is not head-hopping — omniscience is a narrator with consistent access and a voice of its own, while head-hopping is uncontrolled drift with no governing intelligence.
- A viewpoint character reporting what she cannot perceive: her own expression, another character's motive, the color of her eyes in the absence of a mirror.
- The mirror.

### Dialogue

- Dialogue whose uniform turn-taking or exposition conflicts with the characters and situation. Clear, cooperative speech can be intentional.
- Said-bookisms and adverb tags: *he exclaimed excitedly, she opined.*
- Repetitive, imprecise choices such as every light *flickering* or every speaker *murmuring*. These words can be exactly right; historical corpus frequencies do not establish an error in this story.
- Names used in direct address more often than people use them.
- Exposition delivered between characters who both already know it.
- Every character in the same register — same vocabulary, same sentence length, same jokes.
- Evasion or directness imposed regardless of the character's actual intention.

### Scene and structure

- A scene that ends where it began: nothing decided, nothing learned, no relation altered.
- Backstory arriving before the reader has a reason to want it.
- Sensory inventory — all five, one room.
- Research showing as exposition rather than as texture.
- Stakes announced rather than dramatized.
- Weather opening. Waking up. Travel between scenes that contains no scene.

## Write

When the brief relies on subtext, let a successful beat carry it. Keep explicit interpretation, summary or a moral when it is an intentional part of the form.

Check whether an ending adds something or merely explains an effect already achieved. Do not delete a necessary ending or force resolution where openness is intended.

Choose or preserve a deliberate narrative distance. Transitions can widen or close it, but neither every scene nor every piece needs a shift.

Remove a filter when closer perception serves the intended voice. Keep it when awareness, uncertainty or deliberate distance matters. Free indirect discourse is an option, not a compulsory replacement.

Let dialogue reflect what the characters want and how they communicate. Evasion, interruption, candor and cooperation are all available. Do not rewrite sincere or professional speech into universal evasiveness.

*Said* is invisible; use it, or use an action beat and drop the tag. A beat also does blocking, which a tag cannot.

Choose details for what they contribute, not to cover every sense or meet a three-detail quota. In an edit, preserve plot and setting facts unless invention or substantive rewriting is authorized.

Give characters appropriate diction and rhythm without forcing every voice to be conspicuously different. Keep attribution when it aids clarity.

Make something change. A decision, a relationship, the balance of information between two people, or what the reader knows that a character does not. A scene in which nothing changes is an establishing shot.

Enter late, leave early. Most scenes begin one beat after the writer thinks and end one beat before.

## Mechanics

Chicago, restated, for the conventions fiction actually exercises.

- New paragraph for each speaker, including for an action beat belonging to that speaker.
- A dialogue tag takes a comma inside the closing quotation mark: `"I know," she said.` An action beat is a separate sentence and takes a period: `"I know." She set down the cup.` The difference is not decorative — a beat attributes and blocks, a tag only attributes.
- Em dash inside the quotation for an interruption; ellipsis for trailing off. They are not interchangeable, and the choice tells the reader whether the speaker was stopped or stopped.
- Quotation inside quotation takes single marks.
- A speech continuing across paragraphs takes an opening quotation mark on each paragraph and a closing mark only at the end.
- Interior thought is normally set in roman with no quotation marks; italics are available for direct thought if used consistently and sparingly. Pick one and hold it for the whole work.
- Serial comma. Numbers spelled out more liberally than in nonfiction, since numerals interrupt a voice.
- Silence and white space are punctuation. A section break can carry a transition that a sentence would have to explain.

## Formats

### Short story

One arc, one central change, and an ending that is inevitable in hindsight and not in prospect. The form's economy is its constraint: there is no room for a subplot that does not also advance the main one, and no room to establish a character who does not act.

Begin as close to the change as the story allows. The reader will accept considerable confusion in the first paragraph and very little in the fourth.

An ending should fulfill the story's chosen form. Redundant explanation and an unintentionally missing resolution can both disappoint; a deliberately explicit or unresolved ending can work.

### Scene

A place, a want, an obstacle. Blocking clear enough that a reader never rereads to work out who is where and what they are holding.

The unit test: name what each character wants in the scene, and name what changes by the end. If either answer is missing, the scene is texture.

Historical and real-world material is the one place fiction takes on an obligation resembling evidence. The research should reach the page as texture — what things cost, how work was done, what people were afraid of — rather than as exposition. And writing about real people and real events carries a duty of care that no craft rule will discharge for you.

## Evidence

The legacy `evidence_floor: none` allows invention within a fictional brief; it is not permission to misrepresent research, sources or real people. Do not fabricate reporting, tests or personal experience outside that fictional frame.

Respect the reader's inference and the work's intended explicitness. Redundant explanation is a craft problem, not equivalent to a fabricated factual source.

Where fiction draws on real people, events or cultures, distinguish invented elements from factual claims and handle the research with accuracy and care.

A classroom preset may support an assignment, but does not require research apparatus for every invented scene or make the reader's credentials a research protocol.

## Boundaries

Against `non-fiction`. Craft techniques overlap; the declared factual contract differs. Do not silently apply fiction's permission to invent to a memoir or reported account. Neither genre has one required kind of ending.

Against functional prose. A summary useful in a technical explanation may be redundant in a scene. Judge the device by this work's purpose, not a rule that fiction must always conceal its point.

Against `core/formatting.md`. That module's defaults assume expository prose. Fiction legitimately uses the one-line paragraph, the fragment, the section break as transition, and white space as timing. `core/restraint.md` governs here: the fragment that is doing timing work stays.

Against `core/rhythm.md`. Compatible, and fiction pushes further. Sentence length is characterization and pacing, not only variety.

With `domain/education-level/`. Middle-grade fiction does not automatically need a school-assignment preset. Describe the audience's actual familiarity, language support and genre expectations. A classroom preset can help an explicit teaching task without imposing source analysis or syntax ceilings on the story.

## Examples

These independent fictional briefs specify the creative task and protected details. Invention is permitted only where the brief says so.

**The beat, then its explanation**

**Supplied facts:** The scene's action is that she puts the ring on the counter and leaves. The author wants the relationship's ending conveyed by that action, without an explanatory moral.

> She set the ring on the counter and walked out. It was a moment of quiet devastation, the end of everything they had built together, and she knew there was no going back.

> She set the ring on the counter and walked out.

**Filter verbs and psychic distance**

**Supplied facts:** The author requests close third-person narration. The character sees the open door, remembers standing on the step and locking it, and knows the key remains in his left pocket. Those details are supplied, not inferred from the filter verb.

> He noticed that the door was open and felt a wave of unease wash over him as he realized someone must have been inside.

> The door stood open. He had locked it. He had stood on this step and locked it and put the key in his left pocket, where it was.

**Dialogue where everyone cooperates**

**Supplied facts:** The author requests an indirect version of this fictional exchange. John forgot Tuesday's birthday; his partner bought a cake, put it in the fridge and is concealing anger. Running the tap until it goes cold is a supplied action. Rewriting the fictional dialogue is authorized.

> "I am angry that you forgot my birthday," she said. "I understand why you feel that way," he replied. "I apologize sincerely."

> "There's cake in the fridge." "I know." "Bought it myself. Tuesday." She ran the tap until it went cold. "It's fine, John. It's a cake."

**Sensory inventory**

**Supplied facts:** The kitchen has the sensory details in the first passage. The edit should focus on the ticking radiator and old-coffee smell. No burnt pot, elapsed time or person who left it on is established.

> The kitchen smelled of bread and old coffee, the light was golden and warm, the radiator ticked softly, and the linoleum felt cool underfoot.

> The radiator ticked. The kitchen smelled of old coffee.

**Theme stated at the door**

**Supplied facts:** Maria returns in the cold after a week away. The porch light is on; she guesses it has been on all week, and nobody mentioned it to her. The author wants the implication left unstated.

> Standing there in the cold, Maria understood at last that home is not a place but the people who wait for you in it.

> The porch light was on. All week, she guessed. Nobody had mentioned it.

**Unintended shift in distance**

**Supplied facts:** The scene is set in 1900 in a village founded during the twelfth century, with a recorded population peak in 1841. The character dislikes it. The author requests consistent distant narration; an exact count of winters is not supplied.

> The village had stood since the twelfth century, its stone houses weathered by six hundred winters. God, she hated this place. Local records indicate the population peaked in 1841.

> The village had stood since the twelfth century. Its population had peaked in 1841. She disliked the place.

**Research as exposition**

**Supplied facts:** In this fictional scene, the family's former common land is now called Harrow's field. The father has adopted that name. No specific act or legislative year is established, and the author wants the loss conveyed through the naming.

> "As you know, Father, the enclosure acts of 1773 permitted landowners to consolidate strips of common land, which has had a significant effect on our family's tenure."

> Her father had stopped calling it the common. "Harrow's field," he said now.

**Deliberate distance**

**Supplied facts:** The author wants this distant, formal vignette. The ferry departs at dawn and Nora remains on the quay. No interior response or closer viewpoint is requested.

> At dawn, the ferry departed. Nora remained on the quay.

*No change. A fixed narrative distance is doing the requested work.*
