# Domain: Fiction

Narrative. No thesis, no takeaway, no summary. The only module in this directory with no evidence rung, because fiction makes no truth claims — and the only one whose central discipline is refusing to state what the writing has already accomplished.

This is the hardest anti-slop test in the repository, and it is worth being precise about why. Slop cannot resist explaining its own subtext. That is not a quirk of phrasing; it follows from what the system is doing. A model producing narrative is producing a legible signal that meaning is present. Subtext is by definition the part of the meaning that was not stated. A process optimized to demonstrate that it has meant something will state the thing whose whole value lay in going unstated — and it will do so in the last line, where the damage is greatest, because that is where a summary belongs in every other genre it has learned.

Everything below follows from that. The characteristic machine failure in fiction is not clumsiness. It is a scene that works, followed by a sentence explaining that it worked.

## On show and tell

The received advice is a bad binary and it produces bad correction. Fiction needs summary. A novel that dramatized every meal would be unreadable, and Burroway's actual distinction is between scene and summary as tools with different jobs, not between a virtue and a vice.

The variable that matters is Gardner's psychic distance: how far the narration stands from the character's consciousness. It runs from the long view — *It was the winter of 1853, and a large man stepped out of a doorway* — through the named and characterized — *Henry had never much cared for snowstorms* — to the interior, where the character's idiom takes over the syntax entirely and the narrator disappears.

Any position on that scale is legitimate. Two things are faults. Moving without intending to, so a paragraph drifts from the far view to the interior and back inside four sentences, which reads as unsteadiness rather than range. And sitting at one distance for a whole piece, which is why uniformly "vivid" writing exhausts a reader as quickly as uniformly distant writing bores one.

Free indirect discourse is the interior end handled well: the character's diction and judgment carried inside the narrator's third person, with no tag and no quotation marks. It is also precisely what a filter verb destroys. *She noticed that the door was open* puts a pane of glass between the reader and the perception. *The door was open. She had locked it.* does not.

## Detect

### Explanation

- The emotion named directly after it is shown: *She slammed the door. She was furious.*
- A closing line that states the theme.
- A paragraph glossing the beat above it — the narrative equivalent of a laugh track.
- Dialogue followed by an account of what the speaker meant.
- Symbolic objects identified as symbolic.
- A character reaching a realization the reader reached two pages earlier, and articulating it.

### Distance and focalization

- Filter verbs holding the reader at arm's length: *she saw, he felt, she noticed, he realized, she watched as.*
- Unintended shifts in psychic distance inside a paragraph.
- Head-hopping mid-scene. Note that omniscience is not head-hopping — omniscience is a narrator with consistent access and a voice of its own, while head-hopping is uncontrolled drift with no governing intelligence.
- A viewpoint character reporting what she cannot perceive: her own expression, another character's motive, the color of her eyes in the absence of a mirror.
- The mirror.

### Dialogue

- Everyone speaking in full sentences, strictly in turn, meaning exactly what they say.
- Said-bookisms and adverb tags: *he exclaimed excitedly, she opined.*
- The measured machine-fiction vocabulary, which is the same failure arriving from the other direction. Across 67 models, `flickered` sits in the over-represented list of 98.5%, `muttered` and `murmured` of 82.1% and 73.1%, `whispered` of 68.7%. Light that flickers and voices that drop are the two families a model reaches for hardest. None of these words is bannable — a candle does flicker — so read the density, not the instance.
- Names used in direct address more often than people use them.
- Exposition delivered between characters who both already know it.
- Every character in the same register — same vocabulary, same sentence length, same jokes.
- Dialogue that answers the question asked. Most real answers arrive at an angle.

### Scene and structure

- A scene that ends where it began: nothing decided, nothing learned, no relation altered.
- Backstory arriving before the reader has a reason to want it.
- Sensory inventory — all five, one room.
- Research showing as exposition rather than as texture.
- Stakes announced rather than dramatized.
- Weather opening. Waking up. Travel between scenes that contains no scene.

## Write

Show the beat and stop. The meaning belongs to the reader; hand it over and leave the room.

Suspect the last line of every scene and every chapter. It is where the explanation goes when it is going anywhere. Cut it, read the scene again, and restore it only if something genuinely fell out.

Choose a psychic distance for each stretch and move deliberately. Open wide and close in, or the reverse, but know which you are doing and let the paragraph break carry the shift.

Cut filter verbs and put the reader inside the perception. Where the sentence needs the character's judgment, use free indirect discourse rather than a reporting verb.

Dialogue is people failing to say things. Let them evade, interrupt, answer a question nobody asked, and pursue two conversations at once. What a character wants and what a character says are related by pressure, not by equality.

*Said* is invisible; use it, or use an action beat and drop the tag. A beat also does blocking, which a tag cannot.

Three right details beat an inventory. Choose them for what they reveal, not for coverage — Burroway's significant detail is significant because it is doing two jobs at once.

Give each character their own diction, rhythm, and characteristic evasion. If you can delete the attributions and still tell who is speaking, the dialogue is working.

Make something change. A decision, a relationship, the balance of information between two people, or what the reader knows that a character does not. A scene in which nothing changes is an establishing shot.

Enter late, leave early. Most scenes begin one beat after the writer thinks and end one beat before.

## Mechanics

Chicago, restated, for the conventions fiction actually exercises.

- New paragraph for each speaker, including for an action beat belonging to that speaker.
- A dialogue tag takes a comma inside the closing quotation mark: `"I know," she said.` An action beat is a separate sentence and takes a period: `"I know." She set down the cup.` The difference is not decorative — a beat attributes and blocks, a tag only attributes.
- Em dash inside the quotation for an interruption; ellipsis for trailing off. They are not interchangeable, and the choice tells the reader whether the speaker was stopped or stopped.
- Quotation inside quotation takes single marks.
- A speech continuing across paragraphs takes an opening quotation mark on each paragraph and a closing mark only at the end.
- Interior thought is normally set in roman with no quotation marks; italics are available for direct thought if used consistently and sparingly. Pick one and hold it for the whole work.
- Serial comma. Numbers spelled out more liberally than in nonfiction, since numerals interrupt a voice.
- Silence and white space are punctuation. A section break can carry a transition that a sentence would have to explain.

## Formats

### Short story

One arc, one central change, and an ending that is inevitable in hindsight and not in prospect. The form's economy is its constraint: there is no room for a subplot that does not also advance the main one, and no room to establish a character who does not act.

Begin as close to the change as the story allows. The reader will accept considerable confusion in the first paragraph and very little in the fourth.

The ending is the hardest technical problem in the form, and the most common failure is the explained ending — a final paragraph that tells the reader what the story was about. The second most common is the withheld ending, which mistakes refusing to resolve for declining to explain. Resolve the situation; decline to interpret it.

### Scene

A place, a want, an obstacle. Blocking clear enough that a reader never rereads to work out who is where and what they are holding.

The unit test: name what each character wants in the scene, and name what changes by the end. If either answer is missing, the scene is texture.

Historical and real-world material is the one place fiction takes on an obligation resembling evidence. The research should reach the page as texture — what things cost, how work was done, what people were afraid of — rather than as exposition. And writing about real people and real events carries a duty of care that no craft rule will discharge for you.

## Evidence

None. This is the only module in `domain/` with no rung, and the exception is the point rather than an oversight. Fiction makes no truth claims, so the sourcing ladder does not apply to it.

The obligation that replaces it is to the reader's inference. Fiction asks a reader to construct meaning from what is shown, and the writer's side of that bargain is to supply enough and then stop. Explaining the subtext is the narrative equivalent of an unsourced claim: it takes back the work the reader came to do.

Where fiction touches real people, real events, or a living culture, the obligation returns as accuracy and care, and `core/accuracy.md` applies to the research even though nothing on the page is a claim.

Fiction has no floor, so where a tier carries a rung it governs the research behind the page — never what appears on it. A change of register is never a lower standard of proof.

## Boundaries

Against everything else in this directory. Every other domain here rewards stating the point; this one punishes it. A writer moving from `technical` or `professional` prose into fiction carries the summary reflex across, and it lands as the explained ending.

Against `core/formatting.md`. That module's defaults assume expository prose. Fiction legitimately uses the one-line paragraph, the fragment, the section break as transition, and white space as timing. `core/restraint.md` governs here: a flagged construction that is doing work stays.

Against `core/rhythm.md`. Compatible, and fiction pushes further. Sentence length is characterization and pacing, not only variety.

With `audience/`. Fiction pairs with a tier for its *reader*, not its writer. Middle-grade fiction is written at `middle-school` and is not simpler to write. The tier governs vocabulary and syntactic load; it does not license explaining the subtext, and children's writers know this better than most adult ones.

## Examples

**The beat, then its explanation**

> She set the ring on the counter and walked out. It was a moment of quiet devastation, the end of everything they had built together, and she knew there was no going back.

> She set the ring on the counter and walked out.

**Filter verbs and psychic distance**

> He noticed that the door was open and felt a wave of unease wash over him as he realized someone must have been inside.

> The door stood open. He had locked it. He had stood on this step and locked it and put the key in his left pocket, where it was.

**Dialogue where everyone cooperates**

> "I am angry that you forgot my birthday," she said. "I understand why you feel that way," he replied. "I apologize sincerely."

> "There's cake in the fridge." "I know." "Bought it myself. Tuesday." She ran the tap until it went cold. "It's fine, John. It's a cake."

**Sensory inventory**

> The kitchen smelled of bread and old coffee, the light was golden and warm, the radiator ticked softly, and the linoleum felt cool underfoot.

> The radiator ticked. Somebody had left the coffee on since morning and the pot had gone to tar.

**Theme stated at the door**

> Standing there in the cold, Maria understood at last that home is not a place but the people who wait for you in it.

> The porch light was on. It had been on all week, she guessed, and nobody had thought to mention it.

**Unintended shift in distance**

> The village had stood since the twelfth century, its stone houses weathered by six hundred winters. God, she hated this place. Local records indicate the population peaked in 1841.

> The village had stood since the twelfth century, and every one of its six hundred winters was in the stone. She hated it the way you hate a relative.

**Research as exposition**

> "As you know, Father, the enclosure acts of 1773 permitted landowners to consolidate strips of common land, which has had a significant effect on our family's tenure."

> Her father had stopped calling it the common. He called it Harrow's field now, the way everyone did, and he said it the way you say a word in a language you are not going to learn.
